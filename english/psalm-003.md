BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM III.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*The psalm of David when he fled from the face of his son Absalom.*[^1]

This title is found in all the Hebrew, Greek and Latin copies, whence it cannot be doubted that the Psalm ought to be understood to the letter of David himself, and is a prayer of David himself for his deliverance from the persecution of Absalom. But because David was a figure of Christ, insomuch that in the Prophets Christ himself is named David, as is plain from Jeremias chap. xxx, Ezechiel chap. xxxiv, and Osee chap. iii; therefore the Psalm can and ought to be understood of Christ also. Nay, the sense principally intended by the Holy Ghost seemeth to be of Christ. Now it is to be observed that the name David set in the title is not of the genitive case, but of the dative: for in the Hebrew there is the article ל *le*, which can denote the genitive and the dative case; but the Seventy always translated it τῷ, which is the article of the dative alone, and most prudently did the Seventy do this, to signify that the author of the Psalms is chiefly not David, but God. And so the sense is: *Psalmus David* (A Psalm to David), that is, a Psalm inspired by God to David himself.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. WHY, O LORD, ARE THEY MULTIPLIED THAT AFFLICT ME? MANY ARE THEY WHO RISE UP AGAINST ME.**

David complaineth, and wondereth, speaking with God in prayer, at the multitude of his persecutors; for, as we have it in II *Kings* xv, all Israel with their whole heart followed Absalom. That the same befell Christ is known from the Gospel, chiefly at the time of the passion: for then his son, that is, his people, rebelled against him, saying:

<!-- p. 20 (PDF 31) -->

"We have no king but Cæsar," *John* xix. And he withdrew from them as one weak, and fleeing through death; but a little after he returned through the resurrection. Absalom signifieth *the father's peace*, because in truth the war was the son's, but in the father there was always peace, as is plain both of David, who wept for the death of his son, and of Christ, who prayed for his persecutors; and as in the persecution of Absalom, Achitophel, an inward counsellor and familiar friend of David, betrayed him, and afterwards killed himself by hanging, II *Kings* xvii: so in the passion of Christ, Judas, one of his inward familiar friends, betrayed him, and in the end hanged himself with an halter.

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. MANY SAY TO MY SOUL: THERE IS NO SALVATION FOR HIM IN HIS GOD.**

This seemeth to be referred to inward temptations, in that the devils suggested to him within, in his mind, thoughts of distrust, as though he had trusted in the Lord in vain. It can also be referred to the speeches of men, who from this misfortune judged David to be forsaken by God, in whom nevertheless he had been wont to trust: for this is almost ordinary, that the imperfect think good men who are set in grievous distress to have hoped in God in vain. So his wife said to Job: "Dost thou still continue in thy simplicity?" *Job* ii. So the wife of Tobias also said: "It is evident thy hope is come to nothing, and thy alms now appear," *Tob.* ii. So lastly they said of Christ: "He trusted in God; let him deliver him, if he will," *Matt.* xxvii.

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. BUT THOU, O LORD, ART MY PROTECTOR, MY GLORY, AND THE LIFTER UP OF MY HEAD.**

This is the voice of a just man set in tribulation, such as David was, but above all Christ, who is the head of all. The sense is: many say to me that I have trusted in God in vain, but they are all deceived; for thou, O Lord, forsakest not them that hope in thee, therefore "thou art my protector," that is, thou hast taken me up to be protected against all the darts of the enemy: for in the Hebrew it is *clypeus meus* (my shield); nor dost thou only defend me, but thou dost also glorify me: therefore thou art also my glory, that is, the cause of my glory. And from these things it cometh that thou art the lifter up of my head, that is, makest me glad, so that I, who for sorrow went with my head hanging down, now rejoicing lift up my head. And this phrase is familiar to the Hebrews, whence also in the last chap. of *Jer.* it is said: "Evilmerodach the king lifted up the head of Joachin, and brought him forth out of prison"; and the Lord in the Gospel: "Lift up your heads, because your redemption is at hand," *Luke* xxi. That these things are true the examples teach, not only of Christ and of David, but also of Joseph, Job, Tobias, Daniel, etc.

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. I HAVE CRIED TO THE LORD WITH MY VOICE, AND HE HATH HEARD ME FROM HIS HOLY HILL.**

In the Hebrew copy we read *clamabo, et exaudiet* (I will cry, and he will hear); in the Greek and the Latin, *clamavi, et exaudivit* (I have cried, and he hath heard). According to the former reading David signifieth that, out of the trust which he expressed in the verse above, he will cry to the Lord in the present tribulation, and will without doubt be heard after the wonted manner. According to the latter reading he proveth by his own example what he had said above, namely, that God is his shield and his glory. For often, saith he, at other times, being afflicted, I have cried to the Lord, and he hath always heard me. As to the words, note that this, *I have cried with my voice*, hath an emphasis to express affection; he prayed not with a low voice, but cried, and cried with his own voice, that is, not by another, but by himself; not in spirit only, but also with the bodily voice. And that, *from his holy hill*, can signify mount Sion, where the ark of the Lord was, in which the presence of the Lord abode after a peculiar manner, and whence he gave answers; or also heaven, which is called by Solomon the firm dwelling place of God, III *Kings* viii, and by the Apostle, *Heb.* xii, is called "mount Sion," and the heavenly Jerusalem.

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. I HAVE SLEPT AND TAKEN MY REST, AND I HAVE RISEN UP, BECAUSE THE LORD HATH PROTECTED ME.**

Some things are to be observed as to the words, before we expound the meaning. The verb *dormivi* (I have slept), both in Hebrew שכבתי *schacabthi* and in Greek ἐκοιμήθην, signifieth properly *to lie down* and *to lie*; and because all who sleep lie down and lie, it signifieth also *to sleep*: but the verb *soporatus sum* (I have taken my rest) in the Hebrew and the Greek signifieth nothing but *to sleep*. And so the sense of those words is: I have slept, that is, I lay down, or lay, that I might sleep, and being in very deed overcome with sleep, I fell asleep, and afterwards awaking I rose up. Observe moreover that this, *quia Dominus suscepit me* (because the Lord hath protected me), is in the Hebrew and the Greek of the future tense, *quia Dominus suscipiet me* (because the Lord will protect me); or, as the Hebrew word יסמכני *iismakni* soundeth, *sustentabit me* (he will uphold me). But it is to be known that it is the custom of the holy tongue to put the future for the past, when a certain continuance is signified, as *Job* i: "So did Job all days."

[Margin: The custom of the holy tongue.]

That word *faciebat* (did) is in the Hebrew of the future tense: rightly therefore Jerome and the Vulgate Interpreter translated *suscepit* (hath protected), or *sustentavit* (hath upheld); and they might also have rendered *suscipiebat* (was protecting), or *sustentabat* (was upholding). Let us come to the meaning. David in that persecution of his son saith: "I have slept," or lay down to sleep, that is, I gave way, I fled, I resisted not, and so I lay as one overwhelmed; "and taken my rest," that is, so forsaken as though I had been dead, whence Absalom got possession of the kingdom, as though the king had been dead; "and I have risen up," that is, a little after I took heart again, and began to resist, because the Lord upheld me. But far more significantly do all these things agree to Christ: for he could truly say: "I have slept," that is, of my own will I lay upon the cross, that I might die upon it, as they compose themselves upon a bed who wish to sleep. "No man," saith he, *John* x, "taketh my life away from me, but I lay it down."[^2] "And taken my rest," that is, I was truly laid asleep in the sleep of death, and this also of my own will, not unwillingly, whence "bowing

<!-- p. 21 (PDF 32) -->

his head, he gave up the ghost," *John* xix. Now he calleth his death a sleep, because it was as easy for him to rise again from the dead as for others to awake from sleep; "and I have risen up, because the Lord hath protected," or upheld, "me," that is, a little after I rose from the dead, not relying on any human help, but leaning upon the infinite power of the divinity. And in this manner he proved that to be true which he had said in the verse above: "I have cried to the Lord with my voice, and he hath heard me from his holy hill."

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. I WILL NOT FEAR THOUSANDS OF THE PEOPLE SURROUNDING ME: ARISE, O LORD; SAVE ME, O MY GOD.**

Of David these things are most clear; for he is said to have risen up when he took heart again, and made himself ready to resist his enemies: therefore he now saith that he feareth not that innumerable people, because he trusteth in God, not in his own strength, or in the arms of his companions; and for that cause he asketh God to arise, to save him out of the hands of his enemies. God is said by a metaphor to arise when he beginneth to help: even as he is said to sleep when he helpeth not. "Arise," saith he in another place, "why sleepest thou, O Lord?" And beautifully are *exsurrexi* (I have risen up) and *exsurge* (arise) joined together, as if he said: I, thou rousing me by an inspiration sent into me, have risen up; do thou also therefore, I rousing thee by prayer, arise, and fight for me. Now to Christ all these things are most rightly fitted, not by reason of the head, but by reason of the body; that is, Christ saith these things after the resurrection not for his own person, but for his body, which is the Church, which in those beginnings had innumerable enemies, but, relying on God, overcame them all.

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. FOR THOU HAST STRUCK ALL THEM WHO ARE MY ADVERSARIES WITHOUT CAUSE: THOU HAST BROKEN THE TEETH OF SINNERS.**

In the Hebrew it is read thus: *Quoniam tu percussisti omnes inimicos meos in maxilla, dentes impiorum contrivisti* (For thou hast struck all my enemies on the cheek, thou hast broken the teeth of the wicked); but it is credible that the Seventy Interpreters read, for לחי *lechi*, which signifieth *the cheek*, לחינם *lechinnam*, which signifieth *in vain*; and it is credible also that the copies which the Seventy had were better than those which we have. But yet each reading hath a good sense. God therefore is asked, whether by David for himself, or by Christ for his Church, to fight against the enemies, because he hath always done so at other times, to wit, hath struck all the enemies of the godly. And if indeed we read *sine causa* (without cause), the sense will be that God always punisheth those who without cause persecute the just, and breaketh their teeth, that is, bringeth it to pass that they cannot bite, that is, cannot hurt, but can only bark and affright. For although the just sometimes seem to be oppressed by the wicked, yet at length all things work together unto good for them, and the wicked pay the penalty of their persecution. But if we read *in maxilla* (on the cheek), the sense will be that God not only punisheth, but also confoundeth and putteth to shame the wicked. For the striking of the cheek is wont to be done not only for pain, but also for contempt: and that which followeth of the breaking of the teeth agreeth very well with the striking of the cheek: by which breaking is signified what we said above, namely, that it cometh to pass by the grace of God that the enemies of the just, whether they be devils or men, can bark after the manner of dogs, but cannot bite, that is, truly hurt.

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. SALVATION IS OF THE LORD, AND THY BLESSING IS UPON THY PEOPLE.**

[Margin: For God to bless is to do good.]

The Psalm is concluded by an invocation and praise of God, as if he said: From what hath been said we can conclude that salvation is of the Lord, and is to be acknowledged as received from him. For in the Hebrew it is *Domino salus* (Salvation to the Lord), that is, salvation is to be acknowledged as received from the Lord; which salvation is thy blessing, O Lord, upon thy people; for, for God to bless is to do good.

## OF THE WORD SELA.

In this Psalm there is found in three places, namely, after the second verse, after the fourth, and after the last, the Hebrew word סלה *sela*, which is found in some other Psalms, yet not in all; it is found also in certain places of the prophet Habacuc, and nowhere else. St. Jerome writeth of this word in his epistle *to Marcella*, in which he relateth three opinions: the first, of certain Rabbins, as I think, who will have it that by this word is signified a change or division of the rhythm, or a lifting up of the voice. The second, of the Seventy Interpreters, and also of Theodotion and Symmachus, who interpreted that Hebrew word *diapsalma*, a word equally obscure. The third, of Aquila and of the fifth and sixth editions, that *sela* is the same as *always*. This last opinion St. Jerome followed in his version from the Hebrew. And certainly the first opinion seemeth improbable; for if that word signified a change, or a division of the rhythm, or a lifting up of the voice, it would be set in all the Psalms, and chiefly in the longer ones. But we see that in this very short Psalm it is found thrice, as also twice in the fourth Psalm, and in the forty-sixth Psalm, which are very short; in certain very long ones, never. Next, it would not be set at the end of Psalms, but only in the midst: for what change, or division, or lifting up is to be signified when the Psalm is ended? But we see that it is found at the end of the third, ninth, twenty-fourth, and forty-sixth Psalms. The second opinion is probable on account of the authority of the Seventy Interpreters. Now diapsalma signifieth, as Augustine interpreteth it in his Commentary on the fourth Psalm, a pause in the singing of the psalm; but certainly a reason can scarcely be given why that pause should be marked in very few Psalms, and in the very short ones only. The third opinion is perhaps more probable; for, on account of the authority of St. Jerome, we see that this *always* is rightly

<!-- p. 22 (PDF 33) -->

set wherever *sela* is found. In this Psalm, in the second and fourth verses, *sela* can rightly be expounded *always*: "Many say to my soul: There is no salvation for him in his God *always*," that is, granted that God hath sometimes preserved David from dangers, yet he will not always preserve him; now assuredly he will not preserve him. But David answereth: "I have cried to the Lord with my voice, and he hath heard me from his holy hill *always*," that is, ye say that he will not always preserve me; I on the contrary say that I am always heard, and I hope that I shall always be preserved. Moreover, as at the end of Psalms there is wont to be set *amen*, or *for ever*, or *alleluia*, as is plain to those who read them, so *always* is rightly set there, which signifieth a desire that the things which have been said may be firm and ratified. So in this place, "thy blessing is upon thy people *always*," that is, may it always be, as if he said: *Amen*, or *continually*, as elsewhere.

[^1]: Translator's note: Bellarmine does not number the title, and so counts eight verses in this Psalm. The Douay-Rheims Bible counts the title as verse 1, so that its verses 2 to 9 are his 1 to 8.

[^2]: Translator's note: so Bellarmine quotes it (*animam meam*). The Vulgate at John x, 18 reads *Nemo tollit eam a me: sed ego pono eam a meipso*, and the Douay-Rheims "No man taketh it away from me: but I lay it down of myself".
