BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XVIII.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*Unto the end, a Psalm for David.*[^1]

The title hath nothing new. The argument of the Psalm is the praise of the divine law. The Prophet compareth the divine law with the heavenly body, than which nothing among bodily things seemeth to be more beautiful, more profitable, more powerful. The same Psalm can be expounded of Christ and the Apostles, as St. Augustine doth.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. THE HEAVENS SHEW FORTH THE GLORY OF GOD, AND THE FIRMAMENT DECLARETH THE WORK OF HIS HANDS.**

Being about to compare the law with the heaven, and thence to extol it wonderfully, he saith that the majesty of the heaven is so great, that from it most of all shineth forth the glory of God, who made the heaven. "The heavens shew forth the glory of God," that is, before the other works of God, which all praise their maker, it is the heavens which by their greatness and beauty make the glory of God manifest. "And the firmament declareth the work of his hands," he repeateth the same in other words; for in this place the heavens and the firmament signify the same thing, namely that whole heavenly body in which are the stars, and the sun itself also and the moon. For in chap. i of *Gen.*: "God called the firmament, Heaven," and in it he set the sun, the moon and the stars. Now it mattereth nothing whether we say heaven or heavens. For among the Hebrews the noun hath the form of the plural number, and is joined with verbs of the plural number. But the Interpreters render now *cœlum* (heaven), in the singular number, now *cœlos* (heavens), in the plural number. The firmament therefore, in which, according to the manner of speaking of holy Scripture, are all the heavenly lights, "declareth," and maketh known to men "the work of the hands of God," that is, the chief and most noble works of the hands of God, from which is gathered the exceeding great glory of the Creator.

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. DAY TO DAY UTTERETH SPEECH, AND NIGHT TO NIGHT SHEWETH KNOWLEDGE.**

Wonderful indeed is the preaching whereby the heavens shew forth the glory of God. And this is shown in three ways. *First*, because the heaven preacheth without ceasing; *secondly*, because it preacheth in the words of all tongues; *thirdly*, because it preacheth to the whole world. In this verse is declared the preaching without ceasing; and because the heaven preacheth by days and nights, since by day is beheld the beauty of the sun, and by night the beauty of the stars; and because days and nights abide not, but glide away, and succeed one another, therefore by a poetic device the Prophet feigneth that one day, having finished its course and its preaching, delivereth to the following day the word of preaching, and that the night also, having finished its course, and as it were sung its hymn, delivereth to the following night the office of singing. "Day," saith he, "to day uttereth speech," that is, the day when it endeth delivereth to another day the word of the divine praise. "And night to night sheweth knowledge," that is, and the night likewise when it endeth, "sheweth," that is, declareth to the night that succeedeth the knowledge of praising God, and so continually, and without any ceasing, days and nights as it were lead the dance, and celebrate God with praises.

[Margin: The preaching of the heavens wonderful for three reasons.]

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. THERE ARE NO SPEECHES NOR LANGUAGES, WHERE THEIR VOICES ARE NOT HEARD.**

Here now the Prophet showeth that the preaching of the heavens is made in all tongues, that is, is understood by all nations, as if the heavens spoke in the tongues of all nations, because, namely, all men see the beauty and excellence of the heaven, and thence can come to the knowledge of the beauty and excellence of the Creator. "There are no speeches nor languages," that is, there are no tongues, of which tongues "their voices" are not heard, that is, the voices of the heavens, that is, which tongues are not expressed by the voices of the heavens.

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. THEIR SOUND HATH GONE FORTH INTO ALL THE EARTH: AND THEIR WORDS UNTO THE ENDS OF THE WORLD.**

The third praise of the eloquence of the heavens is set forth, that not only without ceasing, and in all tongues, but also in the whole earth the heavens are heard shewing forth the glory of God. By *sound* is not understood any bodily harmony, such as some philosophers have placed in the heavens; but that preaching of glory which ariseth from the beauty of the heaven considered by men. *Into all the earth* also is the same as *the ends of the world*; for the Prophets very often repeat the same thing twice. In the Hebrew there is read, for *sonus eorum* (their sound), *kavam*, which signifieth *linea*, or *regula eorum* (their line, or their rule). But the Seventy seem to have read, not קום *kavam*, but קולם *kolam*, with a single letter added, which is *sonus eorum* (their sound). For if they had read *kavam*, they would never have rendered φθόγγος αὐτῶν, that

<!-- p. 105 (PDF 116) -->

is, *sonus eorum* (their sound), unless they had been most unskilful. Add that St. Jerome also rendered from the Hebrew *sonus eorum*. And so also the Apostle cited it, *Rom.* x; and *their sound* agreeth with that which followeth, *their words*, where the selfsame thing is repeated in other words. From which we understand that the Hebrew text hath been corrupted by the fault of the scribes, and that after the times of St. Jerome. Moreover St. Paul citeth this place, *Rom.* x, wishing to prove that the preaching of Christ hath reached to all nations. From which we understand that by the heavens the Apostles ought to be expounded, at least in the allegorical sense. And in truth the holy Apostles, as also other holy preachers, are most rightly compared to the heavens, because they are lifted up from the earth by contemplation, wide by charity, bright by wisdom, always serene by tranquillity of mind, most swiftly moved by an Intelligence through obedience, raining by instruction, thundering by rebukes, lightening by miracles, bestowing many good things on the earth, and seeking nothing from it, by true liberality, most pure from all filth by holiness of life, lastly the dwelling place of the most high King by perfect justice. For the soul of the just man is the seat of wisdom.

[Margin: The Hebrew text is corrupted.]

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. HE HATH SET HIS TABERNACLE IN THE SUN: AND HE, AS A BRIDEGROOM COMING OUT OF HIS BRIDE CHAMBER.**

Although the whole heaven sheweth forth the glory of God, yet chiefly doth that most noble part of the heaven, which is the sun. Therefore in the sun, as in the most excellent part of the whole world, "God hath set his tabernacle." And rightly he saith tabernacle, not house, since God sojourneth in the sun in the time of our pilgrimage, wherein we see him through the glass of creatures; and among bodily creatures the sun, as we have said, holdeth the first place. But when we shall have come to our country, we shall see God not in the tabernacle of the sun, but in his own house, which is, namely, his eternity. In the Hebrew there is: *Soli posuit tabernaculum in eis* (For the sun he hath set a tabernacle in them), that is, God hath set in the heavens a tabernacle for the sun, he hath placed the sun in the heaven as in a tabernacle. The Greek reading might indeed in some sort be brought back to the Hebrew, if, when it is said, ἐν τῷ ἡλίῳ ἔθετο τὴν σκηνὴν αὐτοῦ, that word αὐτοῦ be taken for αὐτόθι, that is, *there*, which is not unusual. For then the sense would be: *In the sun*, that is, for the sun, *he hath set a tabernacle there*, that is, in the heavens. But the interpretation is harsh, *in sole* (in the sun), that is, *soli* (for the sun). But because there appeareth in the Hebrew text no ambiguity or obscurity of words, and yet it is certain that the Seventy Interpreters, most learned men, could not have rendered that which they rendered, if they had read the words which are found in the Hebrew copies, we are compelled to say that they read otherwise. Either meaning is true, namely that God hath set his tabernacle in the sun, and that he hath set for the sun a tabernacle in the heavens. Now the Prophet proveth that God hath set his tabernacle in the sun, as in the most excellent creature, by three reasons, of which the first is drawn from beauty, the second from strength, the third from beneficence. "And he," saith he, "as a bridegroom coming out of his bride chamber." This is the reason drawn from beauty: He, saith he, riseth comely, shining, adorned, as if he were some bridegroom who came forth from the bridal chamber; and truly nothing more beautiful and more bright than the sun is found among all bodies.

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. HATH REJOICED AS A GIANT TO RUN THE WAY: HIS GOING OUT IS FROM THE END OF HEAVEN.**

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. AND HIS CIRCUIT EVEN TO THE END THEREOF: AND THERE IS NO ONE THAT CAN HIDE HIMSELF FROM HIS HEAT.**

The second reason is drawn from strength and power, in that without weariness, with the greatest swiftness, he runneth over a way well nigh immeasurable. "He hath rejoiced as a giant," or as one *strong* and *mighty* (as is read to the word in the Hebrew, כגבור), "to run the way." Rejoicing signifieth the cheerfulness which they are wont to have who do a thing without labour, nay rather with pleasure. And so it is signified in this place that the sun passeth through the immense space of the heaven, with a most swift course, without any weariness or labour. "His going out is from the end of heaven, and his circuit even to the end thereof." By the end of heaven (*summum cœlum*) the East is denoted. For in this place by *summum* is understood not what is high, but what is outermost, as the Hebrew word קץ *kets*, and the Greek ἄκρου signifieth. Now that is called the outermost part, where a thing beginneth, and where it endeth. And the heaven beginneth from the East, because thence its motion ariseth, and there also it endeth. The sense therefore is, that the sun riseth from the East, and, the whole compass of the heaven being run through by way of the West, returneth to the East. It mattereth little that in the Hebrew and the Greek there is in the future *exultabit* (he shall rejoice), where we have *exultavit* (he hath rejoiced). For the future tense is often taken for the past. And so St. Jerome rendereth from the Hebrew *exultavit*; and so also both he and St. Augustine read in the Commentary. "And there is no one that can hide himself from his heat." This is the last reason, drawn from the profit which all lower things receive from the sun, seeing that the sun so cherisheth all things with his life-giving heat, that he may be called a certain common parent of all things which are born in the earth and in the sea. And this is the cause why the sun so constantly and so carefully goeth round and visiteth the whole world, that nothing may hide itself, that is, may be shut out from the partaking of so signal a benefit.

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. THE LAW OF THE LORD IS UNSPOTTED, CONVERTING SOULS: THE TESTIMONY OF THE LORD IS FAITHFUL, GIVING WISDOM TO LITTLE ONES.**

The Prophet now applieth the comparison, as if he said: Beautiful is the heaven, most beautiful the sun, but the law of the Lord is much more beautiful; bright is the heaven, most bright the sun, but the law of the Lord is much brighter; profitable is the heaven to men, most profitable the sun, but the law of the Lord is much more profitable. Now he setteth down in order six praises of the divine law. The first is: "The law of the Lord is unspotted, converting souls," that is, the law of the Lord is most beautiful, as being without any blemish,[^2]

<!-- p. 106 (PDF 117) -->

or foulness, since it permitteth no sin, as human laws permit, and therefore when the law of the Lord hath been considered and thoroughly seen, "it converteth souls," that is, it converteth souls to the love of itself, and consequently draweth them to God, the author of so beautiful a law. With that phrase, *convertens animas* (converting souls), *est* (is) is understood, and therefore we have expounded it, *convertit* (it converteth). The second praise is in those words: "The testimony of the Lord is faithful, giving wisdom to little ones," where by the testimony is understood the same law. For in the Scriptures, and chiefly in the Psalms, the law of the Lord is not only called law, precept, commandment, statute, and the like, which signify law among other writers also; but it is also called testimony, justice, or justification, and judgment, as is plain chiefly from *Ps.* cxviii. The law of the Lord is called a testimony, because it testifieth to men what is the will of God, what the Lord requireth of us, what punishments he hath prepared for transgressors, and what rewards for those that keep it. He saith therefore: "The testimony of the Lord is faithful," that is, the law of the Lord, which will most certainly visit the good with rewards, and the wicked with punishments. "Giving wisdom to little ones," that is, it is giving, or it giveth, wisdom to little ones, that is, to men who are little ones in understanding it giveth the light of prudence, that they may know how to do good works, and to shun sins; for the Prophet in this place calleth little ones those men who are not strong in spiritual judgment, such as are all the friends of this world. And by *wisdom* he understandeth spiritual prudence, which correcteth manners, and formeth them to the rule of the divine law.

[Margin: Six praises of the divine law.]

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. THE JUSTICES OF THE LORD ARE RIGHT, REJOICING HEARTS: THE COMMANDMENT OF THE LORD IS LIGHTSOME, ENLIGHTENING THE EYES.**

The third praise of the divine law is, that after it hath begun to be loved, as the first praise hath it, and to be kept, as the second hath it, it filleth a man with wonderful gladness: for nothing is more pleasant than a good conscience. "The justices," saith he, "of the Lord,"[^3] that is, the law of the Lord, or the precepts of the Lord, which are called justices, because they are most just, and because they make just him that keepeth them, these precepts, I say, since they are right, are rejoicing, that is, "they rejoice hearts;" for right hearts agree exceedingly well with right precepts, and therefore they are glad and rejoice, when the occasion of keeping the precepts cometh. The fourth praise is: "The commandment of the Lord is lightsome, enlightening the eyes," that is, the law of the Lord, bright with the light of the divine Wisdom, enlighteneth the eyes of the mind, because it maketh us to understand the will of God, and what are true goods, and true evils. It teacheth also the ceremonies of God, full of divine mysteries. The law of the Lord enlighteneth also by way of disposition; for wisdom will not enter into a malicious soul, since nothing more hindereth the knowledge of God, wherein true wisdom consisteth, than uncleanness of heart. "For blessed are the clean of heart: for they shall see God," as Christ saith, *Matt.* v.

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. THE FEAR OF THE LORD IS HOLY, ENDURING FOR EVER AND EVER: THE JUDGMENTS OF THE LORD ARE TRUE, JUSTIFIED IN THEMSELVES.**

The fifth praise is, that the law of the Lord maketh the foregoing goods to be not temporal, but eternal. "For the fear of the Lord is holy," that is, the fear of the Lord, whereby a man trembleth to offend God, which by another name is called *piety*, "endureth for ever and ever," as to its reward; the goods which the keeping of the law bringeth forth, or the chaste fear that maketh a man keep the law, are not ended in death, but endure for ever. In which manner it is said elsewhere, *Ps.* ix: "The patience of the poor shall not perish for ever." In the Hebrew there is, *the fear of the Lord* טהורה *tehorah*, that is, *clean*; and in the Greek likewise there is ἁγνός, not ἅγιος. And so filial fear is signified, which hath no servility mingled with it, of which it is said in another Psalm, *Ps.* cxi: "Blessed is the man that feareth the Lord: he desireth exceedingly in his commandments."[^4] For he that feareth with servile fear keepeth the commandments not willingly, but against his will: but he that feareth with filial fear, "desireth exceedingly in his commandments," that is, he desireth greatly, and willeth altogether to keep them. The last praise is, that the law of the Lord is true, and just of itself, not needing to be justified from elsewhere. "The judgments of the Lord are true, justified in themselves," that is, the commandments of the Lord, which are called judgments, because by them God judgeth men, and which are the norm and rule of discerning virtues from vices, and good works from evil: these commandments, I say, most true, are justified in themselves, that is, they need not to be shown just by any one: for by this very thing, that they are the commandments of God, they are proved to be just. And besides, the ten commandments, of which David chiefly speaketh, since they are the first principles of the natural law, contain in themselves such justice, that in every place and time, and particular case, they are just, and admit no dispensation; whereas other laws often need circumstances of place, time and persons, that they may be just. And so the law of the Lord alone is justified in itself. In the Hebrew there is, *the judgments of the Lord* אמת, *truth*, that is, most true, are just, or are justified, יחדו, which signifieth *alike, together, into one*; but our Interpreter hath rightly rendered, *in semetipsa* (in themselves). For in this place *to be justified together*, or *alike*, is nothing else but not to need to be justified from elsewhere.

[Margin: Verse 11.]

**11. MORE TO BE DESIRED THAN GOLD AND MANY PRECIOUS STONES: AND SWEETER THAN HONEY AND THE HONEYCOMB.**

This is the conclusion from the foregoing, as if the Prophet said: Since the precepts of the Lord are so good, surely they are to be set before all the riches and delights of this world: for they are "more to be desired than gold and many precious stones," that is, had in the greatest plenty; and "sweeter than honey and the honeycomb," that is, not only than any honey whatever, but also than a honeycomb full, and

<!-- p. 107 (PDF 118) -->

overflowing with the purest honey. For that phrase, *and the honeycomb*, is put to signify plenty, that it may answer to that which had been said, many precious stones. In the Hebrew there is, *super aurum et topazion multum, et super mel et redundantiam favorum* (above gold and much topaz, and above honey and the overflowing of honeycombs); but the Interpreters have rendered the sense. Now the topaz is a kind of precious stone: but the Prophet hath put the species for the genus. How far removed is this truth from the mind of carnal men! how few of the carnal are there, who for a small gain, or a slight delight, would not despise all the divine precepts! And yet it is most true that the keeping of the law is more profitable and more pleasant than all treasures, and all pleasure.

[Margin: The judgments of the carnal, different from the judgments of the saints.]

[Margin: Verse 12.]

**12. FOR THY SERVANT KEEPETH THEM, AND IN KEEPING THEM THERE IS A GREAT REWARD.**

He proveth by his own example, or experience, that what he hath said is true. For, saith he, "thy servant keepeth them," the precepts, "and in keeping them there is a great reward" that befalleth him, that is, thy servant hath proved by experience that he receiveth many good things, while he keepeth the precepts. From the Hebrew St. Jerome rendereth, *servus tuus docebit ea* (thy servant shall teach them). But Pagninus rendereth, *servus tuus illustratur in eis* (thy servant is enlightened in them). But the Seventy have most rightly rendered, *custodit ea* (keepeth them). For the Hebrew word נזהר *nizhar*, although in other conjugations it signifieth *to teach*, or *to shine*, yet in the passive conjugation, such as it is in this place, signifieth *to keep oneself*, or *to beware*, or *to admonish oneself*. And so it could have been rendered to the word thus, *servus tuus custodit se in eis* (thy servant keepeth himself in them), that is, he abstaineth from sinning, keeping thy precepts, which is the same as, *thy servant keepeth them*, that is, the precepts.

[Margin: Verse 13.]

**13. WHO CAN UNDERSTAND SINS? FROM MY SECRET ONES CLEANSE ME: AND FROM THOSE OF OTHERS SPARE THY SERVANT.**[^5]

He had said that he keepeth the commandments of God; he correcteth himself, and excepteth the sins of ignorance, which can scarcely be avoided. As if he said: Thy servant bewareth of sins, by keeping thy law, not altogether, but as far as human frailty suffereth. Beautifully in the Hebrew it is read to the word, *ignorantias quis intelligit?* (who understandeth ignorances?) Where he setteth ignorance against understanding, wishing to show that it is most hard to avoid the sins which are committed through ignorance; and yet, because they are truly sins, and could have been avoided, he addeth a prayer: "From my secret ones cleanse me." That which followeth, "and from those of others spare thy servant" (*et ab alienis parce servo tuo*), doth not signify that God should forgive us the sins of others, as this place is commonly wont to be cited; but that God should keep us from the fellowship of perverse men. For men of good will, such as was holy David, ought before all things to beware of ignorance of their own sins, and of the seduction of wicked companions. For the word *alienis* (strangers), there is in the Hebrew זדים *zedim*, which signifieth *the proud*. St. Jerome rendereth, *a superbis libera servum tuum* (from the proud deliver thy servant). But the Seventy read *zarim*, not זדים *zedim*: now זרים signifieth *strangers*. The sense is almost the same, save that according to the Hebrew, as it now standeth, there is signified deliverance from one kind of evil men, that is, from the proud: but according to the Greek text, and the true Hebrew reading, there is signified a general deliverance from all perverse friends. Our reading therefore is thus to be expounded: From strangers, that is, from those who are of strange manners, spare thy servant, that is, by sparing remove them from the friendship of thy servant.

[Margin: Verse 14.]

**14. IF THEY SHALL HAVE NO DOMINION OVER ME, THEN SHALL I BE WITHOUT SPOT: AND I SHALL BE CLEANSED FROM THE GREATEST SIN.**

He giveth the reason why he so greatly feareth the familiarity of the wicked: For, saith he, if those men of strange manners "shall have no dominion over me," that is, if by too great familiarity they shall not have made me subject to themselves, and brought me to their own will; "then shall I be without spot, and I shall be cleansed," that is, I shall be clean, "from the greatest sin," that is, from grievous and mortal sin; for every mortal crime can be called the greatest, since it turneth away from God, the greatest good, and leadeth to the punishment of hell fire, plainly the greatest. St. Augustine readeth, *a delicto magno* (from a great sin), as it is in the Greek, but it mattereth little.

[Margin: Why mortal sin is the greatest?]

[Margin: Verse 15.]

**15. AND THE WORDS OF MY MOUTH SHALL BE SUCH AS MAY PLEASE: AND THE MEDITATION OF MY HEART ALWAYS IN THY SIGHT.**

Not only shall I then be without spot, but then also "they shall be such as may please," that is, it shall come to pass that the words of my mouth may please, "and the meditation of my heart always in thy sight," that is, the hymns which I sing to thy praise with heart and mouth shall please thee always, because they shall come forth from a clean heart and a simple mouth.

[Margin: Verse 16.]

**16. O LORD, MY HELPER, AND MY REDEEMER.**

My canticles, I say, shall please thee, by thy grace, not by my merits: because that I am without spot, clean from the greatest sin, and therefore that my words please thee, I have by thy gift, who art "my helper, and my redeemer," helper in good things, and redeemer from evil things
