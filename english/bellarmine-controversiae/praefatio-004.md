I undertake to dispute of the Controversies of the Faith against all the heretics of this time, and to gather very many, and most diverse, questions as it were into one place, and to embrace each several one, as members of one body, according to the strength which God shall vouchsafe to supply from heaven. A hard work truly, and certainly, in my judgment at least, of such a kind, that if it is to be handled not lightly, and perfunctorily, but accurately, and as the dignity, and difficulty of the matter demandeth: it requireth a knowledge well nigh infinite, not only of various disciplines, and tongues, but also of all antiquity, of all histories, and times: All which things since I myself perceive how small they are in me, and yet it is necessary to sustain, and bear through, the burden laid upon me: I will strive at least to make up for slowness of wit by labour, and diligence; and to bring it to pass, that since all the other helps, and aids of learning are wanting to us, zeal certainly, and industry may in no wise be found wanting.

But before I begin to dispute of the questions proposed, some few things seem to me needful to be said of the usefulness of disputations of this kind: then it must be briefly set forth, what, and how many are the controversies of this time.

And the usefulness indeed of the disputations set before us may easily[^1] be understood from this, that they comprehend the better, and more necessary part

<!-- p. 54 (PDF 77) -->

of all Theology. For we are to treat not of eaves-droppings and farms, not of light matters, of which it mattereth little, whether they be thus or otherwise: not of Metaphysical subtleties, which may be unknown without danger, and sometimes even be assailed with praise: but of God, of Christ, of the Church, of the Sacraments, of justification, of the help of grace, of free will, and of very many other most weighty, and most difficult[^2] questions, which belong to the very foundations of the faith; of which if any man would by disputing shake even one, as Saint Augustine wisely warned in the first book against Julian, the second chapter, he must needs take away the whole of what we believe in Christ. Next, to call heretical men back to the light of faith, or at least to break their assault, and fury, to keep those wild beasts from the Lord's fold, to protect the Church of Christ, and to pluck the little sheep already taken out of the jaws of the wolves, and restore them to the folds of the Lord: how great, I pray, is the usefulness, how true a cause of gladness, how plentiful a harvest of glory? But none of these things will he ever effect, who hath not first been long, and much conversant in these questions, and both diligently furnished himself with the arms of the Church, and learned to beat back the darts of the enemies, and to turn them again upon the enemies themselves.

And lest perchance our speech be less approved by some, because they judge the plague of heresy to be lighter than it truly is, since they measure it perhaps by the common vices, and sins, which they observe to be daily purged, and healed by the Sacrament of penance: I will say of the grievousness of heresy not all the things which might be said, nor even very many, lest I take up a speech without measure, but a few only, which may seem both to be necessary, and also able to suffice in this place.

For I will pass over that St. John Chrysostom, in the 50th homily to the people of Antioch, and St. Augustine, Book XXI, Chap. 25, of the City of God, make the heretical man far worse than Heathens, and Publicans: I will not mention that, as storms of winds are wont to stir, and trouble the seas, so heresy, whatever region it hath invaded, presently raiseth in it tumults, battles, seditions: I will keep silence, that our forefathers, whereas in other persecutions they awaited the enemy with a quiet mind; yet when the war of the faith came on, were all so moved in mind, that they were not satisfied with themselves, unless they gathered the whole forces of the Church, and, as if called out by a common trumpet, all ran together to resist. To drive away other vices, no man departed from his own station: to repel heresy, they doubted not to forsake their houses, and provinces, and to go to regions unknown, and far distant, with the greatest hazard of goods and of life. Other things well nigh infinite I will willingly suffer to be passed in silence, which yet would otherwise help me in some measure. This one thing I will say, that the perversity of heresy is by so much more grievous than all other

<!-- p. 55 (PDF 78) -->

crimes, and enormities: by how much the plague is more fearful, and more to be dreaded than common diseases.

There are two things, which make the plague deservedly horrible, and terrible above other diseases. One, that with the utmost swiftness it spreadeth its poison even to the heart, and extinguisheth a man, sound a little before, almost in a moment of time: The other, that when it killeth one, it infecteth a hundred others, and creepeth far, and wide so swiftly, that if to-day it hath invaded one house, in a short time it filleth the whole city with corpses.

Altogether the very same thing is heresy in souls, that the plague is in bodies. The first gift of grace, which we receive from the heavenly Father in conversion, and justification, the first motion of the heart reviving, the first sense of spiritual life, without any controversy, and doubt, is faith: thence afterwards by little and little the mind is stirred up to hope, the will is kindled to love, the tongue is loosed to confession, the hands are made ready for works. Since therefore heresy straightway striketh at the very heart of the soul, and in snatching away the gifts of grace cometh even to the first, and extinguisheth that beginning of the divine, and heavenly life: what pestilence can be thought of more pernicious, and more deadly than heresy?

The first foundation of the spiritual building, by the confession, and judgment of all, is faith. For thence the walls of hope rise by little and little, the roof of charity is stretched over, and at last the various and manifold ornaments of good works are added. The house of God, saith St. Augustine, sermon 22, on the words of the Apostle, is founded by believing, raised by hoping, perfected by loving: when therefore in the shaking, and moving of this building, there is no stopping at the roof, nor at the walls, but it cometh even to the digging out, and overthrowing of the lowest foundations, which heresy doth: how great, I pray, is that ruin? how great the loss? what hope then remaineth, when no part of the building is left, but the whole together at one stroke falleth utterly to the ground? The first ray of the divine light, which he kindleth in our hearts, who hath called us out of darkness into his marvellous light, what else is it than faith? for thence, beginning as it were from the dawn, the light of the just goeth forwards, and increaseth, as the Wise Man saith, even to perfect day.

Since therefore heresy suddenly snatcheth the day from the mind of man, and leaveth not even that first brightness of the dawn kindled: what is more horrible, more wretched, more deadly than heresy? I will say these same things more plainly. If any Catholic falleth into sin, if perchance he committeth theft, adultery, murder: it goeth wretchedly indeed with him. But nevertheless because there is still in him some sense of life, still the foundation of the building, still the light of the dawn, that is faith; he hath many, and great helps to everlasting

<!-- p. 56 (PDF 79) -->

salvation, he walketh not in darkness, he knoweth his physician, he can still out of the faith, which he hath, cry to God, build upon the foundation, call upon the common deliverer, implore his clemency, and mercy; nor is the motherly bosom of the Church wanting to him, and all the shops of spiritual medicines also stand open. But the heretical man hath none of these things, but, the light of faith being extinguished in his heart, he followeth after empty shadows, and phantasms; he knoweth not whither he goeth, and the more he runneth, the farther he departeth from the way, and is ever wrapped in thicker darkness, until from the inner darkness, which he suffereth in his mind, he come to that outer darkness, which is in hell fire. Nor indeed is this an amplification, but rather an extenuation.

Doth not a darkness most thick, and palpable seem to thee to be poured over those, who, though they would seem the wisest of all men, yet sometimes say such things, bring forth such things into the midst, sell such things for the Gospel, as even boys, and decrepit old women also would understand to be dreams, and dotages? what can be feigned more impious, and foolish than to make God, who is the fountain of goodness and justice, the author of all crimes, and enormities? But this Peter Martyr doth in the commentary on the second book of Kings, and also John Calvin in Book I of the *Institutes*, chap. 18, § 4.

What is more childish, and silly, than to say that God in a stone is, truly, and properly a stone; in iron, iron; in a trunk, a trunk; in a stock, a stock; in all things, all things? but this Michael Servetus teacheth in the sixth epistle to Calvin. What is more absurd, and against not only all reason, but also sense, than openly to assert, that infants while they are baptized, after the example of St. John the Baptist perceive certain divine motions with the inward senses, hear the promises of the Gospel, and give faith to God promising? But this Martin Luther openly asserteth, in the commentary on the third chapter of the epistle to the Galatians, and the Centuriators who followed him, Century II, chap. 4, nor do they so much as reverence St. Augustine, who in the epistle to Dardanus, question 2, left it written: If on account of John alone it is credible that all infants use counsel, and reason before the age: then also on account of Balaam's Ass, which once spoke wisely, men are to be admonished to look for the counsels of Asses in their deliberations.

Next, who would not be amazed that men could be found, who persuaded themselves, that nothing else is required for the justification of the wicked, than that they hold for certain that they are just, that their sins are forgiven them; so that now not the opinion dependeth on the truth of the thing, but on the contrary, the truth of the thing on the opinion; and yet this very incredible paradox is the fourth article of that most famous

<!-- p. 57 (PDF 80) -->

Confession of Augsburg, which is in the place of a Gospel to the greatest part of the heretics of our time.

What now shall we say of John Wiclef, whose sixth article, condemned in the Council of Constance, Sess. VIII, is this: "God ought to obey the Devil"? what of John Calvin, who in Book II of the *Institutes*, chap. 16, so seriously, so of set purpose teacheth, that our Lord Jesus Christ, which surely pious ears refuse to hear, at one time endured the torments of hell fire with the damned, and reprobate spirits? whence also there arose afterwards some who said, that Christ despaired on the Cross, and even was condemned to hell. With how foul a darkness,[^3] thinkest thou, was that mind held, which could think so great an impiety?

But in what place shall we set those words of Martin Luther, which he left written in the book *On the Councils*: "I had to do a little before with Nestorians, who most obstinately disputed against me, that the divinity of Christ cannot suffer"?

Behold, Christian Reader, with what, not so much errors they labour, as furies they are driven, in what darkness they are wrapped, into what precipices they rush, who have once made shipwreck concerning the faith: And yet I have not chosen to touch the far more absurd mysteries of the Anabaptists, and Libertines. Behold with how great a miracle God hath divided Israel from the Egyptians: where Israel, that is, the Catholic Church of Christ is, there is light, so that all altogether from the greatest even to the least acknowledge the way of justice, and truth: but where the Egyptians, that is, the heretics are, there is darkness horrible, and palpable, so that it often striketh even upon the very senses of man.

And would that the heretic hurt himself alone, and did not straightway spread his poison far, and wide, but that it was most truly written by the Apostle, that the words of heretics spread like a canker: this our age is a most ample witness. For who knoweth not that the Lutheran plague, sprung up a little before in Saxony, presently seized almost all Germany; thence going forth to the North, and East, consumed Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Gothland, Pannonia, Hungary; then borne with equal swiftness to the West, and South, in a short time laid waste France, England, Scotland, kingdoms once most flourishing; at the last crossed the Alps, and pierced even into Italy?

Which things being so, as they who labour under the plague, lest they infect the rest, are deservedly shut out from the company of others, and we dare neither enter their houses, nor touch anything that hath been theirs: so also the books, meetings, conversations, banquets of heretics, even from the first rise of the Church, by a most wise counsel, have been forbidden to all the faithful by our forefathers'

<!-- p. 58 (PDF 81) -->

authority. "Let no dealings," saith St. Cyprian (1), "be joined with such, let no banquets, or conversations be mingled; and let us be as separated from them, as they are fugitives from the Church." And St. Leo (2): "Shun," saith he, "the viperous conversations of heretics, let us have nothing in common with those, who, opposing the Catholic faith, are Christians in name alone." Blessed Athanasius also in the life of Antony the Great: "Antony," saith he, "so detested heretics, that he said to all, that one ought not even to come near them." What of this, that the Apostle Paul so often, and so seriously, speaking of heretics, presseth that saying: *Now these avoid*? And St. John the Apostle, and Evangelist, as Irenæus relateth against Valentinus, (3) when he had once entered a bath, presently, as soon as he knew that Cerinthus the heretic was within, leaped out of the bath unwashed; desiring, to wit, by his example to admonish all posterity, that heresy is a contagious disease, and to be guarded against with all one's strength, and with the utmost foresight of mind.
