CHAPTER II.

*That the word of God is contained in the books which are called Canonical.*

In the first place Moses, the Prophets, Christ himself, John, the Apostles, either themselves confirmed the divine dogmas from the Scriptures, or at least exhorted others to read the Scriptures, nor did they ever send any man back to the judgment of the inward spirit, the Scriptures being neglected. Whose testimonies shall be brought forward by us, not because we think that they are made much of[^1] by the adversaries; but lest the Scriptures, whose authority the adversaries sometimes abuse against us who deservedly venerate them, should seem to make for their opinion.

Moses therefore: *If thou perceive,* saith he, *that there be among you a hard and doubtful matter in judgment,* etc. And below: *Thou shalt do whatsoever they shall say, that preside in the place which the Lord shall choose, and what they shall teach thee according to his law* (3). Where holy Moses teacheth plainly enough that the controversies arisen among the people of God are to be decided out of the law of the Lord. In the same manner Isaias also crieth: *To the law rather, and to the testimony* (4). And Malachias: *The lips of the Priest shall keep knowledge, and they shall seek the law at his mouth: because he is the Angel of the Lord of hosts* (5). David also: *Blessed are they who search his testimonies* (6).

Christ moreover first confirmeth his own mission by the testimony of Scripture, bringing forward those words of the Prophet: *The spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he hath anointed me,* etc. (7). Then he confuteth the Sadducees from the divine writings, when he saith: *Do ye not therefore err, because you know not*

[Right column]

*the scriptures?* And below: *And as concerning the dead that they rise again, have you not read in the book of Moses* etc. (8) Further, by what other argument did he put the Pharisees to silence than by that which he drew from David? And again he sendeth the same Pharisees, not to some inward spirit, but to the divine Scriptures, saying: *Search the scriptures* (9).

Now John the Baptist brought as witness of his mission, not the judgment of the inward spirit, but Isaias the Prophet; for being asked who he was: *I,* saith he, *am the voice of one crying out in the wilderness, make straight the way of the Lord, as said the prophet Isaias* (10). Of the Bereans this is related, not without praise, in the Acts of the Apostles, that when they had heard Paul the Apostle, they daily searched the Scriptures, whether these things were so (11). And the Apostles themselves, Peter, Paul, John, James, Jude, not only in their several epistles frequently use the testimonies of the Law and the Prophets, which it would be too long to rehearse in this place; but sometimes also they proclaim the authority of the Scriptures in general: *We have,* saith St. Peter, *the more firm prophetical word: whereunto you do well to attend, as to a light that shineth in a dark place* (12). And St. Paul: *From thy infancy,* saith he, *thou hast known the holy scriptures, which can instruct thee to salvation. All scripture, inspired of God, is profitable to teach, to reprove, to correct, to instruct in justice, that the man of God may be perfect, furnished to every good work* (13). Lastly God himself to Josue: *Let not the book of this law,* saith he, *depart from thy mouth: but thou shalt meditate on it day and night.* (14) They therefore fight with Moses, with the Prophets, with the Apostles, with Christ himself, and God the Father, and the Holy Ghost, who despise the sacred writings and the divine oracles.

Next, the rule of the Catholic Faith ought to be certain and known; for if it be not known, it will not be a rule to us: if it be not certain, it will not be a rule at all. Now the private revelation of the Spirit, however certain it be in itself, can yet in no wise be known to us, unless perchance it be confirmed by divine testimonies, that is, by true miracles; of which they especially suffer an extreme want, who in this our age no less than the rest boast of the spirit. For who shall assure me that the Anabaptist doth not lie,

when he saith that he is inspired by the spirit? But grant that he doth not lie: by what way shall I know that that spirit is a spirit of light, and not a spirit of darkness? And since so many at this time glory that they have the Holy Ghost for guide and teacher, and yet so disagree among themselves in minds and opinions that some are heretics to others, it surely cannot be that all think rightly. But if either all are deceived, or, which is altogether necessary, some: who will dare to affirm that he is not of the number of those who are deluded by the spirit of Satan?

But than the sacred Scriptures, which are contained in the Prophetical and Apostolical writings, nothing is better known, nothing more certain; so that he must needs be most foolish who denieth that credit is to be given to them. For that they are most known, the Christian world is witness, and the consent of all nations, among whom for many ages now they have always held the highest authority: and that they are most certain and most true, and contain not human inventions, but divine oracles.

First, the witness is the truth of the prophecies, of which St. Augustine speaketh thus: "The credit of Scripture hath not undeservedly a wonderful authority in the world and among all nations, which, among the other true things that it spoke, it foretold with true divinity should believe it." And again: "How much less, saith he, are those writings to be believed, which, full as it were of fabulous antiquities, they would bring forward against the authority of the best known and divine books, which foretold that the whole world should believe it, and which the whole world, as was foretold by it, hath believed: which showeth that it hath related past things truly, from these things which it foretold[^2] as to come, since they are fulfilled with so great truth (1)."

Secondly, the witness is a certain incredible and plainly divine agreement and concord of so many men, who in divers places, times, tongues, occasions wrote the sacred volumes, so that they seem to have been not so much divers writers, as divers pens of one writer. And so rightly Theodoret, in the preface on the Psalms, and Saint Gregory, in the preface on Job, judged that the tongues and hands of the sacred Writers are to be called nothing else than pens of the Holy Ghost.

And St. Augustine, speaking of this admirable concord of the Scriptures:

[Right column]

"Our authors, saith he, in whom the Canon of the sacred writings is not in vain fixed and bounded, far be it that they should in any way disagree among themselves. Whence not undeservedly, when they wrote those things, that God spoke to them, or by them, hath been believed, not by a few babblers in schools and gymnasia with contentious disputations, but in fields and cities, with learned and unlearned, by so many and so great peoples. They themselves indeed ought to have been few, lest that should grow cheap by multitude which ought to be dear by religion, and yet not so few that their agreement is not to be wondered at. For neither in the multitude of Philosophers, who also by literary labour have left monuments of their tenets, would one easily find any among whom all things that they held agree (2)."

Thirdly, the witness is God himself, who at times hath defended this his Scripture from human profanation by a heavenly punishment. For when King Ptolemy wondered why none of the ancient Historians or Poets had made mention of the divine books, Demetrius Phalereus answered, as Josephus and Eusebius, *Preparation of the Gospel,* relate from Aristæus (3): That this Scripture is divine, and given by God himself; therefore if any profane men wished to touch it, they were struck by God and straightway drew back. And he affirmed that a certain Theopompus, because he had wished to adorn something of the divine volumes in Greek speech, was suddenly, by a certain agitation of mind sent upon him from God, vehemently troubled, and forced to desist from the work begun: and that Theodorus, a writer of Tragedies, because he had determined to transfer I know not what from the divine Scripture to a certain play, was struck blind and deprived of his eyes: so by a sudden calamity the notable rashness was straightway repressed.

Fourthly, the witness is Scripture itself: if its predictions of things to come were true, as the event afterwards proved, why should not its testimonies of things present be true? True therefore is that which David saith: *The spirit of the Lord hath spoken by me, and his word by my tongue* (4). True that which Zachary saith: *Blessed be the Lord God of Israel etc. As he spoke by the mouth of his holy Prophets, who are from the beginning* (5). True that which

Peter saith: *The holy men of God spoke, inspired by the Holy Ghost* (1). And Paul: *All scripture, inspired of God* (2). True lastly that which all the prophets repeat again and again: *Thus saith the Lord.*

Lastly, the witness is the well-nigh infinite number of divine miracles, which have been wrought in all ages to prove those dogmas which are contained in these Writings.

Wherefore, since sacred Scripture is the most certain and safest rule of believing, he surely will not be of sound mind who, neglecting it, shall commit himself to the judgment of the inward spirit, often deceitful, and always uncertain. And well did Saint Augustine, in the prologue *Of Christian Doctrine,* admonish Christians in these words: "Let us beware of such most proud and most dangerous temptations, and let us rather consider that the Apostle Paul himself, though cast down and instructed by a divine and heavenly voice, was yet sent to a man, that he might receive the Sacraments, and be joined to the Church: and that the Centurion Cornelius, although an Angel announced to him that his prayers were heard, and his alms regarded, was yet delivered to Peter to be instructed; by whom he should not only receive the Sacraments, but also hear what was to be believed, what to be hoped, what to be loved."

To this is added, that the providence of God so ruleth and governeth each thing as the nature of each requireth: and the nature of men requireth this, that, since we are endowed with soul and body, and take in bodily things more easily than spiritual; by those things which are perceived by the senses of the body, as by certain steps, we be led to things spiritual and heavenly. God therefore doth not teach all men commonly by inward inspiration what he would have believed of himself, or what done by his own: but he willed to instruct us by bodily letters, which we might both see and read. "From that city, whence we are pilgrims, saith Augustine, letters have come to us; they are the Scriptures, which exhort us to live well (3)."

Nor indeed, if David, Moses, Isaias, Peter, Paul, John, and some few others, admitted in a manner into the company of the Angels, drew wisdom by themselves from the very fountain of truth, will the rest of men also forthwith have need neither of the divine writings, nor of any teachers. For there ought to be one condition of foundations, another of walls, one of mountains, another of hills.

[Right column]

Are all Apostles? are all Prophets? are all foundations? are all mountains? and if all are mountains, where are the hills? if all are foundations, where is the house? if all are Apostles and Prophets, where is the congregation of people? of which it is written in Psalm VII: *And a congregation of people shall surround thee* (4). We therefore are built, as the Apostle saith, upon the foundation of the Apostles and Prophets (5), by whose preaching and writings we are instructed: but they, who themselves saw, and were ministers of the word, are upheld by and rest upon the chief corner stone itself, Christ Jesus.

When the sun riseth, the tops of the mountains are lighted up; and thence the rays of light are conveyed to the hills, and even to the lowest valleys. So, according to Psalm LXXI, the mountains receive peace for the people, and the hills justice: "The mountains, saith St. Augustine, are lofty souls, the hills are little souls; but therefore do the mountains receive peace, that the hills may be able to receive justice. What is the justice which the hills receive? Faith: because the just man liveth by faith. But the lesser souls would not receive faith, unless the greater souls, which are called mountains, were enlightened by wisdom itself, that they might be able to pass over to the little ones what the little ones can take." And below: "When we lift up our eyes to the Scriptures, because the Scriptures were ministered by men, we lift up our eyes to the mountains, from whence help shall come to us (6)."

Lastly, if in any human Commonwealth, the laws and institutions of the ancestors being taken away, that were lawful to every man which by his own natural prudence he judged equitable and just; how great a disturbance of all things, how great a confusion would follow? in how short a time would that whole Commonwealth utterly perish? and yet there is no man who hath not some understanding in human things, and who is altogether void of all judgment. What then would come to pass, if in that Commonwealth which is divine rather than human, and in which many things must of necessity be believed above natural reason, many things done above human strength, the sacred words inspired of God were taken out of the midst, and the inward inspiration alone were to be waited for and followed? what of this, that the greatest part of men consisteth of the rude and unskilled, who have never perceived, not even in a dream, that anything was shown them from God; what then of these? shall they wait always?
