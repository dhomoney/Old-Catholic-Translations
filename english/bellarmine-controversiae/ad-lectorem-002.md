# TO THE READER

Three causes in all, Christian Reader, have moved me to suffer these disputations of mine, such as they are, to come forth into the light. The first reason that moved me was this, that I judge it not only not to hurt, but even to profit the cause of the Church, if very many write at this time. It is the opinion of St. Augustine, (1) both known to many, and worthy to be known by all, that it is to be wished, where heresies flourish, that whosoever are endowed with any ability of writing should all write, although they be about to write not only of the same matters, but perhaps even the same things in other words. For it is expedient that the heretics understand that in the camp of the Catholics there is not one or two, but many, who dare to meet them face to face. This advantage moreover cometh from the writing of many, that the Catholic books themselves are more quickly and more easily brought to the hands of all, and, while some men light upon some books and others upon others, yet all are furnished with the same arms in the common danger. Which opinion of a most prudent and most learned man both the examples of the ancient Fathers and daily experience confirm. When the Arian heresy was once raging, there wrote with care and at large, both Greeks and Latins well nigh without number: Athanasius, Basil, Epiphanius, Didymus, both Gregories, both Cyrils, Hilary, Ambrose, Augustine, Gregory of Bætica, Idacius Clarus, Phœgadius,[^1] Fulgentius, and others. And they so agree among themselves, and as it were conspire together, whether they bring forward testimonies of Scripture for the Catholic faith, or answer the arguments of the heretics, that all seem to repeat and to press home the same things, and all even to have received them from some one man. Nor is that wont to be, nor can it be, imputed to them as a fault. For that many wrote, the cause was the ardour of faith, and the zeal of religion, which the Holy Ghost had kindled in their breasts; that they wrote the same things came from this, that they had drawn the antidote of the same poison from the same spirit, and with the same defences, against the same enemies, for the same possession, under the same author and leader, God, they were fighting.

(1) Book I *On the Trinity*, chap. iii. *Against Lying*, chap. vi.

But if those ancients with so great ardour both wrote themselves, and stirred up others to write, when the world laboured under one or two heresies; and when among the heretics not very many wrote; and when the art of Printing had not yet been devised, which out of one volume thrusteth forth swarms of books almost in a moment; what were it fitting should be done in this our age, wherein both innumerable heresies rend the Church on every side, and they are rare among the adversaries who do not write something, whose books no longer creep as a canker, but fly about like armies of locusts: and lastly it is certain and well ascertained, that heretical writings not only bring a plague daily to many men, but have sometimes brought ruin to whole provinces? This therefore was the cause why I suffered these disputations to be committed to the press; for otherwise I shrank from that thing in no small measure. But a second reason urged me more vehemently.

Writers of all orders and nations have in this age put forth excellent volumes, and have nobly defended the cause of the common faith against the common enemies, with minds conspiring together. And since one hath undertaken one argument to be explained, and another another, and hath laboured with all his strength in handling it as exactly as might be;[^2] there are extant to-day, upon well nigh every head of the controversies, very many disputations of divers authors, and those most learned, and (as was necessary) most long. But not many abound in so great a store of books, and in so great leisure, that out of disputations so various and so prolix they can in a short time come to know all the controversies, and easily provide themselves with those defences and arms which they need for defending, according to their power, all the parts of the faith, who among the Germans, the French, the Britons, the Poles, the Bohemians, the Pannonians, the Illyrians, the Swiss, and other nations labouring under heresy, must discharge the office of pastors and teachers. Wherefore after the most learned labours of famous men, this also seemed to be wanting, that all the controversies should be brought together as it were into one body, and should be so set forth and explained by a certain order and method, that in a little time, at small cost, and with no great labour, they who would might fetch arms out of one armoury, with which being at least in some sort furnished, they might without great danger to themselves stand in the battle against the enemies. Which indeed I have endeavoured to accomplish, not truly of my own accord, but at the bidding of my Superiors; whether I have achieved it, shall be for others to judge. But because the kind of writing itself seemed most profitable, we have given these disputations to the light, that they who will may use them, until something fuller and more laboured in the same kind come forth from some other, who may more abound both in leisure and in wit.

There remaineth the third cause, which not only urged me, but plainly also, as I said at the beginning, drove and compelled me to commit these labours to posterity. When Gregory XIII, Supreme Pontiff, of blessed memory, out of zeal to help the German and the English nation, had founded two most ample Colleges of German and of English youths in the City

<!-- p. 51 (PDF 74) -->

of Rome, that is, in the very citadel of religion; this charge was committed to me, that I should explain to the students of those Colleges the controversies of the faith, as diligently and as plainly as could be done, in our School; and should arm them, according to my slender power, as new soldiers of the Church, who were to meet the forces of hell in their own provinces. That occasion therefore being offered, I set upon the thing, and entered upon such a manner of disputing, that I might embrace all the controversies of the faith, and partly divide them into their own members, partly join and couple them with others, so that one might be linked with another, and one hang upon and be born of another, and out of all there might arise one body of doctrine, whole and perfect, and complete in all its parts. Which disputations of ours, first taken down by the hearers in the School while I was teaching, then copied again and again by many, not without great labour or cost, and carried to divers places, began to be demanded for the press with so great earnestness, that some even threatened that they would commit them to print against our will and in spite of our resistance. Wherefore I was compelled myself to send forth the work, as yet rude and unpolished (for, being hindered by the daily office of teaching, I could not put the last hand to it), lest to my great grief I should see printed the disputations taken down in the schools, torn, that is, and maimed, and everywhere filled with faults.

I would have thee, good Reader, if there shall be anything in these commentaries which may be of use to thee for the glory of God and the profit of the Catholic Church, to use it, and to give thanks to God, the one author of good things, not to me: but if anything shall meet thee in reading, as will often happen, either too brief, or somewhat uneven, or said less properly and elegantly: remember this, that these things were not at the beginning written with the intent that they should be published, but that they might help the memory in teaching; and attend not to how each thing is said, but to what is said.
