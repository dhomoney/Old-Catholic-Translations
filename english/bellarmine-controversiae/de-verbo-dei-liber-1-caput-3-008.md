CHAPTER III.

*The objection from the words of Blessed Paul is refuted: The letter killeth, but the spirit quickeneth.*

"The Apostle Paul, say they, in express words distinguisheth the letter from the spirit, and plainly referreth the former to the old Testament, which he also calleth killing; but the latter he affirmeth to be proper to the new Testament, and nameth it quickening. *He hath made us,* saith he, *fit ministers of the new testament, not in the letter, but in the spirit. For the letter killeth, but the spirit quickeneth* (1). Therefore, as the old Testament is the Jews', and pertaineth nothing to Christian men; so neither shall the divine Scriptures, which are nothing else than letters, pertain to Christian men."

To this argument we answer easily; and first, if the matter standeth so, and the sacred writings pertain nothing to us, why doth Christ himself say, *John* v: *Search the scriptures?* why did Paul write so many letters? why, I *Tim.* iv, did he admonish his disciple to attend unto reading? But in truth the Apostle Paul would have neither the sacred Scripture to be understood by the killing letter, nor the inward speaking of the Spirit by the quickening spirit. Nor are the letter and the spirit anything else to Paul than the law and grace to John. *The law,* saith John, chap. i, *was given by Moses; grace and truth came by Jesus Christ* (2). And St. Augustine, *Against Faustus*: "The same law, saith he, which was given by Moses, was made grace and truth by Jesus Christ, when the spirit was added to the letter, that the justice of the law might begin to be fulfilled, which, not being fulfilled, made men guilty by transgression also (3)." Now that Paul by the letter signifieth the law,

[Right column]

can be understood from the words that follow. For when he had said: *He hath made us fit ministers of the new testament, not in the letter, but in the spirit. For the letter killeth, but the spirit quickeneth;* he straightway added: *Now if the ministration of death, engraven with letters upon stones, was glorious; so that the children of Israel could not steadfastly behold the face of Moses, for the glory of his countenance, which is made void: how shall not the ministration of the spirit be rather in glory* (4)? By which words he left no room for doubting but that by the killing letter the law written upon stones is to be understood.

But it will be worth the labour to explain briefly why that law given by Moses is called the letter, why killing, why proper to the old Testament; lest, while we refute the Libertines and Swenckfeldians, we should seem to grant something to the Lutherans.

And the first doubt indeed will be very easy and almost none, if Moses be compared with Christ, and the law with grace. For Moses, when he carried down to the people the law given by God, carried down nothing else than letters graven in stone; but Christ neither wrote any letters, nor brought any written by another, but breathed into the hearts of his own the spirit of love and grace, which cannot even be written. And so deservedly the law of Christ is named spirit and grace; the law of Moses, the letter.

But why the law is said to kill, the opinion of all is not the same. Origen,[^1] *Against Celsus* (5), seemeth to signify that the killing letter is Scripture itself, when it is expounded according to the proper signification of the words; but that the same is the quickening spirit, if it be expounded mystically and spiritually: "He named it the letter, saith he, when we take the divine writings according to the sense; but the spirit, when according to the understanding." And more clearly in Book VII, before the middle: "We say, saith he, that the law is twofold, to wit, one according to the letter, the other according to the meaning, as our forefathers also taught. Now that which is taken so as it is written, not so much we, as God by a certain Prophet, nameth a law not good, and commandments not good. Whom our Paul imitating pronounced that the letter killeth, which is all one as if he had said that the words kill. But the Spirit, saith he, quickeneth, which is as much as if he had written that we attain life by the understanding of them."

Thus Origen; whose opinion, if it be so taken that nothing at all in the divine Scriptures can be expounded according to the letter, containeth an undoubted error, and hath long since been exploded by the Fathers. See Epiphanius, epistle to John, and in the heresy of Origen; Chrysostom, homily XIII on Genesis; Jerome, in the epistle to Pammachius, *Of the Errors of John of Jerusalem,* and on chap. x of Daniel; Augustine, Book XIII *Of the City of God,* chap. 21, and Book VIII *On Genesis,* chap. 1. But if it be taken so, that those things which are said figuratively are forbidden to be explained according to the proper signification of the words; the opinion is indeed true, but in no wise fitteth this place of Paul. For the Apostle, when he saith that the letter killeth, speaketh not of the figures of the Scriptures, but of those most known commandments which were written on the two tables of stone. For thus he saith: *The letter killeth, but the spirit quickeneth. Now if the ministration of death, engraven with letters upon stones, was glorious,* etc. And again in[^2] the epistle to the Romans, where he several times repeateth that the law killeth, he bringeth forward no other law than that: Thou shalt not covet. "And surely, to use the words of Augustine, it is not something said figuratively, which is not to be taken according to the sound of the letter, when it is said: Thou shalt not covet; but it is a most plain and most wholesome commandment: which if any man shall fulfil, he shall have no sin at all (1)."

Now St. John Chrysostom, on II Corinthians, chap. iii, and they who have followed him, Theodoret, Theophylact, Œcumenius, as also the Commentary which is attributed to St. Ambrose, and some others, teach that the killing letter is the law punishing; the quickening spirit, grace delivering from sins. For the law commanded not only adulterers and robbers, but even him who had gathered sticks on the Sabbath, to be put to death (2). But grace absolveth even the most criminal men, and those defiled with every kind of enormities, by the laver of regeneration, or by the word of reconciliation.

But this explanation also doth not altogether satisfy: for the divine law, as it appointeth punishments for the wicked, so it appointeth rewards for the good; and as it is written: *Cursed be he that abideth not in the words of this law, and fulfilleth them not in work* (3); so it is also written, *Lev.* xviii: *Keep my laws and my judgments, which if a man do, he shall live in them* (4). Why then is the law said to kill, and not also to bring life? for if

[Right column]

it can deservedly be called the killing letter for this reason, that it killeth its transgressors; by like reason it may be called the quickening letter, because it prolongeth life to them that keep it.

Most true therefore, and most agreeable to the words of the Apostle, is the opinion of St. Augustine, to wit, that the law without grace was called the killing letter by the Apostle Paul for two causes. First, because, since it cannot be fulfilled, it maketh him to whom it is given in some sort a transgressor. *The law,* saith the same Apostle, *Rom.* iv, *worketh wrath. For where there is no law, neither is there transgression*[^3] (5). Secondly, because by the very prohibition it increaseth concupiscence: for, as one rightly saith: *Nitimur in vetitum semper, cupimusque negata* (We strive ever after that which is forbidden, and desire the things denied us): *Sin,* saith the Apostle, *taking occasion by the commandment, wrought in me all manner of concupiscence* (6), and St. Augustine, *Of the Spirit and the Letter*: "That doctrine, saith he, which commandeth: Thou shalt not covet, is a good and praiseworthy law: but where the Holy Ghost doth not help, inspiring a good concupiscence in place of the evil concupiscence, that is, pouring forth charity in our hearts; surely that law, though good, increaseth the evil desire by forbidding: as the rush of water, if it cease not to flow in that direction, becometh more vehement when a bar is set against it, and when it hath overcome the mass thereof, being hurled down in a greater heap, rolleth more violently down the slope. And this it is, that sin deceiveth by the commandment, and by it killeth, when transgression also is added, which is none where there is no law (7)."

Wherefore the law killeth neither of itself, nor by any viciousness of its own, but by occasion only, and that taken, not given. For *sin,* as the Apostle saith, *taking occasion by the commandment, seduced me, and by it killed me. Wherefore the law indeed is holy, and the commandment holy, and just, and good. Was that then which is good, made death unto me? God forbid. But sin, that it may appear sin, by that which is good, wrought death in me* (8).

The last question remaineth: why the law, or the letter, is said to be proper to the old Testament. For neither in the old Testament did the law prevail without grace, or the letter without the spirit; since so many most just men flourished at that time, men *walking,* as Luke saith of Zachary and Elizabeth, *in all the commandments and justifications of the Lord without blame* (9): nor in the

new Testament doth the spirit reign without the letter, or grace without the law, since Christ said: *You have heard that it was said to them of old: Thou shalt not kill. And whosoever shall kill shall be in danger of the judgment. But I say to you, that whosoever is angry with his brother, shall be in danger of the judgment* (1); and what followeth. And again: *Unless a man be born again of water and the Holy Ghost, he cannot enter into the kingdom*[^6] *of God* (2); and: *Going, teach ye all nations; baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost: teaching them to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you* (3).

What then is that which the Apostle saith: *He hath made us fit ministers of the new testament, not in the letter, but in the spirit* (4), whereby he shutteth out the letter, that is, the law, from the new Testament, in which we see so many laws prescribed by Christ himself? But this question is easily solved, if we discern from the proper end of each Testament what of itself and properly pertaineth to each. The proper end of the old Testament was to bring this about, that mankind, corrupted and vitiated in the first parent himself as in the root, should be admonished of its sickness and wounds, and thence, fear and sorrow being conceived, should seek the Physician. To this end the law leadeth. *By the law is the knowledge of sin,* saith the Apostle (5), and again: *I do not know sin, but by the law* (6), and Blessed Augustine: "The profit of the law, saith he, is to convince man of his infirmity, and to compel him to implore the medicine of grace, which is in Christ" (7).

Wherefore of itself and properly the old Testament, which is contained in laws and letters, bringeth fear, engendereth unto bondage, as Paul speaketh, *Rom.* viii and *Gal.* iv. But they who are read to have been just and free under the old Testament, were such not by the letter of the old Testament, but by the spirit of the new Testament. "Those saints, saith St. Augustine, dispensed the old Testament[^7] according to the fitness of the time, but belonged to the new Testament (8)." And *Of the Merits and Remission of Sins*: "The kingdom of death the grace of the Saviour alone destroyeth in any man whatsoever, which wrought also in the ancient saints, whosoever, before Christ came in the Flesh, yet belonged to his helping grace, not to the letter of the law, which could only command,

[Right column]

not help (9)." Now the end of the new Testament is no other than to cure, to heal, to deliver. And all this grace performeth. *Unhappy*[^8] *man that I am,* saith Paul, *who shall deliver me from the body of this death? The grace of God, by Jesus Christ our Lord* (10): and St. Augustine in the book *Of the Spirit and the Letter*: "By the law, saith he, is the knowledge of sin, by grace the healing of the soul from the fault of sin (11)."

Therefore the new Testament of itself and properly bringeth not the law, but grace, and is the ministry not of the letter, but of the spirit alone, and is nothing else than the charity of God poured forth in our hearts by the Holy Ghost who is given to us, which the Prophet foretold so long before, when he saith, *Jer.*: *Behold the days shall come, saith the Lord, and I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel, and with the house of Juda: not according to the covenant which I made with your*[^9] *fathers, in the day that I took them by the hand to bring them out of the land of Egypt: the covenant which they made void, and I had dominion over them, saith the Lord. But this shall be the covenant that I will make with the house of Israel, after those days, saith the Lord: I will give my law in their bowels, and I will write it in their heart* (12). And so, if the question be of that which of itself and properly pertaineth to each Testament, Paul said most truly: *He hath made us fit ministers of the new testament, not in the letter, but in the spirit.* Nor less truly John:[^10] *The law was given by Moses; grace and truth came by Jesus Christ* (13).

But although the new Testament of itself and properly bringeth not laws, yet it embraceth laws in a twofold manner. For first, all those laws which have regard to living well and blessedly the grace of the new Testament doth not destroy, but confirmeth: inasmuch as it supplieth strength to human infirmity for fulfilling them: *Do we,* saith the Apostle, *destroy the law through faith? God forbid: but we establish the law,* and below: *For what the law could not do, in that it was weak through the flesh; God sending his own Son, in the likeness of sinful flesh and of sin, hath condemned sin in the flesh; that the justification of the law might be fulfilled in us* (14), and St. Augustine, book *Of the Spirit and the Letter*: "The law therefore was given, saith he, that grace might be sought; grace was given, that the law might be fulfilled. was the law not fulfilled, but by the fault of the wisdom of the flesh. Which fault was to be shown by the law, healed by grace (1)."

Next, Christ also made certain new laws, as of Baptism, of the Eucharist, of the other Sacraments, whether to be administered or to be received: because, although Christ did not therefore come to us that, as another Moses, he might press us with the weight of a new law, but that he might by the help of grace raise us up lying under the burden of the law; yet he thought that to the old commandments of morals those most wholesome laws were to be added, which, while they lead us to the Sacraments, and through the Sacraments bring us to grace, not only do not press with the burden of weights, but even lift up after the manner of wings, as Augustine speaketh in the book *Of the Perfection of Justice.* But of the first objection enough hath been said.

The other objections, because they are common to the Swenckfeldians and Libertines with the Lutherans and Calvinists, will be more conveniently solved in that disputation in which, the Lord helping, we shall treat of the interpreter of the Scriptures and the judge of controversies.
