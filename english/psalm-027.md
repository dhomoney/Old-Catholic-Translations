BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XXVII.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*A psalm for David himself.*

[Margin: It is Christ who speaketh in this Psalm.]

Theodoret and Euthymius expound this Psalm of David fleeing the persecution of Saul. But many things are against it: *first*, that this is not indicated in the title, as is elsewhere almost always wont to be done, when a Psalm looketh to a history. *Secondly*, that in this Psalm he who speaketh calleth himself a king; but David at that time was not yet king. *Thirdly*, that in the same Psalm he who speaketh saith that he hath been heard, and that his flesh hath flourished again: which certainly cannot agree with David for that time in which he was fleeing from Saul. Some more recent writers expound it of David now king, and perfectly delivered from the persecution of Saul. But if it were so, he would not say: "Unto thee will I cry, O Lord, be not thou silent to me;" but he would say: "Unto thee, O Lord, have I cried," etc. Therefore the exposition of St. Augustine and St. Jerome seemeth to be preferred, who will have it that the Psalm was sung by David in the person of Christ: for David, who foresaw at once the passion and

<!-- p. 158 (PDF 169) -->

glorification of Christ, could fitly join them together, as he also did in Psalm xxi, of which very long and very beautiful Psalm, this which we have in hand to be explained seemeth to be as it were an abridgment.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. UNTO THEE WILL I CRY, O LORD: O MY GOD, BE NOT THOU SILENT TO ME: LEST THOU BE SILENT TO ME, I BECOME LIKE THEM THAT GO DOWN INTO THE PIT.**[^1]

They are the words of Christ hanging on the cross, and asking a speedy resurrection. For *Deus meus* (my God), in the Hebrew there is צורי *tsuri*, *my rock*, or *my strong one*: and although this very name is put to signify God, and the Seventy rightly rendered, *my God*; yet from the force of the Hebrew word we understand that God is invoked in this place, because he is most mighty, and can command death itself, to restore Christ quickly. For *ne sileas a me* (be not thou silent to me), St. Jerome rendereth, *ne obsurdescas mihi* (be not deaf to me). But it is to be known that the word חרש signifieth *to hold one's peace, to be silent, to show oneself deaf*. And so the sense is, *be not silent*, as though thou hadst not heard my voice, show not thyself as it were deaf to me. That phrase, *a me* (from me), is the same as *mihi* (to me), by a Hebrew phrase. Briefly he asketh to be heard, and to be answered that God will do what is asked. That which followeth, *lest thou be silent to me, I become like them that go down into the pit*, containeth the cause why he would be answered, namely because, unless God hear and answer, he will be like other mortal men, who are dissolved by death, and go down to hell not to return. "Lest thou be silent to me," that is, lest perhaps thou hear not, and hence it will come to pass, that I become in all things like "them that go down into the pit," that is, those who die, and go down to hell, without hope of rising again, save at the last day. The sense might also be: If thou hear not, I shall be like dead men; for as the dead can do nothing at all, so man without the help of God can do nothing at all. For the Hebrew word בור *bor* signifieth *a cistern, a pit, a deep hole*; it can therefore be taken for *the sepulchre*, but better for the underground place of souls, as *Isa.* xiv: "But yet thou shalt be brought down to hell, into the depth of the pit," and chap. xxxviii: "They that go down into the pit shall not look for thy truth;" as it is also taken in that prayer of the Church: "Deliver the souls of all the faithful departed from the pains of hell, and from the deep pit, deliver them from the lion's mouth," etc.

[Margin: Man without the help of God can do nothing at all.]

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. HEAR, O LORD, THE VOICE OF MY SUPPLICATION, WHEN I PRAY TO THEE; WHEN I LIFT UP MY HANDS TO THY HOLY TEMPLE.**

What he had said before, *ne sileas* (be not thou silent), and *ne taceas* (hold not thy peace), he now saith more plainly: "Hear, O Lord, the voice of my supplication;" for he would have God answer, that this might be a sign of his being heard. That word, *Domine* (O Lord), is not in the Hebrew, but only, *hear the voice of my supplication*. St. Jerome, in the epistle *to Sunnia and Fretela*, thinketh that it was added by some one for the sake of explanation: perhaps also it hath fallen out of our copies, and was present in those copies which the Seventy used. That phrase, *dum oro ad te* (when I pray to thee), is in the Hebrew more expressively בשועי, *when I cry to thee*, that is, when I pray "with a strong cry," as the Apostle speaketh, *Heb.* v. That phrase, *when I lift up my hands*, signifieth by a sign that very same prayer: for the Hebrews were wont to lift their hands on high when they prayed, of which ceremony the sacred books are full: but the Gentiles also imitated the same rite. And so it is the same, *when I pray to thee*, and *when I lift up my hands to thy temple*. But the Prophet alluded meanwhile to the hands of the Lord stretched out on the cross, and lifted up to heaven; for then the Lord could most truly say: "When I cry to thee," and "when I lift up my hands," etc., when he prayed from the cross. For the word, *ad templum* (to the temple), in the Hebrew there is דביר, which properly signifieth *the oracle*, whence God gave answers. But in this place, whether temple, or oracle, it is taken for the heavenly temple and oracle.

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. DRAW ME NOT AWAY TOGETHER WITH THE WICKED; AND WITH THE WORKERS OF INIQUITY DESTROY ME NOT.**

Christ alone could truly say this: for he alone was in all ways "separated from sinners," as the Apostle saith, *Heb.* vii. And as he alone was the one upon whom sin could not fall, so most justly he asketh that he be not judged, and perish not with sinners, but rather slay death itself, and rising from the dead, triumph gloriously over the Prince of death, and over death itself. Rightly in the edition of Sixtus V and Clement VIII it hath been corrected, *ne trahas* (draw not), where before was read, *ne tradas* (deliver not): for since both the Hebrew word and the Greek signify *to draw*, it is likely that through the likeness of the letters, by the fault of copyists, *ne tradas* crept into the Latin edition. That phrase, *ne perdas* (destroy not), is not in the Hebrew, but was added for the sake of explanation. And so the sense is: Draw me not to destruction with the rest of men, who are sinners, since I am not a sinner.

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. WHO SPEAK PEACE WITH THEIR NEIGHBOUR, BUT EVILS ARE IN THEIR HEARTS.**

He describeth sinners by that sin which is more universal, and more common; for therefore in another Psalm it is said: "Every man is a liar," *Ps.* cxv; and in another: "Deliver me from the unjust and deceitful man," *Ps.* xliii;[^2] and of Christ the apostle Peter saith, I *Epist.* chap. ii: "Who did no sin, neither was guile found in his mouth," as though guile extended almost as widely as sin. Certainly there are very many, who would seem friends, and to wish well

<!-- p. 159 (PDF 170) -->

to their neighbours, and yet, blinded by self-love, have evil in the heart, that is, are moved with hatred or envy toward the same neighbours.

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. GIVE THEM ACCORDING TO THEIR WORKS, AND ACCORDING TO THE WICKEDNESS OF THEIR INVENTIONS.**

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. ACCORDING TO THE WORKS OF THEIR HANDS GIVE THOU TO THEM: RENDER TO THEM THEIR REWARD.**

This is not an imprecation, but a prophecy, as we have often noted elsewhere: for by these words it is signified that this will altogether come to pass, that the evil come to an evil end, unless indeed from evil they become good. The sense therefore is: "Thou shalt give to sinners according to their works," that is, thou shalt visit them with that punishment which their works deserve. "And according to the wickedness of their inventions," supply, thou shalt give to them, that is, as they out of their malice have thought out and invented various ways of vexing the just: so by thy wisdom thou shalt find various ways of tormenting sinners. And lastly, "thou shalt render to them their reward," that is, they have rendered to the just evil for good, but thou shalt turn that rendering upon their own head; for thou shalt make it hurt them, not the just.

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. BECAUSE THEY HAVE NOT UNDERSTOOD THE WORKS OF THE LORD, AND THE OPERATIONS OF HIS HANDS: THOU SHALT DESTROY THEM, AND SHALT NOT BUILD THEM UP.**

Hence it appeareth that what is contained in the two verses above was a prophecy, not an imprecation; for he saith not: *Destroy them*, but *thou shalt destroy them*; or, as it is in the Hebrew, *he shall destroy them*. By these words the root of all evils is signified: for the root is, to be unwilling to understand the works of the Lord, not to apply the mind to consider and know the wonderful works of God, which he hath wrought in the creation, in the redemption, in the government of mankind: for if any one consider these, it can scarce be but that he be wonderfully kindled to the love of God. Hence, I *Cor.* ii, Blessed Paul saith: "If they had known it, they would never have crucified the Lord of glory;" and the Lord himself, *Luke* xix: "If thou also hadst known, and that in this thy day, the things that are to thy peace;" and below: "They shall not leave in thee a stone upon a stone, because thou hast not known the time of thy visitation." As to the words, there is in the Latin edition some obscurity, which is not in the Hebrew, nor in the Greek; for in these editions it is read: *Quoniam non intellexerunt in opera Domini, et in opera manuum ejus* (Because they have not understood unto the works of the Lord, and unto the works of his hands); or, as St. Jerome rendereth: *Quoniam non intellexerunt opera Domini, et opus manuum ejus* (Because they have not understood the works of the Lord, and the work of his hands): for that preposition, *in*, is joined to both nouns by a Hebrew phrase, but according to the use of the Latin tongue, it might have been taken away from both places. But the Latin Interpreter took it away from the former place, and left it in the latter, and so made the sentence more obscure. Now why the Prophet, when he had said: "They have not understood the works of the Lord," added: "And the operations of his hands," the reason seemeth to be, that he might explain

[Margin: The works which God hath done by himself, not by second causes.]

that he speaketh chiefly of that work which he himself doth by himself, not by second causes, such a work as was the creation of heaven and earth, the incarnation of the Word, the miracles of Christ, the resurrection and ascension of the Lord, and the like. He saith therefore: Because sinners have not understood the works of the Lord, and chiefly those works which he hath wrought with his own hands, that is, by himself immediately; therefore thou, O Lord, "shalt destroy them, and shalt not build them up," that is, thou shalt destroy them, and when thou hast destroyed, it shall not repent thee to have destroyed, therefore thou shalt never more build them up. The Prophet alludeth to the word, the work of the Lord's hands, which work is as it were a certain building, as if he said: Because they have not understood the building of God, God shall destroy them, and shall never more build them up. This fitteth excellently the city, and the temple, and the very kingdom of the Jews, all which God destroyed for the unbelief of the Jews, nor will he ever build them up again. It fitteth also sinners one by one, who understand not that they are the building of the Lord, and made to his image, and redeemed by the price of the Lord's blood, and that so many benefits of nature and of grace have been bestowed upon them; therefore at length they shall be so destroyed, never more to be rebuilt, that nothing remain but the ruin of the building, that is, that whereby they may feel punishment perpetually. In the Hebrew it is in the third person, *he shall destroy, and shall not build up*: perhaps by the fault of copyists the first Hebrew letter, which marketh the person, hath been changed; perhaps the last Greek letter, from which the person is understood, hath been added by the fault of copyists; perhaps the Interpreters looked not so much to the word, as to the sense.

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. BLESSED BE THE LORD, FOR HE HATH HEARD THE VOICE OF MY SUPPLICATION.**

The Prophet passeth to foretelling the glory of the Lord's resurrection, and in the person of Christ giveth thanks to God, saying: "Blessed be the Lord," that is, let the Lord be blessed by all, "for he hath heard the voice of my supplication."

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. THE LORD IS MY HELPER AND MY PROTECTOR: IN HIM HATH MY HEART CONFIDED, AND I HAVE BEEN HELPED.**

He explaineth wherein his prayer was heard, and saith: "The Lord is my helper and my protector," supply, is wont to be. Therefore "in him hath my heart confided," that is, relying on the help and protection of God, I refused not to enter battle with the devil, and with death itself, nor was I disappointed of my hope, since God helped me, so that I came forth plainly a conqueror. In the Hebrew, for *helper and protector*, there is, *strength and shield*. From which we understand that he received from God the help of inward strength, and the protection of outward defence.

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. AND MY FLESH HATH FLOURISHED AGAIN, AND WITH MY WILL I WILL GIVE PRAISE TO HIM.**

He describeth the effect of the help and protection, which was the glorious resurrection, for which he saith he will praise God with his whole heart. "My flesh hath flourished again,"

<!-- p. 160 (PDF 171) -->

that is, my flesh, which had withered by death, hath not only returned to life, but hath returned above all to the flower of age, of health, of gladness, of comeliness. "And" therefore "with my will," that is, from the heart, "I will give praise to him" with the confession of praise and thanksgiving. In the Hebrew it is now read: *Gavisum est cor meum, et in cantico meo confitebor ei* (My heart hath rejoiced, and in my song I will give praise to him). And so for *caro mea* (my flesh), there is read *cor meum* (my heart); for *voluntate mea* (my will), there is read *cantico meo* (my song). For that for *refloruit* (hath flourished again) there is read *gavisum est* (hath rejoiced), as St. Jerome rendereth, mattereth not much: for the word עלז signifieth *to be glad, to exult*, and by a metaphor, *to flourish again*, as the Seventy rightly rendered, because when trees flourish again, they seem in their own manner to be gladdened and to exult. But why those most learned men rendered *caro mea*, where there is now לבי *libbi*; and *voluntate mea*, where there is now משירי, which signify nothing but *my heart* and *my song*, no other reason can be given, but that they did not so read as we now have in our copies, but the words were afterwards transposed and changed: for if for משירי there be read בשרי, which signifieth *my flesh*, and that word, לבי *libbi*, be carried a little further on, the reading will be made which the Seventy had, and which they rightly interpreted; namely this will be the reading, ויעלז בשרי ולבי אהדנו, that is, *and my flesh hath flourished again, and with my heart*, or *with my will, I will give praise to him*.

[Margin: Verse 11.]

**11. THE LORD IS THE STRENGTH OF HIS PEOPLE, AND THE PROTECTOR OF THE SALVATION OF HIS ANOINTED.**[^3]

This is the matter of the confession, of which he said, *I will give praise to him*, namely that *the Lord is the strength of his people*, which he showed, when he so mightily protected the salvation of his Christ, who is the head of the whole people, and on whom the strength and salvation of the whole people dependeth. From the Hebrew St. Jerome rendereth, *Dominus fortitudo mea* (the Lord is my strength); others render, *Dominus fortitudo eis* (the Lord is strength to them), and so sound the words which are now in the Bibles; but the Seventy seem not to have read למו *lamo*, which is *to them*, but לעמו, that is *of his people*. Now he saith, *protector salvationum* (the protector of the salvations), not *protector salvationis* (the protector of the salvation), perhaps because he was protector unto a manifold salvation, namely, that the dead flesh should not be corrupted, that the blood shed should not perish, that not only the soul should be restored to the body, and the blood to the veins, but that there should be added immortality, impassibility, agility, brightness, and whatever other things there are which are required for a glorious body.

[Margin: Verse 12.]

**12. SAVE, O LORD, THY PEOPLE, AND BLESS THY INHERITANCE: AND RULE THEM AND EXALT THEM FOR EVER.**

Christ, who is the head of the Church, being glorified, this remaineth, that his body, that is the people of God, which is the peculiar inheritance of God, which he purchased with his blood, be glorified likewise. Christ therefore saith to the Father, or the Prophet to Christ: *Save thy people*. And that thou mayest save, *bless* by justifying, *rule* by preserving, and protecting in the way, *exalt by glorifying*, which glorification may last for ever.

[^1]: Translator's note: Bellarmine does not number the title, and divides the Psalm into twelve verses. The Douay-Rheims Bible has nine: its verse 1 holds the title and his verse 1; its 2 is his 2; its 3, his 3 and 4; its 4, his 5 and 6; its 5, his 7; its 6, his 8; its 7, his 9 and 10; its 8, his 11; and its 9, his 12.

[^2]: Translator's note: the words are at Ps. xlii, 1. The reference to Psalm xliii is given as printed.

[^3]: Translator's note: the Douay-Rheims has *salvation* for the plural *salvationum*, which Bellarmine explains below, and *his anointed* for *Christi sui* (his Christ).
