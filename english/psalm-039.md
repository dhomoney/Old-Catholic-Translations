BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XXXIX.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*Unto the end, a psalm for David.*

There is nothing new in the title. Some of the later writers expound this Psalm too meanly, referring it to a bodily disease of David; of which disease we read nothing in the Scriptures, but only in the fables of the Rabbins. Blessed Paul, *Heb.* x, expoundeth of Christ some things which are said in this Psalm, which in no way agree to David. There are also certain other things which cannot be fitted to David, as these: "In the head of the book it is written of me;" and these: "But I am a beggar and poor." Rightly therefore St. Jerome, St. Ambrose, St. Augustine and Euthymius expound this whole Psalm of Christ and the Church, that is, of the head and the body, so that Christ always speaketh; but first in the person of his body, then in his own person.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. WITH EXPECTATION I HAVE WAITED FOR THE LORD, AND HE WAS ATTENTIVE TO ME.**[^1]

Christ in the person of his people setteth forth the long expectation of redemption. For it waited above four thousand years, while in the mean time by the Patriarchs and Prophets, partly in figures, partly in oracles, there was promised deliverance from the miseries into which mankind had fallen by the sin of the first man. "With expectation, saith he, I have waited for the Lord," that is, for a long time, without ceasing, I have waited for the Lord to have mercy on, to visit, and to deliver his people; "and he was attentive to me," that is, and at length I was not defrauded of my hope: for he bent his ears to hear me. From the Hebrew ויט אלי it is gathered that that, *intendit mihi* (he was attentive to me), signifieth properly, *inclinavit se ad me* (he inclined himself to me), or *extendit se ad me* (he stretched himself towards me), namely to hear.

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. AND HE HEARD MY PRAYERS, AND BROUGHT ME OUT OF THE PIT OF MISERY AND THE MIRE OF DREGS.**

He explaineth what he said, *he was attentive to me,* saying: *He was attentive to me,* for "he heard my prayers;" and the effect of the hearing was, that "he brought me out of the pit of misery and the mire of dregs." By the pit (*lacus*) of misery is understood a deep hole; for the Hebrew word בור *bor* signifieth *cisternam* (a cistern), and the Hebrew word שאון signifieth *tumultum, confusionem, desolationem* (tumult, confusion, desolation), and the like. There is described therefore a deep place, wanting light, and full of rotten mire, into which many have fallen, from whose moving and wailing there ariseth tumult, and great confusion, which the Seventy Interpreters called in general misery. This is the state of the wicked, who want the light of the knowledge of God, and of his commandments, and are stuck fast in the slime of carnal desires; from which it cometh not only that they cannot walk to eternal felicity, but that they are perpetually in tumult and contention among themselves. From this deep the grace of the Redeemer delivereth us, when we begin by faith to know the true God, and true and everlasting goods, and by hope and charity to be delivered from earthly desires, and to possess the peace of God, and tranquillity of soul. But this is to be observed with St. Augustine, that he who is said to have been heard, that he might be brought out of the pit, had already lifted up his head, and

known his misery, and therefore had begun to cry out of the deep; for God inspireth into his elect, while they yet remain in sin, the spirit of faith, and of fear, and of invocation, without which preventing grace no man would ever cry out of the deep, but would rather contemn God and the admonitions of salvation, according to that of *Prov.* xviii: "The wicked man when he is come into the depth of sins, contemneth."

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. AND HE SET MY FEET UPON A ROCK, AND DIRECTED MY STEPS.**

He who had fallen into the pit of misery had fallen from the way in which God had set him at the beginning of creation, that by it he might come by a straight and easy path to the kingdom: therefore he who afterwards brought him out of the pit of misery, and the mire of dregs, restored him to the way, to a way stable and firm, and no less straight and plain; and this is what he saith: "And he set my feet upon a rock," that is, my feet, which he plucked out of the deep and miry pit, he set upon a high and firm rock; "and the rock was Christ," I *Cor.* x, who saith of himself, *John* xvi:[^2] "I am the way, the truth, and the life;" therefore he set the feet of the justified upon the faith and doctrine, and the examples of Christ, that they may follow his footsteps. "And directed my steps," that is, he not only set my feet in a way paved with stones, but also in a straight way, and so directed my steps into the way of peace.

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. AND HE PUT A NEW CANTICLE INTO MY MOUTH, A SONG TO OUR GOD.**

From this, that God set me in a way most firm and most straight, it came to pass that I began to sing *a new canticle:* for before, as I was an old man, so I sang old canticles of the loves of the world; but being renewed in the spirit of my mind, I began to sing a new canticle of the love of God: which canticle God himself put into my mouth, and the same is a song most pleasing to our God. In the Hebrew for *carmen Deo nostro* (a song to our God), there is, *laudem Deo nostro* (praise to our God). Therefore *a song to our God* ought to be understood, *a song of the praise of our God;* and this is the new canticle of which we have spoken above: for he who singeth the loves of the world, without doubt praiseth the world; and he who singeth the loves of God, without doubt praiseth God.

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. MANY SHALL SEE, AND SHALL FEAR: AND THEY SHALL HOPE IN THE LORD.**

The people of God now delivered from the pit of misery, or the same Christ in the person of the people now delivered, foretelleth that many shall be delivered after their example. "Many, saith he, shall see" the pit of misery and the deliverance of some, "and shall fear," and "they shall hope in the Lord," that is, they shall fear the pit, and shall hope in the deliverer. For this is the beginning of salvation, when God by his grace beginneth to open the inward eyes of sinful men, that they may see the most miserable state of sin, and at the same time know by whom they may be delivered, and thence begin to fear and to hope.

[Margin: Whence is the beginning of salvation?]

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. BLESSED IS THE MAN WHOSE TRUST IS IN THE NAME OF THE LORD; AND WHO HATH NOT HAD REGARD TO VANITIES, AND LYING FOLLIES.**

This is as it were an exhortation, and a praise inviting men to imitate those who are delivered. "Blessed is the man whose trust (*spes*, hope) is in the name of the Lord," that is, he is truly happy, who truly placeth his hope in the Lord, who alone is almighty and merciful, and therefore can and will deliver from all misery them that hope in him. That, *the name of the Lord,* may signify invocation, or the knowledge of familiarity, or power and authority, or simply God himself. He addeth for greater explanation: "And who hath not had regard to vanities, and lying follies (*insanias falsas*)," that is, he hath not looked to the help of any other, and especially hath not looked to the help of things most vain and deceitful, which cannot save, as vain and foolish men look. Some, as Euthymius and certain moderns, refer the vanities to the idols of the gentiles, and the lying follies to the prophecies of the oracles of the gentiles. St. Jerome translateth from the Hebrew, *ad superbias et pompas mendacium* (to the prides and pomps of lies); but the common explanation of Saints Ambrose, Jerome, Augustine, and Chrysostom in their commentary on this place is general, that by *vanities* are understood all vain helps, whether in men, or in other created things; and by *lying follies* are understood the same false, or deceitful helps, which may also be called madnesses (*insaniæ*), that is mad things, which would not be chosen save by madmen. Of this kind are, besides the rest, the predictions of astrologers, or the enchantments of necromancers, or observations and auguries, which many believe and trust, but they are most vain and most foolish.

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. THOU HAST MULTIPLIED THY WONDERFUL WORKS, O LORD MY GOD: AND IN THY THOUGHTS THERE IS NO ONE LIKE TO THEE.**

He passeth now to explain the most high mystery of human redemption, by which very many have been brought, and shall be brought, out of the pit of misery and the mire of dregs, and first he signifieth in general that the works of God are wonderful. "Thou hast multiplied, saith he, thy wonderful works, O Lord," that is, thou hast multiplied thy wonders; "and in thy thoughts there is no one like to thee," that is, and in the thoughts, or in the devices of thy wisdom, there is none who is perfectly like to thee, so that he can be compared to thee. St. Jerome translateth from the Hebrew, *non invenio ordinem coram te* (I find no order before thee), for that which we have: *Non est qui similis sit tibi* (There is no one like to thee), but the Seventy Interpreters have interpreted the Hebrew words more clearly: for the verb ערך signifieth not only *ordinare* (to set in order), but also *æstimare, æquare, comparare* (to value, to make equal, to compare). Therefore we should rightly translate word for word thus: *Non est qui tibi comparari,* or *æquari possit* (There is none who can be compared, or made equal, to thee); which is the same with that which we have in the Greek, οὐχ ἔστιν τις ὁμοιωθήσεται σοι, *non est qui similis sit tibi.*

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. I HAVE DECLARED AND I HAVE SPOKEN: THEY ARE MULTIPLIED ABOVE NUMBER.**

He giveth the reason why no one can be compared with God, as to wonderful works and deep thoughts, and saith: "I have declared and I have spoken," that is, I have made manifest some such works by the Prophets, by wise men, by the very elements of the world: for "the heavens shew forth the glory of God, and the firmament declareth the work of his hands," as is read *Ps.* viii;[^3] but "they are multiplied above number," that is, they are so many, that they cannot be numbered, wherefore neither can all be declared and made manifest. From the Hebrew this also might be the sense: They are greater than can be told; for word for word it is thus, עצמו מספר, *roborata sunt præ enarrari,* or *præ numerari* (they are strengthened beyond being told, or beyond being numbered): for the same verb signifieth *numerare* (to number) and *narrare* (to tell); and perhaps the Prophet of set purpose put an ambiguous verb, that it might be referred to both significations. For truly the works of God, even if we look only at natural things, are strengthened, or have grown, as well in greatness as in multitude, so that they can neither be told, nor numbered: for in single things, even the least, as in one little tree, in one little living creature, in one little flower, there are so many wonders, that not even the greatest philosophers have been able to search out the reason of them. But in this place this is to be observed, that when these words are referred to Christ, it is to be understood that the wonders of God are not untellable, or innumerable in respect of Christ himself, but in respect of us: for he who made those wonderful works knoweth most exactly their greatness and multitude; but mortal men are not capable of so great knowledge. The holy Fathers Ambrose, Jerome and Augustine in their Commentaries, because they read as we now have, "they are multiplied above number" (*multiplicati sunt*), referred this whole verse to the sermons of Christ, and the persuasion of the hearers, so that the sense is: *I have declared and I have spoken* thy wonders, *and they are multiplied,* who believed, *above number,* that is, innumerable men believed, an infinite number being put for a finite by hyperbole, or, many believed above the number of those foreordained to life everlasting, because many reprobate entered with the elect into the floor of the Lord, or into the Lord's net. And the Hebrew copy can without difficulty be fitted to this sense: for where we have *præ numerari* (beyond being numbered), the points being changed, it may become, *præ numero* (beyond number), as is known to them that understand. And indeed this explanation is true, but it answereth not so perfectly to the original text, nor joineth this verse so fitly with the one above, as the former exposition, which is that of Euthymius and others, and is conformable to the version of St. Jerome, nor is it contrary to our Vulgate edition: for that *multiplicati sunt* either is an error of the copyists, as many suspect, and *multiplicati* was written for *multiplicatæ,* that is the thoughts, or *multiplicata,* that is the wonderful works; or at least the Interpreter himself put the masculine gender, saying: *Multiplicati sunt,* because he looked to the Greek noun by which thoughts are signified, which is of the masculine gender, namely διαλογισμοί. We have something like, *Wis.* i, 7: "The spirit of the Lord shall fill the whole world: and that, which containeth all things, hath knowledge of the voice."[^4] Where that *hoc* (that), and that, *quod* (which), are referred to *the spirit of the Lord,* and it ought to have been written: *Et hic qui continet omnia* (And he who containeth all things), according to Latin grammar; but the Interpreter put the neuter gender, because *spirit* in Greek is of the neuter gender, namely πνεῦμα.

[Margin: The works of God are untellable.]

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. SACRIFICE AND OBLATION THOU DIDST NOT DESIRE; BUT THOU HAST PIERCED EARS FOR ME.**[^5]

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. BURNT OFFERING AND SIN OFFERING THOU DIDST NOT REQUIRE: THEN SAID I, BEHOLD I COME.**

Wonderful indeed are all things which God hath made, and in all shine forth his most deep thoughts; but the works of human redemption excel all others as it were infinitely. For what greater thing can be thought, than that God should descend to the form of a servant, and become a beggar and poor, that man may be brought out of the pit of misery, and be lifted up to the highest heaven? That the same God also in the form of a servant should be beaten with rods and crucified among thieves, that he may exalt servants among the angels to the fellowship of an everlasting kingdom? and that all these things should be done most justly and most wisely, without any injury, nay with the highest glory of the Divinity? This admirable mystery therefore Christ himself setteth forth in the following verses by the tongue and pen of David: "Sacrifice, saith he, and oblation thou didst not desire," that is, thou wouldst not be appeased by the sacrifice of cattle, nor by the oblation of loaves, or of frankincense, but by a victim of infinite price: therefore thou wouldst have me take a mortal body, that by obedience unto death I might expiate the disobedience of the first man; and because thou didst not admit the *burnt offering* of cattle, and the *sacrifice* for sin, therefore "said I, Behold I come," that I myself may become priest and sacrifice, by which being appeased towards mankind thou mayest bring men out of the pit of misery and the mire of dregs. Many things are to be noted in this place: *first,* that by sacrifice and oblation is not understood in this place the action of sacrificing, or of offering, but the victim to be slain, as a lamb, or a calf, and the gift to be offered, as bread, or some other thing; for in the Hebrew is זבח *zebach,* and מנחה *minchah;* and St. Paul, *Heb.* x, readeth *hostiam* (victim), for that which we have, *sacrificium* (sacrifice). It is to be noted *secondly,* that what is here said, *sacrifice and oblation thou didst not desire,* is not contrary to so many precepts of the Lord concerning the offering of sacrifices, which are had in Leviticus; for God did indeed of old will sacrifices, oblations and burnt offerings, and victims for sin, but he willed them to signify, not to work, expiation; for, as the Apostle teacheth in the epistle to the *Heb.* chap. x: "It was impossible that with the blood of goats and calves sins should be taken away."[^6] *Thirdly,* it is to be noted that the Latin Vulgate edition, which hath: "*Aures*

*autem perfecisti mihi* (But thou hast perfected ears for me)," agreeth not with the Hebrew text, nor with the Greek: for in the Hebrew there is, *aures autem fodisti mihi* (but thou hast pierced ears for me); but in the Greek, σῶμα δὲ κατηρτίσω μοι, *corpus autem perfecisti mihi* (but a body thou hast perfected for me). But that reading which hath: *Corpus autem perfecisti mihi,* can in no way be rejected, because it is not only had in the Greek text, and received by the Latin and Greek Fathers, as Ambrose and Augustine, Chrysostom and Theodoret, but is also approved by the Apostle, *Heb.* x, and perhaps in the ancient Hebrew text, which the Seventy Interpreters had, there was some word which signified body. The reading also of the Latin edition: *Aures autem perfecisti mihi,* cannot be rejected, both because that reading also is most ancient, as is plain from Chrysostom in his second exposition of this Psalm, and from Jerome in his commentary on this Psalm; and because the Latin Vulgate edition was approved in the council of Trent, and can easily be reconciled with the Greek and Hebrew reading. For the Hebrew verb כרח *carah* signifieth not only *fodere* (to pierce), but also *præparare* (to prepare), or *apponere* (to set before), as is known from book IV of *Kings,* chap. vi, verse 22,[^7] where *the preparing of a table,* and *the setting on of meats,* is signified by this same verb. Now it is the same, *aurem apposuisti mihi* (thou hast set an ear to me), and, *aurem perfecisti mihi* (thou hast perfected an ear for me); nor are these different, *corpus perfecisti mihi,* and, *aures perfecisti mihi,* save that the former speech is proper, the latter is figurative; for he who saith, *aures perfecisti mihi,* signifieth the whole by a part, and not without cause is the whole body signified in this place by the ears, rather than by another member, because the ears are the instruments by which are received the commands of superiors, to whom obedience is due, according to that of *Ps.* xvii: "At the hearing of the ear thou hast perfected for me."[^8] Now Christ took a human body chiefly for this, that he might obey God the Father by obedience in the form of a servant, and by obeying might make satisfaction for the sins of the whole world. "I came, saith he himself, *John* v and vi, not to do my own will, but the will of him that sent me;" and *John* iv: "My meat is to do the will of him that sent me;" and *John* viii: "I do always the things that please him." There remaineth only that reading: *Aures autem fodisti mihi,* which seemeth not to be received, as neither the exposition of those who will have those words: *Aures autem perfecisti mihi,* to signify: *Aures purgasti* (Thou hast cleansed ears), or *aperuisti* (hast opened), so that it is the same as *fodisti,* that is *aperuisti.* For this reading is not had in the commentaries of any ancient Father, that I know; and it seemeth to be contrary to the truth of the mystery; for he who saith: *Aures fodisti,* or *purgasti mihi,* signifieth that he had at some time ears closed, or unclean. But Christ, who speaketh here, always had them open and most clean, so that he could never truly say: *Aures fodisti,* or *aperuisti,* or *purgasti mihi.* It is to be noted *fourthly,* that those words: *Holocaustum et pro peccato* (Burnt offering and for sin), signify not that the burnt offering is the sacrifice for sin: for two kinds of sacrifices are distinguished in that place, namely the burnt offering, and the sacrifice for sin; for in the Hebrew is עולח וחטאח *holah vechataa, holocaustum et peccatum* (burnt offering and sin); but by the name of sin is understood the sacrifice for sin, as also in the Apostle, II *Cor.* v: "Him, who knew no sin, he hath made sin for us." Therefore the sense is, *burnt offering and sacrifice for sin thou didst not require.* Now he maketh mention in these two verses of all the kinds of the ancient sacrifices, to shew that all were figures of the sacrifice of Christ. For, as St. Leo writeth, sermon 8 *On the Passion of the Lord,* the one oblation of the body and blood of Christ fulfilleth all the differences of victims. It is to be noted *fifthly,* that that: *Then said I, Behold I come,* is referred to the time when God decreed that he would not admit the sacrifices of the old Testament for expiation; for then, when God decreed this, the Son of God said: "Behold I come," that by the sacrifice of my body and blood the reconciliation may be fulfilled.

[Margin: A different reading of this place is approved.]

[Margin: Verse 11.]

**11. IN THE HEAD OF THE BOOK IT IS WRITTEN OF ME THAT I SHOULD DO THY WILL: O MY GOD, I HAVE DESIRED IT, AND THY LAW IN THE MIDST OF MY HEART.**

He now setteth forth for what he cometh, when he hath said: "Behold I come," and saith: I come that I may do the will of God, as it is written of me. Now the will of God was, that by the oblation of his body to passion and death he should sanctify us. For so the Apostle expoundeth this place, *Heb.* x, where, after he hath cited this place of the Psalm, he addeth: "In the which will, we are sanctified by the oblation of the body of Jesus Christ once." "In the head of the book it is written of me." St. Ambrose understandeth by *the head of the book* the beginning of the book of Genesis, chap. ii, where it is written: "This now is bone of my bones, and a man shall leave father and mother, and shall cleave to his wife." Which the Apostle expoundeth, *Ephes.* v: "This is a great sacrament; but I speak in Christ and in the church." St. Augustine expoundeth that this is said of the beginning of the book of Psalms, where it is said of Christ: "His will is in the law of the Lord." St. John Chrysostom, Euthymius and Theodoret understand it in general of the books of the Prophets, in which very many things are written of the passion of Christ: nor do they think that stress is to be laid on that word, *in capite* (in the head), because in the Hebrew there is not, *in capite,* but במגלת ספר *bamgillath sepher, in volumine libri* (in the roll of the book). St. Jerome in his Commentary, as also Chrysostom in his second exposition, understandeth by *the head of the book* the beginning of the Gospel according to John, where it is written: "In the beginning was the Word," etc.; and below: "And the Word was made flesh." There are not wanting those who by *the head of the book* understand the beginning of the book of life, of which book of life many things are said in *Ps.* lxviii, *Dan.* xii, *Luke* x, *Philipp.* iv and *Apoc.* xx and xxi; and that which is said in this place according to the Hebrew words, *in the roll of the book,* or, *in the book rolled up,* and closed, squareth with this book. For the book of life, in which are written the names of the predestinate, is closed, because it cannot be read at this time; but yet it is certain that

[Margin: The book of life is closed.]

<!-- p. 261 (PDF 272) -->

in the head of that book Christ is written, and his passion and death. All these are probable: but the simplest and most literal exposition seemeth to be, if by the name of head be understood the sum of the whole book of the divine Scriptures. For in the Hebrew text it is therefore not had in what part of the book this is written, but it is said: *In the roll of the book it is written of me,* as if he said: In the whole roll, because the sum of all Scripture speaketh Christ; for the Lord said, *Luke,* last chap.: "The things must needs be fulfilled, which are written in the law of Moses, and in the prophets, and in the psalms, concerning me;" and in the same place: "He expounded to the two disciples in all the scriptures the things that were concerning him, beginning at Moses and all the prophets." And *John* v, the same Lord said in general: "Search the scriptures, for the same are they that give testimony of me." And that which is had in the Greek text, ἐν κεφαλίδι βιβλίου; and in the Latin, *in capite libri,* may fitly be expounded, *in summa libri* (in the sum of the book): for both in Greek and in Latin not only the beginning of a book is called the head, but also that to which all things are referred. For to recapitulate also is to bring into a sum, and the heads of books are wont to be distinguished according to those things to which the principal parts of the books are brought back. And in this manner is taken what the Apostle saith, *Heb.* viii: "Of the things which we have spoken, this is the sum (*capitulum*): We have such an high priest," that is, the sum of those things which are spoken is, that we have such a high priest, etc. Because therefore the Lord saith not in this place, *in capite primi libri* (in the head of the first book), or *in capite primo libri* (in the first head of the book), but in general, *in capite libri,* Chrysostom, Theodoret, and Euthymius have rightly said that in this place *in the head of the book* signifieth the whole volume of the divine Scriptures: for that cometh to the same as what we say, that the sum of all Scripture pertaineth to Christ; for he is the end of the law, from the Apostle, *Rom.* x. The Lord therefore saith: "In the head of the book it is written of me that I should do thy will," that is, all the Scriptures testify that I was to come into the world for this, "that I should do thy will," by obeying thee most exactly in all things. Then turning to the Father he addeth: "O my God, I have desired it," that is, I have most gladly accepted thy decree, "and thy law in the midst of my heart," supply, I have set, that is, I have desired nothing with greater affection, than to fulfil thy law. Of the just it is said in *Ps.* xxxvi: "His law is in his heart;"[^9] but of the head of the just, "thy law in the midst of my heart" I have set. And they who are Christ's ought to have this spirit of Christ, to prefer the law of the Lord before all things, and to have it always in the midst of the memory, in the midst of the understanding, and in the midst of the will. It mattereth not much that in the Hebrew and Greek there is, *in medio ventris mei* (in the midst of my belly): for it is treated of the belly of the soul, not of the body; and in the soul heart and belly are altogether the same.

[Margin: Verse 12.]

**12. I HAVE DECLARED THY JUSTICE IN A GREAT CHURCH, LO, I WILL NOT RESTRAIN MY LIPS: O LORD, THOU KNOWEST IT.**

Although the chief end of the divine will was, that Christ should die for mankind; yet God the Father willed that Christ should first preach continually, and that the preaching of the truth should be as it were the way to the passion, and that he should be not only the redeemer, but also the doctor and master of mankind; therefore he now saith: "I have declared thy justice," *Matt.* iii and xvii, that is, I have preached thy most just law, and also the works of true justice, which thou requirest of men; and this I have preached openly and publicly, innumerable multitudes of peoples hearing; and thou, O Lord, canst be witness that I have done this, and will do it henceforth. And truly Christ preached all his life: from infancy he began to preach by his example the contempt of visible things, modesty, temperance, humility. Then from his Baptism, in which it was said by the Father: "Hear ye him,"[^10] he began to preach also by word, nor did he cease even unto death to preach by himself, and afterwards he continued the preaching by the Apostles, and will continue it even to the consummation of the world by the successors of the Apostles.

[Margin: Christ not only the redeemer, but also the doctor and master of mankind.]

[Margin: Verse 13.]

**13. I HAVE NOT HID THY JUSTICE WITHIN MY HEART: I HAVE DECLARED THY TRUTH AND THY SALVATION.**

Many preach, while the hope of gain is present, or the fear of loss doth not affright, but when hope ceaseth, when fear presseth, they hide in their heart the preaching of the truth, nor suffer it to break forth abroad. From these vices Christ was very far removed, and teacheth us by his example to do the same; therefore he saith: "I have declared thy justice," and neither through negligence, nor through any fear have I ever hid it in my heart. Now what he hath said of the justice which God exacteth of us, namely that he declared it, and hid it not, the same he now subjoineth of the justice and mercy which is in God himself: for he calleth justice truth, that is faithfulness, by which he faithfully rendereth to every one according to his works; and he calleth mercy *salutare,* that is, the salvation (*salutem*) which he mercifully giveth to them that hope in him. He saith therefore: "I have declared thy truth and thy salvation," that is, I have declared also the truth which is in thee, making manifest to all how faithfully thou renderest unspeakable rewards to the just, and dreadful punishments to the unjust; and at the same time I have declared thy salvation, that is, how mercifully thou savest all who set their hope in thee.

[Margin: Verse 14.]

**14. I HAVE NOT CONCEALED THY MERCY AND THY TRUTH FROM A GREAT COUNCIL.**

That which a little before he had called *salvation,* he now more expressly calleth *mercy,* and joineth it with truth, that is with justice. He saith therefore: "I have not concealed," for any fear, "from a great council," that is, from the multitude of peoples, "the

<!-- p. 262 (PDF 273) -->

mercy and justice," which are in thee; but I have preached them publicly and freely. That these things are most true we manifestly gather from the Gospel: for *Matt.* chap. v, vi and vii, the Lord preached the justice which we ought to do; and in divers parables, *Matt.* xiii, xiv and xxv, he preached the justice and mercy of God.

[Margin: Verse 15.]

**15. WITHHOLD NOT THOU, O LORD, THY TENDER MERCIES FROM ME: THY MERCY AND THY TRUTH HAVE ALWAYS UPHELD ME.**

He passeth now from the preaching to the passion: and as he himself made manifest to men the justice and mercy of the Father, so now he prayeth the Father not to defer his mercy and truth in him, by delivering him through a speedy resurrection from all suffering and death. "Withhold not thou, O Lord, saith he, thy tender mercies from me (*ne longe facias*, put not far)," that is, thou seest, O Lord and Father, into how bitter a passion I have fallen, because I was not silent in making manifest to men thy justice and mercy: therefore do thou also not put thy tender mercies far from me, by raising me up speedily, as hitherto "thy mercy and thy faithfulness have always upheld me."

[Margin: Verse 16.]

**16. FOR EVILS WITHOUT NUMBER HAVE SURROUNDED ME; MY INIQUITIES HAVE OVERTAKEN ME, AND I WAS NOT ABLE TO SEE.**

[Margin: Verse 17.]

**17. THEY ARE MULTIPLIED ABOVE THE HAIRS OF MY HEAD: AND MY HEART HATH FORSAKEN ME.**

He giveth the reason why he said: "Withhold not thy tender mercies from me;" and saith: "For evils without number have surrounded me." For truly the sufferings of Christ were very many, and all together, as it were in one troop, rushed upon him. And why his sufferings were so many, the cause was, because our sins, which he took upon himself, are innumerable. "My iniquities, saith he, have overtaken me," that is, the iniquities of all men, which the Father "hath laid on me," as is said *Isa.* liii, and which therefore he made mine, that I might bear them in my body upon the tree, they all, I say, have overtaken me. "And I was not able to see," that is, so many are the iniquities which have overtaken me, that I have not prevailed to see how many they are. For "they are multiplied above the hairs of my head," that is, they surpass by their multitude the number of the hairs: for the hairs of the head are so many, that they cannot be numbered; and therefore, as it were overwhelmed by so great a multitude of evils, I fainted, "and my heart hath forsaken me," that is, vigour itself, and life itself hath forsaken me. Moreover what Christ here saith, "I was not able to see," is not to be taken properly, as though in truth the Lord could not see the number of sins, by reason of their exceeding multitude; for without doubt he knoweth most exactly the number of sins past, present and to come; but he speaketh after the manner of men, and meant only to signify that the sins which he took upon himself to expiate are innumerable. The like is that which is said, *Mark* chap. vi: "And he could not do any miracles there, and he wondered because of their unbelief;" for he could, if he had willed, have done miracles in that place; but he is said not to have been able, that the unbelief of that people might be signified, by reason of which he would not do miracles in that place. As also he did not properly wonder, because wonder is born of ignorance, which had no place in Christ; but he was said to wonder, because he gave the sign of one wondering, to shew that it was a wonder that that people was held by so great unbelief. St. Augustine will have this verse and the two above to be taken in the person of the weak members of Christ, that is of the imperfect faithful: we do not reject his opinion, but we have wished to shew how these three verses are to be expounded, if they be taken as written in the person of Christ, as the foregoing verses were truly written in the person of Christ, which the Apostle alleged to the Hebrews, chap. x, with which these seem to be continued.

**18. BE PLEASED, O LORD, TO DELIVER ME, LOOK DOWN, O LORD, TO HELP ME.**

He returneth to the prayer which he had begun in the fifteenth verse, and asketh deliverance from so great evils by a speedy resurrection, as we have said above: "Be pleased, saith he, O Lord," that is, let it please thee, Father, whom I acknowledge also as Lord by reason of the form of a servant which I have taken; let it please thee, I say, "to deliver me" from so many sorrows which have surrounded me. "Look down, O Lord, to help me," that is, thou seemest indeed for a time to have forsaken me, and to have turned thy face from me, while thou sufferest me to endure the torments of the cross without any consolation, but now "look down to help me," that shortly thou mayest fill me with the gladness of a glorious resurrection.

[Margin: Verse 19.]

**19. LET THEM BE CONFOUNDED AND ASHAMED TOGETHER, THAT SEEK AFTER MY SOUL TO TAKE IT AWAY.**

From the resurrection of Christ there followed the greatest confusion of his adversaries, who thought him wholly extinguished by them. This he now foretelleth by way of imprecation: which form of speaking is frequent with the Prophets: "Let them be confounded, saith he, and ashamed," that is, being confounded let them blush, "that seek after my soul to take it away," that is, who seek to take away my life, by slaying me, and utterly extinguishing me: for this was the desire of the Jews, and this they believed they had attained, when they saw Christ fastened to the cross; but a little after, when they heard that he was risen, and saw his resurrection confirmed by the greatest signs and prodigies, and believed by the peoples, they were filled with confusion and blushing; but they shall be filled with much greater confusion and shame in the last day, when they shall see him to be their most just

[Margin: The resurrection of Christ brought the greatest confusion to the Jews.]

<!-- p. 263 (PDF 274) -->

Judge, whom in their council, false witnesses being brought in, they presumed to judge.

[Margin: Verse 20.]

**20. LET THEM BE TURNED BACKWARD AND BE ASHAMED THAT DESIRE EVILS TO ME.**

He repeateth the same, to explain or confirm the thing more. "Let them be turned backward, saith he," that is, being confounded let them withdraw; "and be ashamed," that is, let them feel shame and blush, "that desire evils to me," that is, not those only who seek to kill me, but also all others who desire my confusion and ignominy. St. Augustine expoundeth this verse in a good sense, as though the Lord prayed for his adversaries' conversion from sins to God, that they may now no longer proudly go before, but humbly follow after their Lord. A pious exposition; but the former seemeth more literal, and is more easily continued with the verse above.

[Margin: Verse 21.]

**21. LET THEM IMMEDIATELY BEAR THEIR CONFUSION, THAT SAY TO ME: 'TIS WELL, 'TIS WELL.**

He repeateth the very same a third time, saying: "Let them immediately bear their confusion," that is, let not their confusion be deferred for a long time; but straightway, namely after the most short space of three days, let them experience their confusion, "that say to me: 'Tis well, 'tis well (*Euge, euge*)," that is, who congratulate one another concerning me as conquered, saying: "'Tis well, 'tis well." St. Jerome translateth from the Hebrew, *qui dicunt mihi: Vah, vah* (that say to me: Vah, vah); which word we read in the Gospel to have been that of the Jews deriding Christ hanging on the cross. But the Seventy translated, *euge, euge,* *Matt.* xvii,[^11] and there is no doubt but they also rightly understood the force of the Hebrew word, האח האח. Wherefore that pronoun *mihi* (to me) is not so to be taken, as though the adversaries wished to flatter Christ, or to praise him, or to encourage him, as we are wont when we say to any one: "'Tis well, 'tis well;" but it is to be understood that the adversaries, exulting among themselves over the passion of Christ, said: "'Tis well, 'tis well," so that that *mihi* is taken for *de me* (of me), or *super me* (over me). We read the same a little more clearly in Psalm XXXIV: "Let them not say in their hearts: It is well, it is well, to our mind: neither let them say: We have swallowed him up;" where it appeareth that that *euge* was said in the hearts of those who thought that they had already swallowed up Christ. And that which we have from the Greek, *ferant* (let them bear), St. Jerome translateth from the Hebrew *pereant* (let them perish). But it is credible that the Seventy read not ישמר, from the verb ישם, which is *desolare* (to make desolate), but ישאר *issehu,* a single letter being changed, from the verb נשא, which is *ferre* (to bear). Lastly, that which we have, *confestim* (immediately), St. Jerome translateth from the Hebrew *post* (after). But the Hebrew words, על עקב, signify properly *ad calcaneum* (at the heel), or *ad vestigium* (at the footstep), that is immediately, or forthwith, which is the same; and the same St. Jerome himself translateth this same word in *Ps.* lxix, *ad vestigium.* Rightly therefore the Seventy Interpreters rendered the Hebrew words, παραχρῆμα, and the Latin Interpreter faithfully the Greek words.

[Margin: Verse 22.]

**22. LET ALL THAT SEEK THEE REJOICE AND BE GLAD IN THEE: AND LET SUCH AS LOVE THY SALVATION SAY ALWAYS: THE LORD BE MAGNIFIED.**

As he foretold by way of imprecation confusion to his persecutors: so now by way of a good prayer he foretelleth gladness to his followers. "Let them rejoice, saith he, and be glad in thee," that is, they shall rejoice and be glad in God, namely with a divine and unspeakable joy, "all that seek thee," that is, all who seek the glory of God, and who love God, and place their hope in him. "And let them say always: The Lord be magnified," that is, and let them not ascribe to themselves, nor to their own strength or virtues, the good which they have; but let them say: "God be magnified by all that love thy salvation," that is, who love the Saviour whom thou hast sent to men, namely Christ Jesus, or, who love the salvation which is from thee, that is, who desire the true and eternal salvation, which thou alone canst give.

[Margin: Verse 23.]

**23. BUT I AM A BEGGAR AND POOR: THE LORD IS CAREFUL FOR ME.**

He returneth to his own state, in which he was at the time of the passion, and saith: "I am a beggar and poor," that is, in want and destitute of human help. For there are in the Hebrew two words, אביון and עני; the former signifieth *egenum, inopem, mendicum* (in want, needy, a beggar): the latter signifieth *afflictum, humilem, destitutum* (afflicted, humble, destitute): all which agreed to Christ in all the time of his mortality, but chiefly when he hung naked on the cross. But although he was in want and poor as to temporal goods, yet he was most rich in his Father's protection; therefore he subjoineth: "The Lord is careful for me," or, as it is in the Hebrew, *Dominus cogitat pro me* (the Lord thinketh for me). Now he calleth the Father Lord, because he speaketh in the form of a servant, that is, as the son of man, according to which nature he hung on the cross.

[Margin: Verse 24.]

**24. THOU ART MY HELPER AND MY PROTECTOR: O MY GOD, BE NOT SLACK.**

What he had said in a single word: "The Lord is careful," that he now setteth forth in two words, saying: "Thou art my helper and my protector." For God the Father was careful for his Son, by helping and protecting him, helping in the overcoming of present evils, and protecting in the removing of future ones. He concludeth lastly: "O my God, be not slack," namely deliver me from all evils by a speedy resurrection.
