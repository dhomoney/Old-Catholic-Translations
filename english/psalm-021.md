BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XXI.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*Unto the end, for the morning protection, a Psalm for David.*[^1]

This Psalm speaketh so openly of the passion and resurrection of the Lord, that it is plainly rash to wish to wrest it to another sense. The title regardeth the resurrection, which is signified in that phrase, *susceptio matutina* (the morning protection, or taking up). For the resurrection was wrought in the morning time, and by the power of God taking up Christ in a manner from the sepulchre, and bringing him forth to the air of life, according to that of the third Psalm, *et exsurrexi, quoniam Dominus suscepit me* (and I have risen up, because the Lord hath protected me). The Hebrew word אילם *aielem* signifieth a hart, or, as others will have it, *a star*. But the Seventy Interpreters have rightly rendered *susceptionem* (taking up), because with them the word איל *aial* signifieth *to take up*. Which is plain from Psalm cvi, verse 17, where for the word אורים *avilim* they read אילם *aialem*, and rendered, *et suscepit eos* (and he took them up). Nor are the readings from the Hebrew far from the same sense, *pro cerva matutina* (for the morning hind), or *pro stella matutina* (for the morning star). For the sense is: for Christ rising again. For he, rising again, is rightly compared to a star, because he rose full of glory, girded with light as with a garment, and he himself saith of himself, *Apoc.*, last chap.: *I am the bright and morning star*. He is also like to a hart, or a hind, first because venomous creatures do no hurt to the hart, nay rather he with his breath draweth serpents out of their holes, and killeth them, as St. Jerome on this place, and Basil on *Ps.* xxviii, say: and Christ *with the spirit of his mouth shall kill the wicked one*, and all the persecutions of the wicked shall be able to hurt him nothing. Then also, because the hart leapeth most swiftly upon the highest mountains, whence in the Canticles Christ is often compared to a young hart leaping upon the mountains. For he on the day of the resurrection with a most swift leap ascended from hell, and from death to immortal and glorious life. Lastly, because the hart is said by a certain natural art to defend himself from old age, and to call back from the grave, with a newness of life restored, a life declining to its failing, as Gilbert writeth, sermon 14 *on the Canticles*: and Christ on the day of the resurrection, by the art of his omnipotence and infinite wisdom, truly called back his body, already dead, from destruction with a newness of life restored. And a hind is named rather than a hart, because the resurrection properly pertaineth to the flesh. For as Christ suffered in the flesh, so also in the flesh he rose again.

[Margin: The title is applied to Christ.]

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. O GOD MY GOD, LOOK UPON ME: WHY HAST THOU FORSAKEN ME? FAR FROM MY SALVATION ARE THE WORDS OF MY SINS.**

David speaketh in the person of Christ hanging on the cross, and set in the utmost pain of the passion, as is plain from the Gospel, *Matt.* xxvii, in which we read that Christ, being very near to death, said: "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?" which are the first words of this Psalm. For that phrase, *look upon*

<!-- p. 121 (PDF 132) -->

*me*, is not in the Hebrew, but was added by the Seventy Interpreters for the sake of explanation. Now that Christ complaineth that he is forsaken by God doth not signify that he was forsaken by the person of the Word, as if the hypostatic union were dissolved: or left destitute of the grace and good will of the Father; but it signifieth that it was permitted by God, that he should suffer in his human nature those horrible torments, and a death full of ignominy; from which, if God had willed, he could most easily have delivered him. Nor did that complaint arise from impatience, or from ignorance, as if Christ knew not the cause of his passion, or did not willingly bear to be forsaken by the Father in those pains; but that complaint was a certain protestation of his most bitter passion. For because in the whole course of the passion the Lord had borne all things with so great evenness of mind, that not even a groan was heard; men might have believed that he, made impassible by some higher power, had not felt the pains. Therefore, then, being nigh to death, he protesteth that he is true man, and truly passible, and that, forsaken by the Father in his pains, he hath drunk the bitterness of the passion with an inward and most great feeling of pain. He saith therefore: "O God my God." Christ in the form of a servant calleth the Father his God, because in that hour he properly worshipped him as true God, sacrificing to him the sacrifice of his body, a sacrifice the most excellent of all that had ever been made. "Look upon me;" he asketh that he would see where he is placed for his honour, would acknowledge the obedience of his Son, would accept the sacrifice offered for mankind. "Why hast thou forsaken me?" seemeth to be not only a protestation of true passion, as we have said, but also a certain wonder, as if he said: Can it be, that thou hast permitted thy most beloved and only begotten Son to come into this abyss of pain? Like are those words: "God so loved the world, as to give his only begotten Son," *John* iii; and those of *Rom.* viii: "He spared not even his own Son, but delivered him up for us all." "Far from my salvation are the words of my sins." Many, fearing lest they brand Christ with the mark of sin, expound it in a forced manner. Some read it with a question: *Longe a salute mea verba delictorum meorum?* (Are the words of my sins far from my salvation?) But such a question is feigned without ground, since it is found in no text. Others expound: *My sins*, since there are none, *are far from my salvation*, that is, they do not hinder my salvation. But the Prophet would more easily have said: *Far from my death are the words of my sins*, if sins had not been the cause of the death of Christ. Others join a part of this sentence with what goeth before, thus: *My God, why hast thou forsaken me far from my salvation?* But all the texts have another pointing. And then what shall that signify: *the words of my sins?* Others lastly consider that the Hebrew word שאגתי signifieth *roaring*, not *sin*, and interpret: *Longe a salute mea verba rugitus mei* (Far from my salvation are the words of my roaring), that is, my roaring, or my cry, wherewith I implore help, is far from my salvation, that is, it cannot

[Margin: Christ never forsaken by God.]

obtain salvation for me. But the Seventy Interpreters without doubt did not read שאגתי, but שגאתי, a single letter being transposed, and rightly rendered *delicta* (sins), not *rugitum* (roaring). The plain and true meaning therefore is this: "Far from my salvation are the words of my sins," that is, with good reason have I said that I am forsaken in pains, because the sins of the whole world, which I have taken upon me, cannot be joined with my salvation. For it is necessary that I suffer and die, if I will purge them. Now that Christ can attribute our sins to himself, as if they were his own, the Scriptures everywhere teach, *John* i: "Behold the Lamb of God, behold him who taketh away the sins of the world;"[^2] I *Pet.* ii: "He himself bore our sins in his body upon the tree;" *Isa.* liii: "God hath laid on him the iniquities of us all;"[^3] II *Cor.* v: "Him, who knew no sin, he hath made sin for us," that is, a victim for sin. As therefore a victim for sin cannot but be sacrificed, if sins are to be purged: so Christ, made a victim for the sins of the whole world, rightly saith: "Far from my salvation are the words of my sins," that is, I cannot escape death, since the sins of the whole world are laid upon me, that I may pay the penalty for them. Moreover, *the words of sins* is a Hebrew phrase. For the Hebrews use the name of *word* to signify *a thing*, or *a matter* in general. And so the sense is: "The words of my sins," that is, this very thing, that I have taken upon me so many sins, for which I am to satisfy, "is far from my salvation," that is, cannot be joined with my salvation, and thereby is the cause that I escape not death.

[Margin: Christ a victim for the sins of the whole world.]

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. O MY GOD, I SHALL CRY BY DAY, AND THOU WILT NOT HEAR: AND BY NIGHT, AND IT SHALL NOT BE REPUTED AS FOLLY IN ME.**

Again by another reason he showeth himself forsaken, without hope of temporal salvation: "I shall cry," saith he, "by day, and thou wilt not hear; and by night," supply, *I shall cry*, "and thou wilt not hear." And the sense is: Although I cry day and night, thou wilt not hear me, that I may be delivered from bodily death. He seemeth to allude to the two prayers which he made, one by night in the garden, the other by day on the cross; but he keepeth not the order, because the sense is that which I have said, although I cry day and night, thou wilt not hear. That which followeth, *and it shall not be reputed as folly in me* (*et non ad insipientiam mihi*), is in the Hebrew, *et non silentium mihi* (and there is no silence for me); but the Seventy seem never to have understood by דומיה *dumiah* *silence*: for in *Ps.* lxi, verse 1, they render *subjecta* (subject); in *Ps.* lxiv, verse 1, they render *decet* (becometh); in this place, *insipientiam* (folly). Therefore either they read something else, or they knew that this word admitteth all those meanings. Now the sense seemeth to be this: "I shall cry by day and by night, and thou wilt not hear;" and yet this cannot be ascribed "to me as folly," because, although I know that I am not to be heard so as to escape temporal death, yet I cry prudently, because I know that I am to be heard as to my principal intention, which is, that mankind be redeemed, and that I be not

<!-- p. 122 (PDF 133) -->

held in death, but rise again to immortal life. Another exposition occurreth to me, in which by the day is understood the time of life, by the night the time of death, and this is the sense: "I shall cry by day, and thou wilt not hear," that is, I shall cry while I live, that I may be delivered from temporal death, and this cry thou wilt not hear; "and by night I shall cry, and it shall not be reputed as folly in me," that is, I shall cry afterwards, when I shall be dead, that I may rise again, and I shall not be foolish, because thou wilt hear this cry of the night; into which sense the Hebrew reading also falleth, *et noctu, et non silentium mihi* (and by night, and there is no silence for me), that is, I shall cry by night, and then thou wilt not be silent as thou wast silent at the cry of the day; but thou wilt answer me, and thereby wilt hear me. And because that phrase, *non exaudies* (thou wilt not hear), can also be rendered *non respondebis* (thou wilt not answer): for the word ענה *hana* signifieth both, the verse could most clearly have been rendered in this manner: *I shall cry by day, and thou wilt not answer me, I shall cry by night, and then thou wilt answer me*; or, according to the Seventy: *I shall cry by day, and thou wilt not hear; I shall cry by night, and I shall not be foolish*, because then thou wilt hear.

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. BUT THOU DWELLEST IN THE HOLY PLACE, THE PRAISE OF ISRAEL.**

He proveth that he will not cry foolishly by night, although he hath not been heard by day, and that he is altogether to be heard, and that by four reasons. *First*, because God is holy and merciful; *secondly*, because he is wont graciously to hear those that call upon him; *thirdly*, because he himself is in the greatest distress; *fourthly*, because he hath trusted in God from his very birth, and hath cleaved to him alone. The first reason is in the present verse. "Thou," saith he, O Lord, wilt altogether hear me, because "thou dwellest in the holy place," that is, in holiness, thou art wholly holy and gracious, and malice or cruelty cannot come nigh to thee; and therefore thou art "the praise" of thy people "Israel," both because thy people Israel praiseth thee, and because on account of thee thy people Israel is praised. For this is the greatest praise of thy people, that it hath a God in every respect most holy. In the Hebrew it is thus, *tu autem sancte, inhabitas laudes Israel* (but thou, O holy one, inhabitest the praises of Israel), that is, thou art holy, and therefore thou art continually in the mouth of thy people praising thee. But it is credible that the Seventy Interpreters did not read קדוש *holy*, but קודש *holiness*, one letter being transposed; and then it ought to be rendered, *tu autem sanctitatis habitator* (but thou, the inhabitant of holiness), which is the same as that which we have: *Tu autem in sancto habitas* (But thou dwellest in the holy place). "The praises of Israel" is in the plural number in the Hebrew, תהלות ישראל *theilloth Israel*; but because it is joined with God, that the meaning might be clearer, the Seventy chose rather to render *laus Israel* (the praise of Israel) than *laudes Israel* (the praises of Israel).

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. IN THEE HAVE OUR FATHERS HOPED: THEY HAVE HOPED, AND THOU HAST DELIVERED THEM.**

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. THEY CRIED TO THEE, AND THEY WERE SAVED: THEY TRUSTED IN THEE, AND WERE NOT CONFOUNDED.**

This is the second reason, from examples; and the meaning is clear. Now there are many examples of this kind in the book of *Judges*. For as often as the children of Israel cried to the Lord, the Lord sent them[^4] a saviour, one of the Judges, as Gedeon, Samson, Samuel, etc.

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. BUT I AM A WORM, AND NO MAN: THE REPROACH OF MEN, AND THE OUTCAST OF THE PEOPLE.**

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. ALL THEY THAT SAW ME HAVE LAUGHED ME TO SCORN: THEY HAVE SPOKEN WITH THE LIPS, AND WAGGED THE HEAD.**

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. HE HOPED IN THE LORD, LET HIM DELIVER HIM: LET HIM SAVE HIM, SEEING HE DELIGHTETH IN HIM.**

This is the third reason, drawn from the distress of Christ crucified. "But I," saith he, "am a worm, and no man," that is, I am in such a state at this time, that I seem made less not only than the angels, but than men; namely "the most abject of men," as Isaias said, chap. liii, nay made beneath all men, since I am set after both Barabbas and the thieves. And so I am made so vile, that I seem to be a worm rather than a man. "The reproach of men," namely of whom men are ashamed, as of a man of dishonour, which was fulfilled when Peter himself swore that he knew not that man, *Matt.* xxvi. "And the outcast of the people," that is, whom even the common people, the vilest part of the nation, so cast off, that it said, "Not this man, but Barabbas." "All they that saw me have laughed me to scorn," that is, they who saw me in that state all mocked me, namely all kinds of men, noble, base, priests, laymen, Hebrews, Gentiles, which was fulfilled when, as St. Luke writeth, chap. xxiii, 35: "The people stood beholding, and the rulers with them derided him;" and below: "And the soldiers also mocked him." "They have spoken with the lips, and wagged the head." That this also was fulfilled St. Matthew teacheth, chap. xxvii: "They blasphemed him, wagging their heads, and saying: Vah, thou that destroyest the temple of God!" "He hoped in the Lord, let him deliver him: let him save him, seeing he delighteth in him." That these same were the words of the Jews St. Matthew witnesseth in the same place: "He trusted," said they, "in God; let him now deliver him if he will." Nor doth it much matter that the Prophet saith, "seeing he delighteth in him" (*quoniam vult eum*), and the Evangelist relateth that they said, "if he will" (*si vult*). For when the Prophet saith, "let him save him, seeing he delighteth in him," there is understood, as he himself saith, which is the same as if he had said: "Let him deliver him if he delighteth in him," that is, if he loveth him, as he himself saith, let him deliver him; or, seeing that he delighteth in him, as he himself saith, let him deliver him. Wonderful altogether is the enlightening of the Prophet, who foreknew and foretold not only the things done, but also the words which the Jews were to say in the passion of Christ. For *speravit in Domino* (he hoped in the Lord), there is now in the Hebrew גל *gol*, that is, *volve ad Dominum* (roll upon the Lord). But the Rabbins have placed the points ill. For it ought to be read גל *gal*, that is, *volvit* (he rolled), as St. Jerome readeth, who rendereth, *confugit ad Dominum* (he fled for refuge to the Lord), which is the same as that which the Seventy rendered: *Speravit in Domino*. For it is the same,

<!-- p. 123 (PDF 134) -->

*he rolled, or rolled himself back upon the Lord, he fled for refuge to the Lord*, and *he hoped in the Lord*.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. FOR THOU ART HE THAT HAST DRAWN ME OUT OF THE WOMB: MY HOPE FROM THE BREASTS OF MY MOTHER.**

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. I WAS CAST UPON THEE FROM THE WOMB. FROM MY MOTHER'S WOMB THOU ART MY GOD, DEPART NOT FROM ME.**

This is the last reason, drawn from the perpetual innocence of Christ himself. That word, *quoniam* (for), is not everywhere a mark of cause, but is often put in the place of the conjunction *et* (and), or *autem* (but). Sometimes also it signifieth nothing, but is added for ornament. "Thou art he that hast drawn me out of the womb," that is, from my birth I am thine, especially since in truth I was born not in the natural manner, as other men, but by thy singular power, the bars of virginity not being opened, as also I was conceived in the womb of the Virgin without the seed of man. St. Jerome rendereth from the Hebrew: *Tu autem propugnator meus ex utero* (But thou art my defender from the womb). But the word גחי *gochi*, from the root גוח *guach*, properly signifieth nothing else but *eductor meus ex utero* (he that brought me forth from the womb). What St. Jerome followed, I confess that I know not. "My hope from the breasts of my mother," that is, as thou didst bring me forth from the womb, so thou art he who chiefly nourished me: the breasts indeed were his mother's, but I understand milk supplied to the mother from heaven, and therefore I learned to trust and hope in thee from the breasts of my mother. "I was cast upon thee from the womb," that is, coming forth from the womb, I fell into thy bosom, who by a singular providence hast care of me. "From my mother's womb thou art my God," that is, as from the beginning of my life thou didst stand by me with a singular providence, so I also from the outset of my life began to worship thee as my God. "Depart not from me:" these words are among the Hebrews the beginning of the following verse; but they are rightly placed also in the Greek and Latin text at the end of the verse above. For they are as it were the conclusion of the foregoing reason, as if Christ said: Seeing that I was cast upon thee from the womb, and from that time thou art my God, therefore by right I require of thee that thou depart not from me, especially since now my most grievous and last tribulation is at hand, that is, the death of my body.

[Margin: Christ truly born of a Virgin.]

[Margin: Verse 11.]

**11. FOR TRIBULATION IS VERY NEAR: FOR THERE IS NONE TO HELP ME.**

This verse might perhaps be expounded of the approaching passion, and of the sorrow and fear which the Lord willed to suffer in the garden. But surely it is much more probable that this whole place is to be understood of approaching death, first because the Lord presently addeth in the past tense: "They have dug my hands," and "they parted my garments amongst them;" then also because a little before he expressed the words of the Jews, which they cast at him when already fastened to the cross; lastly because the Lord, hanging on the cross and very near to death, recited the first verse of the Psalm. Therefore, according to that which he had said, "it shall not be reputed as folly in me," namely, I shall cry to thee, he now prayeth that God be with him in the last tribulation, not that he may be delivered from death, but that he may not be held in death. I ask, saith he, that thou depart not from me, "for tribulation," namely the last and most grievous, "is very near, and for there is none" other who can help me, that I may so die, as to slay death.

[Margin: Verse 12.]

**12. MANY CALVES HAVE SURROUNDED ME: FAT BULLS HAVE BESIEGED ME.**

He relateth the cruelty of his enemies, whom he compareth to bulls, lions and dogs. By the enemies he understandeth the chief priests and Pharisees, who as bulls insulted him, as if they struck at him with their horns, saying: "Vah, thou that destroyest the temple of God," etc., *Matt.* chap. xxvii; and who as lions with open mouth showed their hunger, that is, their vast desire of destroying him, whence also they roared, saying: "Away with him, away with him, crucify him," *John* xix; and who as dogs bit him, namely detracting from him, when they said: "We have found this man perverting our nation," *Luke* xxiii. And: "If he were not a malefactor, we would not have delivered him up to thee."[^5] From which detractions and false calumnies it came at last to pass that the Lord was crucified; therefore he presently subjoineth: "They have dug my hands," etc. But let us see each thing: "Many calves have surrounded me;" by calves he doth not understand in this place any calves whatever, but calves full grown and horned, which may also be called bulls. For that which followeth: "Fat bulls have besieged me," is an explanation, or repetition; especially since in the Hebrew there is to the word, *fortes Basan obsederunt me* (the strong ones of Basan have besieged me). He calleth the calves *the strong ones of Basan*, because they were mighty and fat; for Basan was a region abounding in the best pastures, whence came forth the fattest bulls and rams. Now the chief priests and Pharisees are called strong and fat, because they were powerful and rich. Some distinguish the calves from the bulls, and by the calves understand the people, undisciplined and unwarlike; by the bulls, the chief priests and Pharisees. The exposition is not improbable, but I think that which I have said more probable.

[Margin: Verse 13.]

**13. THEY HAVE OPENED THEIR MOUTHS AGAINST ME, AS A LION RAVENING AND ROARING.**

He signifieth the same chief priests and Pharisees, who with a wonderful desire gaped after his death, as we have said above.

[Margin: Verse 14.]

**14. I AM POURED OUT LIKE WATER; AND ALL MY BONES ARE SCATTERED.**

[Margin: Verse 15.]

**15. MY HEART IS BECOME LIKE WAX MELTING IN THE MIDST OF MY BOWELS.**

[Margin: Verse 16.]

**16. MY STRENGTH IS DRIED UP LIKE A POTSHERD, AND MY TONGUE HATH CLEAVED TO MY JAWS: AND THOU HAST BROUGHT ME DOWN INTO THE DUST OF DEATH.**

In these verses he declareth how he bore himself against the cruelty of his enemies; and he saith

<!-- p. 124 (PDF 135) -->

that he did not resist their violence, but in all things kept humility, patience and meekness, according to that of *Isa.* chap. l, 6: "I have not turned away my face from them that rebuked me, and spit upon me;" and that of St. Peter, I *Epist.* chap. ii: "When he was reviled, he did not revile: when he suffered, he threatened not: but delivered himself to him that judged him unjustly." And so, saith he, "I am poured out like water," that is, I resisted in no way, but like most soft water I gave myself to be turned and driven to every side. "And all my bones are scattered," that is, all strength is fallen away from me, not that I have truly lost power, but that I would not use it, and permitted my adversaries to use their power, according to that of *Luke* xxii: "This is your hour, and the power of darkness." And so I showed myself feeble and weak to resist, as if I had flesh alone without bones. "My heart is become like wax melting in the midst of my bowels," that is, not only did I show myself outwardly patient and mild, but inwardly also I was humble of heart; my heart did not swell, nor was it hardened against the persecutors, unwilling to forgive, and making ready to take revenge, but on the contrary rather it became like wax melting, out of a loving affection toward them, pitying their blindness, and praying for them, *Luke* xxiii: "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do." That phrase, *in the midst of my bowels* (*in medio ventris mei*, of my belly), signifieth, in the midst of my inward parts. For in Scripture *venter* (the belly) is taken for *the inward parts*, as *John* vii: "Out of his belly shall flow rivers of living water;" and *Cant.* v: "My bowels (*venter meus*) were moved at his touch."[^6] "My strength is dried up like a potsherd," that is, my might hath as it were withered, all its freshness being spent, namely because I so suffered myself to be bound and wounded, as if I were without power to resist. "And my tongue hath cleaved to my jaws," that is, I would not even with words hurt my adversaries, or complain of their injustice. "And thou hast brought me down into the dust of death," that is, and so at length, they persecuting, and I not resisting, thou my God, without whose permission nothing can be done, hast brought me to death and burial. For burial itself is called the dust of death, because the dead are wont to be covered with dust.

[Margin: Burial is called the dust of death.]

[Margin: Verse 17.]

**17. FOR MANY DOGS HAVE ENCOMPASSED ME: THE COUNCIL OF THE MALIGNANT HATH BESIEGED ME.**

[Margin: Verse 18.]

**18. THEY HAVE DUG MY HANDS AND FEET. THEY HAVE NUMBERED ALL MY BONES.**

He declareth the manner in which he was brought down into the dust of death. "For," saith he, "many dogs have encompassed me," that is, many detractors, namely the chief priests and the Pharisees, who by false accusations of subverting the people, of refusing tribute, of aiming at the kingdom, and the like, compelled Pilate to deliver me to the soldiers to be crucified. "The council of the malignant hath besieged me." It is a declaration of the words above. For the many dogs are nothing else but the council, that is, the assembly of the malignant. These malignant ones, I say, "have dug my hands and feet," not indeed with their hands, but by their authority. In the Hebrew there is read כארי *caari*, which signifieth *as a lion*, but the Seventy read כארו *caaru*, which is *foderunt* (they have dug). And that it is altogether so to be read there is no doubt, because the Seventy would never have rendered *foderunt*, if they had read *caari*. St. Jerome also rendered from the Hebrew *foderunt*, and thereby is to be believed to have read *cuaru*. In the book of the Massoreth itself also, at chap. xxiv of *Num.*, the Rabbins, treating of those words, *accubuit ut leo* (he lay down as a lion), which is in Hebrew *caari*, warn that in this Psalm it is indeed to be read *cuari*, but according to the more correct copies to be written *cuaru*. For although out of their perfidy they would read *as a lion*, yet they confess that it is written *caaru*, that is, *they have dug*. Lastly, the meaning is much clearer, *they have dug my hands*, than, *as a lion my hands*. Nor doth it much matter that *foderunt* ought in Hebrew to be written כרו *caru*, not כארו *caaru*, because the letter *aleph* is often redundant, and is therefore called paragogic by the Hebrews. "They have numbered all my bones," that is, from the stretching of the body on the cross it came to pass that all the bones could be numbered. Now David added this of the stretching and the numbering of the bones, that we might understand the crucifixion. For otherwise by the digging of the hands and feet there might have been understood wounds made with a sword, not with nails. In the Hebrew there is אספר *assappar*, in the first person, *numeravi omnia ossa mea* (I have numbered all my bones). But it is easy for an error to have happened by the change of one or two letters. Surely the sense is clearer, if we read *numeraverunt* (they have numbered), as without doubt the Seventy Interpreters read, and that reading St. Jerome, St. Augustine, Theodoret and Euthymius followed in the exposition of this place.

[Margin: Verse 19.]

**19. AND THEY HAVE LOOKED AND STARED UPON ME. THEY PARTED MY GARMENTS AMONGST THEM; AND UPON MY VESTURE THEY CAST LOTS.**

He addeth to the punishment of the cross the ignominy of nakedness: They, saith he, curiously looked upon my whole body, because it was stripped of all garments, seeing that the soldiers parted those garments among themselves, and upon the coat which remained they cast lots. All which John the Evangelist, chap. xix, witnesseth to have been fulfilled to the letter.

[Margin: Verse 20.]

**20. BUT THOU, O LORD, REMOVE NOT THY HELP TO A DISTANCE FROM ME; LOOK TOWARDS MY DEFENCE.**

He returneth to the prayer which he had begun in the beginning of the Psalm, and had repeated again in the tenth and eleventh verse. And so, his whole passion being now set forth, he asketh God for a speedy resurrection: for by it he will be perfectly delivered from all persecution and injury of his adversaries. "But thou, O Lord, remove not thy help to a distance from me," that is, my adversaries have come to the height of malice, and have spent all their strength upon me; now it is thine to defer help no longer,

<!-- p. 125 (PDF 136) -->

but to take up my defence as a father. In the Hebrew it is read to the word thus: *Et tu, Domine, ne longe fias; fortitudo mea, ad auxilium meum festina* (And thou, O Lord, be not far off; my strength, make haste to my help). These very words might in some manner, a few things being changed, be brought to agree with the words of the Greek and Latin edition, but it seemeth not worth the pains to labour in this matter, since the sense is altogether the same.

[Margin: Verse 21.]

**21. DELIVER, O GOD, MY SOUL FROM THE SWORD: MY ONLY ONE FROM THE HAND OF THE DOG.**

[Margin: Verse 22.]

**22. SAVE ME FROM THE LION'S MOUTH; AND MY LOWNESS FROM THE HORNS OF THE UNICORNS.**

He declareth in what he would have help be present to him. "Deliver," saith he, "my soul from the sword," *framea* or *gladius*, that is, free my life from the instrument of death. For he putteth the sword, which is an instrument of death, for any instrument of death whatever, which is frequent in Scripture, II *Kings* xii: "The sword shall not depart from thy house." *Ezech.* xxxiii: "If he see the sword coming upon the land," etc. *Rom.* viii: "Who shall separate us from the love of Christ? shall tribulation? or distress? or famine? or the sword?" And he putteth the soul for the life, which likewise is most usual in Scripture. "My only one from the hand of the dog." By the dog he understandeth those dogs of which he had spoken above, but he useth the singular number, either by the figure of collection, or to signify the union in evil of his enemies. And by *my only one* he understandeth the same life of his, which he loved singularly, as it was fitting that the life of the Word incarnate should be loved. "Save me from the lion's mouth," of that lion of which it was said above: "They have opened their mouths against me, as a lion ravening and roaring." "And my lowness from the horns of the unicorns." Before he had said: "Fat bulls have besieged me;" now in the place of bulls are put unicorns, which are untamed beasts, and fiercer even than bulls, to signify that the wickedness and cruelty of his enemies was not lessened by so many torments, but increased. Moreover in all these words the Lord doth not ask that his temporal life be saved, but (as we have often said) he asketh that his life be speedily restored, and so restored, that it be no more exposed to the bites of dogs, or the claws of lions, or the horns of unicorns, or of bulls. That phrase, *humilitatem meam* (my lowness), is in the Hebrew עניתני, which St. Jerome rendereth *exaudi me* (hear me); but Pagninus, *respondisti mihi* (thou hast answered me). But the Seventy Interpreters did not read עניתני, but עניתי *honithi*, one letter being taken away, that is, *my lowness*.

[Margin: Why the soul of Christ is called his only one; and wherefore Christ loved it?]

[Margin: Verse 23.]

**23. I WILL DECLARE THY NAME TO MY BRETHREN: IN THE MIDST OF THE CHURCH WILL I PRAISE THEE.**

Now he beginneth to relate the fruit of his resurrection, which is the conversion of the world to God: "I will declare," saith he, "thy name to my brethren," that is, after I shall have risen again, I will send the apostles into the whole world, and by them "I will declare thy name," that is, I will make the knowledge of thy divinity manifest to all men, who are my brethren on account of the flesh assumed, and so "in the midst of the church will I praise thee," that is, no longer in a corner of Judea, but in the midst of the great Church gathered into one out of Jews and Gentiles will I praise thee, namely by my ministers. This place the Apostle bringeth forward, *Heb.* ii, 11: "For which cause," saith he, "he is not ashamed to call them brethren, saying: I will declare thy name to my brethren; in the midst of the church will I praise thee."

[Margin: Verse 24.]

**24. YE THAT FEAR THE LORD, PRAISE HIM: ALL YE THE SEED OF JACOB, GLORIFY HIM.**

[Margin: Verse 25.]

**25. LET ALL THE SEED OF ISRAEL FEAR HIM: BECAUSE HE HATH NOT SLIGHTED NOR DESPISED THE SUPPLICATION OF THE POOR MAN.**

Because he had said: "In the midst of the church will I praise thee," which was to be fulfilled by his faithful, therefore he now exhorteth the faithful themselves to praise God. "Ye that fear the Lord," that is, ye who have knowledge of him, and worship him. For to fear God is taken in Scripture for to worship. So *Jonas* i, Jonas, being asked of his people: "I," saith he, "am a Hebrew, I fear the God who made heaven and earth;"[^7] and *Dan.* vi: "Let all fear the God of Daniel;" and *Judith* i,[^8] it is said of Judith, "that she feared the Lord very much." And so the sense is: Ye who know and worship the true God, praise him. And lest we should think that the faithful, to whom he speaketh, are few, as being the one nation of the Jews, he addeth: "All ye the seed of Jacob, glorify him, and let all the seed of Israel fear him," that is, let all Israelites glorify and fear God, not those only who are Israelites according to the flesh, but those also who are Israelites according to the promise, such as are all the Gentiles converted to Christ. See St. Augustine in epistle 200, otherwise 187, *to Asellicus*, where he showeth that Christians are true Israelites, not by flesh, but by spirit, faith and promise. The words that follow: "Because he hath not slighted nor despised the supplication of the poor man," the Hebrews make a new verse, and join to it the whole following verse. But the Seventy pointed otherwise. The Lord therefore giveth the reason why he would have God praised by all, namely because he heard the prayer which he had poured forth for his resurrection and glory, for victory against the devil, and the redemption of mankind. Now he calleth himself poor, because such he was in the time of the passion, when naked and forsaken, suffering hunger and thirst, he hung on the cross. In the Hebrew, for *deprecationem pauperis* (the supplication of the poor man), there is ענות, which St. Jerome rendereth *modestiam* (modesty), and it could also be rendered *affliction, lowness, cry*, etc.

[Margin: To fear God in Scripture is to worship him.]

[Margin: Verse 26.]

**26. NEITHER HATH HE TURNED AWAY HIS FACE FROM ME: AND WHEN I CRIED TO HIM HE HEARD ME.**

He repeateth the same in other words. In the Hebrew there is read, *nec avertit faciem suam ab eo, et cum clamaret ad*

<!-- p. 126 (PDF 137) -->

*eum, exaudivit* (neither hath he turned away his face from him, and when he cried to him he heard). It is likely that the Seventy read *iod*, where now there is *vau*, since the letters are alike, and *iod* is the mark of the pronoun of the first person, but *vau* of the third. But the meaning is altogether the same. For if it be said: *Neither hath he turned away his face from him*, by that phrase, *from him*, is understood *from the poor man*, of whom it was said, *he hath not despised the supplication*, or *the affliction of the poor man*: and that poor man is Christ himself.

[Margin: Verse 27.]

**27. WITH THEE IS MY PRAISE IN A GREAT CHURCH: I WILL PAY MY VOWS IN THE SIGHT OF THEM THAT FEAR HIM.**

He had exhorted his faithful to praise God; now he foretelleth that this will altogether come to pass. "With thee is my praise in a great church," that is, the praise which I shall sing to thee by my faithful shall be with thee, or before thee, in a great Church, not in an assembly of a few Hebrews, but in the Church of all nations. This place might also be expounded of the praise wherewith Christ is praised in the Church before God. Euthymius readeth, *a te laus mea* (from thee is my praise); whence he seemeth to have read ἀπὸ σοῦ; but in our text there is παρὰ σοῦ, *apud te* (with thee), and so Jerome, Augustine, Theodoret and others read. The Hebrew word מאתך is rendered by St. Jerome *apud te* (with thee); by others, *a te* (from thee). For it admitteth both expositions. "I will pay my vows in the sight of them that fear him:" by vows there seem to be understood in this place vows of sacrifices and oblations, according to that of *Isa.* xix, 21: "They shall worship him with sacrifices and offerings: and they shall make vows to the Lord, and perform them." For Christ, when he saw that the whole burnt offering of his death had been most pleasing to God, seemeth in a manner to have promised that he would most frequently offer that whole burnt offering by his ministers, in the manner that was fitting, and this is what he now saith: "I will pay my vows in the sight of them that fear him," that is, by my ministers, the priests of the new Testament, I will continually offer a sacrifice most pleasing to God, and that "in the sight of them that fear," that is, of them that worship him, and thereby of the true faithful. For it is not lawful to offer this sacrifice before infidels.[^9]

[Margin: The sacrifice of the new Testament, to be offered by priests, is promised.]

[Margin: Verse 28.]

**28. THE POOR SHALL EAT AND SHALL BE FILLED: AND THEY SHALL PRAISE THE LORD THAT SEEK HIM: THEIR HEARTS SHALL LIVE FOR EVER AND EVER.**

Of this sacrifice "the poor shall eat," who acknowledge their own spiritual poverty and need, "and shall be filled," because they shall taste the good which is above every good, "and they shall praise the Lord," giving thanks to God for so signal a benefit, all they "that seek him," who hunger, and therefore eagerly seek the Lord. "Their hearts shall live for ever and ever." This shall be the fruit of that refection, that hearts refreshed with so noble a food shall live with a spiritual life, the life of grace meanwhile, and afterwards the life of glory for ever and ever. For so the Truth saith, *John* vi: "He that eateth this bread shall live for ever." For as corruptible food preserveth life for a time, so incorruptible food giveth life everlasting. In the Hebrew there is, *vivet cor vestrum in æternum* (your heart shall live for ever), but one letter being changed, of לבבכם, *your heart*, is made לבבם, *their heart*.

[Margin: Verse 29.]

**29. ALL THE ENDS OF THE EARTH SHALL REMEMBER, AND SHALL BE CONVERTED TO THE LORD.**

He giveth the cause why he is to praise God in a great Church, namely because all nations shall be converted to God by the merit of the sacrifice of the cross. "They shall remember," saith he, the beginning whence they came forth, that is, they shall call to mind that they were of old created by God in their first parent, which through original sin they had long since given over to forgetfulness, and therefore had said to wood and stone: "Thou art my father," *Jer.* iii;[^10] "they shall remember," I say, their first creation, and "all the ends of the earth shall be converted to the Lord," that is, all the nations which inhabit the world, even to its uttermost ends. Now by all nations are signified not every single man of the whole world, but some out of all nations.

[Margin: Verse 30.]

**30. AND ALL THE KINDREDS OF THE GENTILES SHALL ADORE IN HIS SIGHT.**

It is a declaration of the verse above. For this is for all the ends of the earth to be converted to the Lord, that all the kindreds of the Gentiles, which are found spread abroad even to the ends of the earth, adore the Lord, having left their idols. Moreover to adore before the Lord is the same as to adore the Lord, because the Hebrew word ישתחוו, which we render *adorare* (to adore), properly signifieth *to be bowed down*. Now to be bowed down before the Lord is the same as to adore the Lord.

[Margin: Verse 31.]

**31. FOR THE KINGDOM IS THE LORD'S; AND HE SHALL HAVE DOMINION OVER THE NATIONS.**

With good reason shall all nations be converted to the Lord, and adore him, because he is the true and natural King of all; therefore he henceforth, not the devils, shall have dominion over the nations.

[Margin: Verse 32.]

**32. ALL THE FAT ONES OF THE EARTH HAVE EATEN AND HAVE ADORED: ALL THEY THAT GO DOWN TO THE EARTH SHALL FALL BEFORE HIM.**

Because he had said: "The poor shall eat and shall be filled, and they shall praise the Lord, and all the kindreds of the Gentiles shall adore him;" lest any one should think that only the poor and the common people are to be converted to the Lord, he addeth concerning the rich and powerful: "All the fat ones of the earth have eaten and have adored," that is, the fat ones of the earth themselves also, who abound in earthly goods, princes themselves, emperors, kings, shall eat of the table of the Lord, and shall adore and praise the common Lord, whose is the kingdom over all nations. Now after the prophetic manner the past is put for the future, *manducaverunt et adoraverunt* (they have eaten and have adored), for *manducabunt et adorabunt* (they shall eat and shall adore). And lastly "all they that go down to the earth," or, as the Hebrew word soundeth, *that go down to the dust*, that is, all mortals, who at length by death are dissolved into dust, "all," I say,

<!-- p. 127 (PDF 138) -->

"shall fall before him," that is, shall bend their knees and adore. And so the conversion of the nations, which is the fruit of the Lord's passion and resurrection, shall be truly general.

[Margin: The general conversion the fruit of the passion of Christ.]

[Margin: Verse 33.]

**33. AND TO HIM MY SOUL SHALL LIVE: AND MY SEED SHALL SERVE HIM.**

He concludeth the Psalm, saying that he will henceforth live for ever to the glory of God, and that his posterity will faithfully serve the same God. Now he putteth the soul for the whole man, which is frequent in the Scriptures, and especially in the Psalms. "My soul," that is, I, shall live to him, "and my seed," that is, my posterity, born again of water and the Holy Ghost, "shall serve him." In the Hebrew there is, *et anima ejus non vivet* (and his soul shall not live); but it manifestly appeareth that the Seventy did not read נפשו, *his soul*, but נפשי, *my soul*. For only a small part of one letter is to be lengthened, that of one word another may be made. So also it is certain that the Seventy did not read לא, *not*, but לו, *to him*. And since the meaning of the reading which the Seventy had is far clearer, nay since the meaning of the reading of the modern Hebrews signifieth plainly nothing, it seemeth that it cannot be doubted that the Hebrew text hath been corrupted by the fault of the copyists.

[Margin: Verse 34.]

**34. THERE SHALL BE DECLARED TO THE LORD A GENERATION TO COME: AND THE HEAVENS SHALL SHEW FORTH HIS JUSTICE TO A PEOPLE THAT SHALL BE BORN, WHICH THE LORD HATH MADE.**

He declareth that which he said: "My seed shall serve him," saying: "A generation to come," that is, the people of the new Testament shall receive good tidings of the Lord, and of his justice, which is the justice of faith. That phrase, *annuntiabitur generatio ventura*, signifieth, *annuntiabitur generationi venturæ* (it shall be declared to the generation to come), or *nuntium accipiet generatio ventura* (the generation to come shall receive tidings). For it is a Greek phrase, such as is found also in *Matt.* xi: "The poor have the gospel preached to them" (*Pauperes evangelizantur*), that is, the gospel is preached to the poor; and I *Tim.* i: "According to the gospel, which hath been committed to my trust;" for in the Greek there is to the word, *quod creditus sum* (with which I have been entrusted); but the Interpreter changed the Greek phrase, which he left in *Matt.* xi. And so the sense is not: "There shall be declared to the Lord a generation to come," that is, a generation to come shall be ascribed to the Lord; but: *Annuntiabitur de Domino*, or *Dominus, generationi venturæ* (There shall be declared concerning the Lord, or the Lord shall be declared, to the generation to come). Which is gathered both from the Hebrew, where there is לדור *laddor*, that is, *to the generation*; and because the same is repeated, or declared, in the words that follow, after the manner of David: "The heavens shall shew forth his justice to a people that shall be born." For *a generation to come* and *a people that shall be born* are the same. The Lord therefore shall be declared to the generation to come, because "the heavens," that is, heavenly men, "shall shew forth the justice of God to a people that shall be born." Now that is called the justice of God, which is the justice of faith, which God giveth freely to them that believe in Christ, and which truly maketh men just. For this the Gospel chiefly taught, that all are sinners, and cannot be justified by their own strength, but that true justice is to be looked for from God alone through the faith of Christ. In the Hebrew there is not *generationi venturæ* (to the generation to come); but after the noun *generationi* there is a point, and then the verb *venient* (they shall come), which is joined with the following, *et annuntiabunt* (and they shall declare). But the Seventy Interpreters had not that point after the noun *generationi*; and the verb *venient* they read in the singular, *veniet* (it shall come), and therefore rightly read, *generationi quæ veniet* (to the generation which shall come), that is, to the generation to come. The noun *cœli* (the heavens), when it is said *annuntiabunt cœli*, is not in the Hebrew, nor in the Greek text, but the Latin Interpreter added it for the sake of explanation. And this word *cœli* is read in St. Jerome and St. Augustine in the commentary on this Psalm, but is not read by Theodoret and Euthymius, Greek writers, because (as we have said) the old Latin Interpreter added it. That which is at the end, *which the Lord hath made*, signifieth that this people of the new Testament is the peculiar people of God, properly born again of God himself, and created in Christ in good works, as the Apostle saith, *to Titus*, chap. iii.[^11] There is a like sentence in *Ps.* cxvii: "This is the day which the Lord hath made."

[Margin: The justice of God maketh men truly just.]
