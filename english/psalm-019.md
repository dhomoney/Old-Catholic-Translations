BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XIX.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*Unto the end, a Psalm for David.*[^1]

This Psalm seemeth to have been sung by David foreseeing the coming of Christ to vanquish the powers of the air, to which also the two following Psalms pertain.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. MAY THE LORD HEAR THEE IN THE DAY OF TRIBULATION: MAY THE NAME OF THE GOD OF JACOB PROTECT THEE.**

Since David expresseth the name of no man, he without doubt speaketh to him, to whom all the desires of the just and the oracles of all the Prophets looked. And as if he saw Christ going forth to war against the devil, and arming himself with prayer, the tribulation of the passion being at hand, he crieth out to him: "May the Lord hear thee in the day of tribulation," that is, of the passion, in which, as the Apostle saith, *Heb.* v, 7, "with a strong cry and tears, offering up prayers and supplications to him that was able to save him from death, he was heard for his reverence." Now he was heard, not that he might escape death, but that by dying he might destroy death, and by rising again restore life; and that shame might be turned into glory, mortality into immortality, as he himself saith, *John* xvii, 1: "Father, the hour is come, glorify thy Son;" of this hearing the Prophet speaketh. To which also pertaineth that which followeth: "May the name of the God of Jacob protect thee." By *the name* can be understood invocation, as in that place: "In my name they shall cast out devils," *Mark*, last chap. There can also be understood power, or authority, as *John* v: "I am come in the name of my Father." Lastly, by the name of God can be understood simply God: for often in the Scriptures the name is put for the person whose name it is, as when blessed Peter saith, *Acts* iv, 12: "There is no

[Margin: The name of God is God himself.]

other name under heaven whereby we must be saved;" and when St. Luke saith in the *Acts*, in the Greek edition, chap. i: "Now the number of persons together was about an hundred and twenty."[^2] The Prophet added *the name of Jacob*, because Jacob signifieth the people of God, of which people Christ is the head, as if he said: May the God of his people protect thee, that by protecting thee, who art the head of that people, the whole people may in consequence be saved. Now protection is asked from the darts of the enemies, that they may not hurt; and they would then truly have hurt, if they had been able to hinder either the resurrection of Christ, or the faith and name of Christ, or the spreading of the Church.

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. MAY HE SEND THEE HELP FROM THE SANCTUARY: AND DEFEND THEE OUT OF SION.**

By *the sanctuary* (*sanctum*, the holy place) he understandeth the holy mountain, which is called Sion, as is explained in the second part of the verse. Now that mountain was called holy, because of the presence of the ark of the Testament. But in this place there seemeth to be meant the heavenly mount Sion, of which the Apostle speaketh, *Heb.* xii, 22: "You are come to mount Sion, and to the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem," etc. For the Prophet maketh mention in this place of mount Sion, to show that God from his most high mountain beholdeth all things (for Sion also is interpreted *a watch-tower*), and therefore can conveniently see Christ fighting, and thence send him succour; but a place so high, that from it all things are seen, is not the earthly mount Sion, but the heavenly. And so he saith: "May he send thee help from the holy mountain," that is, from the most lofty dwelling of the heavens, whence he seeth all things; "and defend thee out of Sion," that is, from that most high watch-tower, whence he looketh forth upon all things.

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. MAY HE BE MINDFUL OF ALL THY SACRIFICES: AND MAY THY WHOLE BURNT OFFERING BE MADE FAT.**

Because the Lord, being about to fight with the enemy of mankind, not only prayed, but also sacrificed, that is, not only offered a prayer of words, so to speak, but also a prayer of deed, which is sacrifice; therefore the Prophet, as he had said of the prayer of words: "May the Lord hear thee," so now saith of the prayer of deed: "May he be mindful of all thy sacrifices," that is, may he not despise, but ever have in remembrance, and regard all thy sacrifices. "And may thy whole burnt offering be made fat," that is, may it be accepted, as whole burnt offerings of fat beasts are wont to be accepted. For a whole burnt offering is the more pleasing, the better and more perfect the beast that is sacrificed. Hence is that of *Dan.* iii, 40: "As in thousands of fat lambs: so let our sacrifice be made in thy sight, that it may please thee." Moreover Christ offered many sacrifices, and at the last a whole burnt offering; therefore the Prophet saith: "May he be mindful of all thy sacrifices: and may thy whole burnt offering be made fat." The many sacrifices are the many sufferings which the Lord bore for the glory of God, while he lived; the whole burnt offering, whereby he offered himself whole, is the death of the cross itself. There is added in this place סלה *sela*, in the Hebrew text, which signifieth *always*. And so the sense is: May the Lord be mindful always of all the sufferings, and of the very death of Christ. Now the prayer of David seemeth a prophecy rather than a prayer: for truly the passion of Christ ever is, and ever shall be, in force before God; nay, even from the beginning of the world it hath ever been in force, and through it all good things are given to us.

[Margin: Christ offered many sacrifices.]

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. MAY HE GIVE THEE ACCORDING TO THY OWN HEART; AND CONFIRM ALL THY COUNSELS.**

He declareth the end of the prayer and of the sacrifice, as if he said: To this end may God hear thee, and accept thy sacrifice, that thou mayest come whither thou desirest to come, and attain the end set before thee, and that there be none who can keep thee from it. "May he give thee according to thy own heart," that is, may he give thee that which is according to thy heart, that is, what thou desirest. "And confirm all thy counsels," that is, and may he establish all that thou hast resolved, may he will it to be ratified, and permit and grant it to be put in execution. In the Hebrew there is, *omne consilium tuum impleat* (may he fulfil all thy counsel), which is the same. And so the sense is: May God hear thee, and accept thy sacrifice, that thou mayest destroy the works of the devil, that thou mayest deliver man from captivity, that thou mayest give life everlasting to them that believe in thee, etc. For that this was the desire of the heart of Christ, and his counsel, and his resolve, the sacred Writings teach. "The Son of God," saith John, "came, that he might destroy the works of the devil," I *John* iii.[^3] And blessed Paul: "Christ came into this world to save sinners," I *Tim.* i. And the Lord himself: "The Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost," *Luke* xii.[^4]

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. WE WILL REJOICE IN THY SALVATION; AND IN THE NAME OF OUR GOD WE SHALL BE EXALTED.**

When that shall have been done which we ask, namely, that thou our king shalt have brought back the victory over the enemies, then "we will rejoice" not only with the heart, but also with the voice (for this the Hebrew word רנן *ranan* signifieth), "in thy salvation," that is, because of thy safety, that is, because thou shalt have returned unharmed from the war, which salvation and safety will overflow upon us; "and in the name of our God," from whom so great a victory hath proceeded, "we shall be exalted," namely we shall account and call ourselves great, not for any greatness which is in us, but for the great Lord whom we have. In the Hebrew there is נדגל *nidgal*, that is, *we will set up a banner*; but the Seventy read נגדל *nigdal*, one letter being transposed, that is, *magnificabitur* (shall be magnified).[^5] And so an error hath crept into the Hebrew by the fault of the scribes, although that reading also hath a very good sense.

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. THE LORD FULFIL ALL THY PETITIONS: NOW HAVE I KNOWN THAT THE LORD HATH SAVED HIS ANOINTED.**

He repeateth again the prayer of good omen, as if he said: May the Lord therefore fulfil all thy petitions, from which so many good things are to follow. "Now

<!-- p. 111 (PDF 122) -->

have I known that the Lord hath saved his anointed," that is, I therefore boldly repeat, that the Lord may hear thee, and fulfil thy petitions, because now by the enlightening of the Lord I have known that they are altogether to be fulfilled: for I have known that "the Lord hath saved," that is, will most certainly save "his anointed," and already in his predetermination hath saved him, hath raised him from death, hath set him in heaven, hath put his enemies under his feet.

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. HE WILL HEAR HIM FROM HIS HOLY HEAVEN: THE SALVATION OF HIS RIGHT HAND IS IN POWERS.**

Because he had said: "Hath saved," he declareth that this was said of salvation in predetermination, not in execution. For, that what hath been predetermined may be fulfilled in its own time, "he will hear him from his holy heaven," and so the Lord will save his anointed. That phrase, *the salvation of his right hand is in powers*, can have a twofold sense; for *potentatus* (powers) can be taken for power and strength, and this the Hebrew word גבורה *gheburah* more properly soundeth, and then the sense will be, *in potentiis* (in powers), that is, in great power the salvation of the right hand of God will be present to Christ. And although the Greek word *dynastia*, and the Latin *potentatus*, properly signify *empire* or *principality*, yet they can be taken for *power* and *strength*, as the same words are taken in *Ps.* lxxxix: "But if in the strong (*in potentatibus*) they be fourscore years," etc.; in *Ps.* xx, last verse: "We will sing and praise thy power" (*virtutes tuas*), where in the Greek there is δυναστείας. *Potentatus* can also be taken for principality and empire, as the Greek word *dynastia*, and the Latin *potentatus* properly signify, and then the sense will be: "He will hear him from his holy heaven, and in principalities," that is, because in principalities, namely in the founding or the preserving of them, the salvation of his right hand is chiefly necessary. For although princes seem to have many defences for their safety, as horses and chariots, arms and soldiers, castles and fortifications, yet all these are nothing, if the salvation of the right hand of God be wanting; therefore he rightly subjoineth:

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. SOME TRUST IN CHARIOTS, AND SOME IN HORSES: BUT WE WILL CALL UPON THE NAME OF THE LORD OUR GOD.**

He goeth on to explain the victory of Christ, which he foresaw would be, and saith: "Some trust in chariots, and some in horses," that is, some of the adversaries trust in armed chariots, others in fierce horses; and by these two names he would have understood all other human defences, which men of old used in wars, both for battle and for flight. "But we," with our head and king Christ, will place our trust not so much in chariots and in horses, as "in the name of God" almighty, called upon by devout prayer. Moreover it is the same, *nomen Domini invocare* (to call upon the name of the Lord), and *in nomine Domini invocare* (to call in the name of the Lord), according to the phrase of holy Scripture. Nor doth it much matter that in the Hebrew text there is נזכיר *nazchir*, *commemorabimus* (we will make mention): for David speaketh not of any mention of God whatever, but of that which is made by those who implore his name, and ask help of him.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. THEY ARE BOUND, AND HAVE FALLEN; BUT WE ARE RISEN, AND ARE SET UPRIGHT.**

He showeth how much more profitable it is to trust in the name of the Lord, than in chariots or horses. They who trusted in chariots, or in horses, "are bound, and have fallen; but we," who trust in God, "are risen, and are set upright." And at the same time he pointeth out a wonderful change of things. For before the victory of Christ, the enemy of mankind, lifted on high, as it were in chariots and on horses, threatened men cast down by original sin; and likewise the princes of the Jews, Herod and Pilate, and other visible enemies of Christ, as it were standing upright, insulted Christ suffering, and his lowly disciples; but a little after, all those, bound together and entangled, fell down, but these rose, and set upright shall stand for ever. In the Hebrew there is כרעו *carehu*, *incurvati sunt* (they are bowed down): whence some think that the Latin word is corrupted, and that it should be read *obliquati sunt* (they are bent aside), and not *obligati sunt* (they are bound); but they are deceived. For the Latin reading followeth the Greek; and in the Greek there is συνεποδίσθησαν, that is, *colligati sunt* (they are bound together), as with fetters, which our Interpreter hath rightly rendered *obligati sunt*, that is, bound and entangled, so that they could not fight or stand, *they have fallen*. And so the Seventy looked not so much to the words as to the meaning.

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. O LORD, SAVE THE KING: AND HEAR US IN THE DAY THAT WE SHALL CALL UPON THEE.**

He concludeth by joining the end with the beginning. At the beginning he had said: "May the Lord hear thee in the day of tribulation;" now he confirmeth the same, turning his speech to God: "O Lord, save the king" from his tribulation; and in the same manner "hear us," and save us "in the day that we shall call upon thee," that is, in the day of our tribulation, in which we will call upon none other than thee. In the Hebrew there are the same words, but the pointing is different. And so St. Jerome and the moderns read thus: *Domine, salva; rex, exaudi nos in die, qua invocaverimus te* (O Lord, save; O king, hear us in the day that we shall call upon thee). But since the noun המלך *hammelech* can be rendered *rex* and *regem*, because the Hebrews do not decline nouns by cases, and the Seventy Interpreters rendered τὸν βασιλέα, that is, *regem* (the king, as the object), and all the ancient Fathers so read; the reading of the modern Hebrews is not to be made much of, to which the pointing was added after the times of the Seventy. Moreover St. Jerome rendered faithfully, but he had a text less correct. This Psalm might fitly be recited, when Christian princes set out to war against the infidels.

[^1]: Translator's note: Bellarmine does not number the title, and divides the Psalm into ten verses. The Douay-Rheims Bible has nine: its verse 1 is the title; its 2 to 6 are his 1 to 5; its 7 holds his 6 and 7; its 8 is his 8; and its 9 holds his 9 and 10.

[^2]: Translator's note: the words are those of Acts i, 15 in the Douay-Rheims. Bellarmine's point lies in the Greek, which has ὀνομάτων, "of names", where the Vulgate has *hominum*, "of persons".

[^3]: Translator's note: Bellarmine's words (*Filius Dei venit, ut dissolvat opera diaboli*) differ from the Vulgate at I John iii, 8, which has *In hoc apparuit Filius Dei*, in the Douay-Rheims "For this purpose, the Son of God appeared, that he might destroy the works of the devil".

[^4]: Translator's note: the words are at Luke xix, 10, not in chap. xii.

[^5]: Translator's note: so the print, in the third person. The Hebrew form he cites is of the first person plural, "we shall be magnified", the *magnificabimur* of the verse.
