#### CHAPTER II. WHAT THINGS ARE NECESSARY THAT A POWER BE FREE, AND WORK FREELY.

<!-- p. 4 -->

In the foregoing chapter we explained only the words or manners of speaking; but now something concerning the thing itself is to be added and established, that afterwards, in explaining the helps of grace and reconciling them with the liberty of the will, we may be able to proceed solidly and efficaciously.

**1.** *The first assertion. — It is of the nature of a power formally free, as such, that it be an active power.* — First of all therefore we lay down that it is of the nature of a power formally free that, in so far as it is such, it be an active power. It is a certain and common assertion, which we can prove, first, from Scripture; for wheresoever it declareth the liberty of the will, it useth words pertaining to a proper power of acting; as is that of *Ecclus.* xv: *Stretch forth thy hand to which thou wilt;* and that: *For do not thou the things that he hateth;* and that of chap. xxxi: *He that could do evil things, and hath not done them.* Likewise that of *Zach.* i: *Turn ye to me,* by which words *we are admonished of our liberty,* as the Council of Trent said; but no man turneth himself save by acting. Like to it is that of *Apoc.* iii: *If any man shall open to me the door, I will come in to him;* for the word of opening includeth efficiency. And lastly II *Pet.* ii:[^1] *Labour, that by good works you may make sure your calling and election;* and the like occur in many places. Secondly, we prove it from the Council of Trent, sess. 6, chap. 5, where, when it had said that men are called by God through grace, that, by freely assenting to the same grace and

<!-- p. 5 -->

co-operating with it, they may be converted to God himself, it explaineth that free co-operation, saying: *So that, God touching the heart of man through the illumination of the Holy Ghost, neither doth man himself do nothing at all, receiving that inspiration, forasmuch as he can also cast it away. Nor yet could he without grace move himself unto justice before him by his own free will;* and therefore in can. 4 it condemneth those who say *that free will co-operateth nothing by assenting to God exciting it, or doth nothing at all, and behaveth itself merely passively.* Therefore, in the mind of the Council, a passive power sufficeth not for free use; liberty therefore is formally in an active power.

**2.** Thirdly, the same is proved by the authority of the Fathers; Augustine, Book XII of the *City of God*, chap. 6 and 7, where, treating of the cause of an evil act, he saith that it is reduced to the will itself as to an efficient and deficient cause. And he teacheth the same in Book I *Of Free Will*, from chap. 12, and Book III, chap. 1 and 2. And he addeth in chap. 3 that that is in our power which we do when we will, wherefore *nothing is so much in our power as the will itself.* Which he repeateth and confirmeth in Book I of the *Retractations*, chap. 9 and 22. Now a thing is not said to be in our power by reason of a passive power, but by reason of an active one, which the very name of power (*potestas*) signifieth. Whence the same Augustine, Book I of the *Retractations*, chap. 1, reviewing the words which he had set down in quest. 1 of Book II *To Simplician*:[^2] *Although it be in every man's power what he willeth;* saith that he spoke them for this cause, *because we say not that anything is in our power, save that which is done when we will, wherein the first and greatest thing is the willing itself. For without any interval of time the will itself is at hand, when we will.* Whence also in the book of *Eighty-three Questions*, question 8: *He perceiveth* (saith he) *that the soul is moved of itself, who perceiveth that there is a will in himself: for if we will, it is not another that willeth for us, and this motion of the soul is spontaneous;* and quest. 24: *Neither sin, nor a deed rightly done* (saith he) *can justly be imputed to any man who hath done nothing by his own will.* And in this manner he saith that both are in the free choice of the will. The will therefore is free through the active power which it hath over its acts. So also Damascene said, Book II *Of the Faith*, chap. 25, that man is of free will for this cause, because he hath in his power those actions which he is said to work freely; and that therefore all deliberation is undertaken for the sake of action; and he addeth in chap. 26: *Those things are in our power which we are free to do or not to do,* and other things which he pursueth, by all which he teacheth that liberty is in us through an active power. The same is gathered from Anselm, the book *Of the Virginal Conception*, chap. 6; Bernard, the book *Of Grace and Free Will*; and Nyssen is cited, Book VII of the *Philosophy*, chap. 1 and 2; Nemesius hath it,[^3] *Of the Nature of Man*, chap. 29, 39 and following.

**3.** Fourthly, it is proved from the scholastics, and first of all from St. Thomas, Part I, quest. 82, art. 4, and quest. 83, in the body and ad 2. Likewise Part I-II, quest. 9, art. 1 and 3, in which places he saith that the will is a power moving itself and others after the manner of an agent, and that the same is free will, in so far as it can move itself to either side. He hath the same in question 22 *Of Truth*, art. 9, in the body, and ad 1, and question twenty-four, article four, in the body, and ad 15, in which solution he declareth more expressly that free will, as it is such, is a power not passive, but operative. The same Capreolus delivereth, on Book II, distinction 24, question 1, article 1, conclusion 5; and Scotus on Book II, distinction 25, in the arguments To the contrary, and in the whole discourse of the question; Durandus, distinction 24, quest. 2, num. 10, 19 and 34;[^4] Gregory, the same distinction, the only question; Henry, Quodlibet 10, question nine, where, a little from the beginning, he saith that the whole will is moving and moved, but moving in so far as it is free, which liberty, he saith, is formally in it. He hath the same in Quodlibet 12, quest. 17: *For this* (saith he) *that anything be said to be truly from free will, it behoveth that it be from it, as from the proximate mover, and thus the will is mistress of its acts.* Bellarmine, Book III *Of Grace and Free Will*, chap. 10.

**4.** Lastly it is declared by reason, which Scotus above hath very well touched: for a free power is that which hath it in its hand and power to be changed, or not to be changed; for this is to be mistress of its act or change, in which dominion liberty consisteth. But this power is not in a passive power, as it is passive; therefore it behoveth that it be in an active power, in so far as it is active. The minor is proved, because it is not in the power and dominion of the patient, as it is such, whether it suffer or not: for this dependeth on the agent, because the patient suffereth for this cause, that the agent acteth upon it. For action bringeth in passion, and precedeth it in the order of nature or of reason, and therefore the passion is such as the action is, and not conversely, speaking causally and *a priori*. Therefore the action is not in the power of the patient, as it is patient, that is, in its dominion, but in that of the agent; because the prior is not in the power of the posterior, as a precept is not in the power of the subject, but of the superior; therefore the passion also is not in the power of the patient, as it is such, but in

<!-- p. 6 -->

the power of the agent, because, the action being posited, the passion necessarily followeth, and therefore the passion can be or be denominated free only so far as the action shall be free; therefore both are in the power and dominion of the agent; therefore liberty is only in an active power, as it is active. Wherefore, although the will be receptive of its own act, and under that respect be a passive power, yet it is not free, save in so far as it hath the power of eliciting and of not eliciting its act, and the reception of it can be free to the will itself only in so far as to effect that act is free to it: for if it received the act from another agent, itself effecting nothing on its part, it would receive it of necessity: and if it were in the power of the other agent not to send such an act into the will, that effect would be free to the other agent, not to the will itself. In a passive power therefore, as it is passive, there is no liberty.

**5.** *Objection.* — Thou wilt say: the patient sometimes determineth the action of the agent, or contributeth to it, or hindereth it; therefore by the same reason it can come from the part of the passive power that the agent change it or change it not; therefore liberty also will be able to be in a passive power, as it is passive. The antecedent is plain in a superior and universal cause acting of necessity; for it is determined to this or that action on the part of the patient, as is plain in the sun drying clay and melting wax. Likewise, in particular causes, the action is greater or less according to the disposition of the patient, and sometimes is altogether hindered by reason of its resistance. And lastly in moral and supernatural things, when God infuseth a habit into the will, the will itself behaveth merely passively, and nevertheless the reception is free to it, because it dependeth on its disposition.

**6.** *Answer.* — I answer by denying the second consequence, because the patient, as it is such, if it be altogether the same, cannot determine the action of the agent, nor more help or hinder the action of one agent than of another: but if it be diverse, or diversely disposed, then the determination, resistance, or like effect will be altogether necessary, or if it partake of some indifference, it will necessarily depend in some manner on a free active power. It is declared: for if the agent be natural and able to effect many effects by reason of a universal virtue, it cannot be determined to a certain effect about the same patient disposed in the same manner, unless the patient be capable of that alone. For if the patient be of itself indifferent to many, there is no cause why such an agent should be determined by it to one action rather than to another, as Scotus rightly noted above, § Against the conclusion. But if the patient be capable of one effect only, then it will indeed determine the action of the agent; but the determination will be altogether natural, or necessary, as is manifest of itself; and in the same manner if the patient be diversely disposed, it determineth indeed the action of such an agent, yet by natural necessity, as is plain in that example of the sun melting wax, or drying clay, for it doth both by natural necessity. And the reason is, because on the part of the recipient, as such, the determination of the action cometh not, save by reason of a natural incapacity, or of a limitation of capacity coming from some disposition, and therefore if that limitation or disposition be natural, the determination also is necessary, nor can it be free on the part of the patient. And the same is to be said of resistance, or hindrance, or the lack of it coming from the natural disposition of the recipient: for the same reason holdeth. But if the agent be free, then it can determine its own action, and consequently the passion also, about the same patient of itself indifferent and capable of many actions. And then indeed the determination is free, but it cometh from the eminence of the active power, and from the dominion which it hath over its action, nor can it come from elsewhere. Sometimes also some passion or reception can depend on a free disposition of the patient, and then, although the reception cannot be proximately free to the recipient itself, because it is not done by it, it can remotely be denominated free, in so far as it dependeth on its free disposition: but that liberty is placed in this, that the effecting of such a disposition is free, and so it always floweth from the active power in which it resideth; and in this manner the reception of the first sanctifying grace can be said to be remotely free to man, in so far as to effect contrition is free to man, and in natural things it can be said to be free to a man to be warmed by the sun, in so far as he can freely approach the sun, or withdraw from it. Proper liberty therefore is always in an active power, as it is active.

[^2]: Translator's note: the print has only *Quamvis sit in cujusque potestate quid possit* ("Although it be in every man's power what he can do"), as the Scribe found on the page image. Augustine's sentence is *Quamvis sit in cujusque potestate quid velit, non est tamen in cujusquam potestate quid possit* ("Although it be in every man's power what he willeth, yet it is not in any man's power what he can do"), and the argument needs its first member; so the words are translated by the sense.
