#### CHAPTER I. (continued)

<!-- p. 3 -->

**8.** *The free from necessity is wont to be said both of the faculty of working without necessity, and of the action itself.* — That therefore is called free in the present place which is free from necessity: and it is wont to be said both of the faculty of working without necessity, and of the action itself. In the former manner free will is so denominated, which is said to be a faculty of will and reason, namely for working with indifference and with dominion over the action, so that it is in its hand to will or not to will to exercise or to hold back an action. Of which Augustine said, Book II *Of the Merits and Remission of Sins*, chap. 18,[^1] that it is the choice of the will, which is freely bent hither and thither, and is among those natural goods which a man can use well and ill. And in this sense it is certain of faith that man is in this manner free by his own nature, or hath some

<!-- p. 4 -->

faculty free from necessity in his works, not only natural, but also supernatural, as the Council of Trent plainly defineth, sess. 6, chap. 5, and can. 5 and 6; and Augustine proveth at large by the Scriptures and by reasons in the book *Of Grace and Free Will*, and more at large the moderns who write of this matter, and we more briefly in Book I *Of the Helps*, from the beginning.[^2] But chiefly may be seen the learned disputations of Cardinal Bellarmine in the whole controversy *Of Grace and Free Will*, with the Preface.

**9.** *Whence an act is denominated free.* — Hence also the act which proceedeth from this free faculty is denominated free: but it behoveth that it proceed from it as it is indifferent. For there have not been wanting some modern Catholics who have denied that indifference, or the lack of necessity, is necessary for the liberty of an act, but have held that the lack of coaction is enough: but this opinion hath been condemned among the assertions of Michael Baius, assertions 30 and [?],[^3] and shall be refuted of set purpose in Book III.[^4] That an act therefore be truly free, it is not enough that it be voluntary, or not coerced; but it is required also that it be not necessary simply, and accordingly that it proceed from a free power, in such wise that this retain its indifference and liberty, and that in the very use and exercise it be suffered to work with its power free and entire, so that it be in its hand to choose between contraries or contradictories, or to work or not to work. Because, that an act be free, it is necessary that it proceed from a free faculty, as it is free; but it will not proceed from a free power, as it is such, unless this have its faculty unhindered as to either part, namely of working and of not working. For what will it profit to the liberty of such an act, that the power hath an innate indifference, if in the very use it be hindered? Wherefore, the distinction given of a twofold necessity being supposed, the one from within through the natural determination of the power to one thing, the other from without through the impulse of some extrinsic agent, the former is repugnant not only to the act, but also to the faculty of free will, and therefore it cannot be that a free power be subject to such a necessity in respect of the same object, because it involveth a contradiction, as is known of itself. But the latter necessity is not repugnant to a free faculty, as I now suppose, and shall prove a little after; yet it is repugnant to a free act, because, that it be such, it behoveth that it proceed from the power, as free, either as to specification, or at least as to exercise, according to the manner in which the act shall be free, because it hath not its being free save by denomination from its proximate principle. But if the act proceed from a power suffering necessity, whether through the intrinsic necessity of a power determined to one thing, or through an extrinsic necessity sent into a power of itself free, then the liberty of the act is hindered and taken away; so that it cannot be called free, nor worthy of praise or blame, because it proceedeth not from the power, as it is free, for the power itself is not able to remove that necessity, or to forestall it, and therefore it cannot be imputed to it that it worketh in such a manner, and not otherwise. Which doctrine the Council of Trent taught clearly enough in the place cited, and it is altogether necessary for saving all the things which Scripture teacheth concerning human actions and their liberty. And therefore I judge that on this point there is no dissension among Catholics, although in the manner of explaining and defending this liberty there may be some diversity.
