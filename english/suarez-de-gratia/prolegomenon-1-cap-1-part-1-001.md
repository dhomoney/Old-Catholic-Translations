## THE FIRST PROLEGOMENON.

### OF THE THINGS REQUIRED FOR FORMAL LIBERTY AND FREE USE.

#### CHAPTER I. OF THE NAMES OF NATURE AND OF FREE WILL.

<!-- p. 1 -->

**1.** Because a free nature is the foundation of grace, and grace is the perfection and health of nature, therefore the disputation concerning grace supposeth some knowledge of such a nature; for which cause grace and nature, or free will, are wont to be joined together, not only in the disputation itself, but even in the title of the whole work, as Augustine wrote one book *Of Nature and Grace*, and another *Of Grace and Free Will*. For this cause, therefore, it is necessary at the entrance of this matter to lay down beforehand what we understand by the name of nature and of free will. For the philosophers in the books of the *Physics* dispute of nature as it is the principle of motion and of rest; but in the present place it is not taken in that breadth, but as it is said by antonomasia of the intellectual or rational nature, which is the principle of moral operations, by which eternal beatitude is gained or lost, to which end the intellectual nature alone is ordained through grace; and therefore Augustine said, Book I *Against Julian*, chap. 3, that the rational nature alone is capable of grace. Now he speaketh of the rational as it comprehendeth the intellectual, because the angelic nature as well as the human is capable of divine grace, since each is free. But grace (as Bernard saith, *Of Grace and Free Will*) none can give but God, and none can receive but free will, that is, a free nature; for this hath need of it, that it may attain the supernatural end. But because human nature is both more familiar to us, and hath need of grace under more titles and in more ways than the angelic, therefore we shall always make our discourse of the rational nature; yet the doctrine may easily be applied with due proportion to the angelic; for where a reason of difference shall occur, it will not be hard to understand, and we shall take care to point it out. But we speak not now of human nature as it is distinguished from the supposit, nor of that more than human state which it obtained in the divine Word through the hypostatic union; for the former consideration of nature is metaphysical, and belongeth nothing to the present cause; and the consideration of the other mystery is a higher one, which we have pursued in its own place according to our strength. In the present place, therefore, human nature is considered in so far as, in a created person, it is capable of grace, and hath need of it for working well and for attaining its end.

**2.** Now it is further to be observed that grace perfecteth nature chiefly in so far as nature is the principle of human and free acts. Whence it cometh that free will and grace are so joined in relation and in office, that they cannot be separated in disputation, as Augustine sufficiently showed, saying: *If there is no grace of God, how doth Christ save the world; and if there is no free will, how doth he judge the world;* and Book III of the *Hypognosticon*, chap. 11: *Neither doth grace without free will make a man to have the blessed life, nor free will without grace;* and Bernard, *Of Grace and Free Will*: *Take away free will, there will be nothing to be saved: take away grace, there will be nothing whereby it may be saved.* Whence the chief difficulty of this matter is placed in reconciling the necessity and efficacy of grace with the liberty of the will, and ignorance of this concord hath been the root and origin of almost all the errors which have been in

<!-- p. 2 -->

this matter. It behoveth us therefore, before the signification of grace, to set down first what is signified by the name of liberty and of free will.

**3.** *Liberty is threefold, but that which is from necessity is the proper moral liberty.* — And first of all there must be set down the distinction of a threefold liberty, which Hugh of St. Victor delivereth thus, in the *Summa of the Sentences*, tract 3, chap. 9: *For there is* (saith he) *a threefold liberty, from necessity, from sin, from misery.* But we number those members otherwise; for there is liberty from servitude, from coaction, and from necessity, of which this third alone is the proper moral liberty, necessary for human acts worthy of praise and blame, of reward or punishment: whence that alone simply deserveth the name of liberty; for the rest are called liberties in so far as they are opposed to some necessity. For servitude bringeth in a certain necessity of obeying, and therefore the lack of servitude is called liberty, *Rom.* viii: *It shall be delivered from the servitude of corruption.* Now servitude can be either of sin or of punishment: and so the lack of fault and the remission of punishment can be called a certain liberty from sin, or from the servitude of sin, according to that of *Rom.* vii:[^1] *When you were the servants of sin, you were free men to justice; but now being made free from sin, and become servants to God, you have your fruit unto sanctification;* and II *Cor.* iii: *Where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is liberty.* Whence also Augustine, Book IV of the *City of God*, chap. 2:[^2] *The good man* (saith he), *although he serve,* namely a man, *is free,* that is, from sin; *but the evil man, although he reign, is a slave,* to wit, of sin. And in the same manner, in the fourteenth book of the *City of God*, the fifteenth chapter, he said that the first man by sinning lost the liberty which he coveted. For he had not coveted liberty from necessity, since he had that from the beginning, and so neither did he lose it; but he desired liberty from subjection, and this he lost, because he was made the slave both of sin, and of punishment, and of misery, and contracted the disorder of concupiscence, and the combat of the *fomes*,[^3] which is a certain penal servitude. And to this signification is reduced every lack of obligation or of debt, whether it proceed from law or from any other cause whatsoever: for thus a dispensation or exemption from a law is accounted a certain liberty; whence also a privilege is wont to be called by the name of a liberty; and he to whom some debt is remitted is said to be freed from it. But if any man consider rightly, all this liberty supposeth the proper liberty from necessity, because none is capable of the proper servitude of fault or of punishment, of precept or of obligation, save a person free in acting; and therefore, as a proper privation supposeth an aptitude, so servitude and obligation suppose a person apt to work with indifference, and without necessity. But because operations free from necessity can be under the right of another, or under some burden and obligation, therefore together with liberty from necessity there can be in works a necessity of servitude and of obligation; or (which is the same) a man can by law or by servitude be deprived of the liberty contrary to these,[^4] and nevertheless retain the proper liberty of his works.

**4.** *The coerced is almost the same as the violent.* — The coerced is almost the same as the violent, for each is against the inward appetite of him who suffereth or worketh; but the violent is said more generally of any motion whatsoever contrary to appetite, whether elicited or innate, whereas the coerced is properly said when it is repugnant to the elicited and vital appetite, although sometimes those words are wont to be confounded. Two things therefore are required for the coerced, namely, that it be done or borne of necessity, and that it be against the inward affection; and so coaction is a certain necessity, and addeth something beyond it. Both are gathered from Aristotle, Book III of the *Ethics*, chap. 1, who saith that the violent is that which is from without, the patient contributing no force, that is, resisting in some manner; as all expound from the same Aristotle, Book II of the *Eudemian Ethics*, chap. 8; because if the patient resist not, the motion will not be violent, although it proceed from without. The coerced therefore, as such, cannot be from within, otherwise it would not be against a thing's own inclination; but if it is from without, by that very fact it is necessary; because liberty from necessity is not in suffering, but in acting, as I shall prove below: for this cause therefore the coerced includeth the necessary, and that which is not free from coaction, as such, cannot be exempt from necessity. But by reason of the other conditions the coerced is not convertible with the necessary, for many things are necessary which are not against the inward appetite, nay, often spring from it. And for the same reason the liberty which excludeth necessity is more universal than the liberty opposed to coaction alone; for all liberty from necessity is also exempt from coaction, but not conversely, as is known of itself; for the love of God in the heavenly country is free from coaction, yet not from necessity.

**5.** Liberty therefore from coaction alone requireth only the lack of violence, so that the motion which is thus called free be not against the inward appetite,

<!-- p. 3 -->

whether it be done of necessity or not. But such a motion, if it be of the will, is to be called voluntary rather than free. For these two properties are distinct in the acts of the will, and therefore they are to be distinguished by names also, lest the ambiguity of words render the disputation uncertain. There can therefore be an act of man that is voluntary, and consequently free from coaction, which nevertheless is not simply free from necessity, as may be seen in the love wherewith God loveth himself, and wherewith he produceth the Holy Ghost, and wherewith the blessed love God, and in the indeliberate acts of the will, and in the affections of the sensitive appetite. And the reason is sufficiently plain from what hath been said, with those things added which Aristotle and St. Thomas have delivered concerning the nature of the voluntary. For that is called voluntary which is from an inward principle with knowledge; and this whole nature can be found in an act, although it be done of necessity, because necessity alone excludeth not knowledge, nor conformity with the appetite, innate or elicited. Whence also arose that common doctrine of the theologians, that force or coaction cannot be brought upon the will in its elicited acts, although it can suffer necessity. For coaction excludeth the voluntary, since it is directly opposed to it; but an act cannot be from the will and not be voluntary, because it cannot but be from an intrinsic principle with knowledge; nor can it be at once voluntary and coerced, because these two are opposed and involve an immediate contradiction; but necessity involveth not that opposition with the voluntary, because necessity itself can be voluntary, or conformable to the inward appetite, as hath been said.

**6.** *Two things to be observed as to the manner of speaking, necessary for understanding the Fathers. — The first.* — Now two things are here to be observed, as to the manner of speaking, because they will be necessary for understanding the sayings of the Fathers. One is that coaction is twofold: one simply so, which bringeth an absolute and unavoidable necessity against the inward affection; the other in a certain respect, such as is that which is wrought by punishments or fears, which induceth not an absolute necessity, but one in a certain respect, namely, for the avoiding of such a disadvantage. The former coaction therefore is that which is altogether repugnant to an elicited act of the will, because, by the very fact that it is elicited, it is no longer coerced; but the latter coaction can stand with an absolute will, nay, even with liberty, since it induceth no absolute necessity; and therefore, although it is sometimes called coaction or violence, as appeareth from Augustine, Book I *Against Gaudentius*, chap. 25, and epistle 48,[^5] it is not coaction simply and absolutely, but the restraining from some evil, as the same Augustine said, Book II *Against the Letters of Petilian*, chap. 83.

**7.** *The second thing to be observed.* — The other thing to be noted is that sometimes a voluntary act is so necessary simply, that the necessity itself is from within, and consequently is conformable to the natural inclination and perfection of the will itself; and then the act, although it be necessary, is so voluntary that it can in no way be called violent or coerced, because it is in no way repugnant to the inward appetite: neither to the elicited, because the act, as is supposed, is voluntary; nor to the innate, because the necessity itself also is not contrary to the will or to its innate inclination, as is supposed. And in this manner the love of God in the blessed is necessary without any kind of coaction or violence, and the affections of the sensitive appetite can be placed in the same order for the same reason. But sometimes a voluntary act can be necessary only from an extrinsic agent that effecteth it, or as it were impelleth; and then, although the act be voluntary, and therefore cannot be called absolutely coerced, nevertheless, since the necessity itself is not conformable to the natural condition and inclination of the will, because it is only from without, an act thus necessary is sometimes wont to be called in some manner violent, at least in a certain respect, because it is against the connatural manner, and against a certain innate appetite. And in this manner a necessity sent into the will in acts that are of themselves free is wont to be called by the Fathers a certain coaction; and conversely an act simply free is wont to be called spontaneous and voluntary, that is to say, perfectly so, and as excluding an extrinsic necessity repugnant to the inward appetite or inclination of the will.
