BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XI.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*Unto the end; for the octave, a psalm for David.*[^1]

Of this title it hath been spoken in *Ps.* vi. The argument of this Psalm is, that we must trust in God, and not in men, because the words of men are for the most part deceitful, but those of God always most true and most faithful.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. SAVE ME, O LORD, FOR THERE IS NOW NO SAINT: TRUTHS ARE DECAYED FROM AMONG THE CHILDREN OF MEN.**

Do thou, O Lord, save me from all dangers, for there is no other in whom I can trust: for there is now no saint, that is, scarcely is there found in the world any man truly pious, and merciful (for that is what the Hebrew word חסיד properly soundeth); nor is there only now no merciful man, but also no truthful man: for truths are decayed from among the children of men, that is, scarcely is there found one who simply speaketh the truth. And so mercy and truth have almost perished from the earth. In the Hebrew it is אמונים *emunim*, which St. Jerome rendered *fideles* (the faithful), so that this is the sentence: the faithful are diminished from among the children of men; but the same word signifieth also truth, or truths, and the sense is the same.

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. THEY HAVE SPOKEN VAIN THINGS EVERY ONE TO HIS NEIGHBOUR: WITH DECEITFUL LIPS, AND WITH A DOUBLE HEART HAVE THEY SPOKEN.**

He proveth that there is now no saint, that is, no merciful man, because men, for the most part, do not speak to their neighbours things useful and good, but vain things, which cannot deliver from dangers; whence also they speak to no purpose, which is how St. Jerome rendered it. He proveth also that truth hath failed, because *deceitful lips*, that is, men who can be called deceitful lips, have spoken *in corde et corde* (in a heart and a heart),[^2] that is, with a double heart, willing to say one thing, and willing to do another, and so to deceive. In the Hebrew it is word for word *labium divisionum* (a lip of divisions), that is, a divided lip, sounding one thing, signifying another; and a divided, or double, lip ariseth from a divided, or double, heart. But because things which are made smooth are divided, therefore the same word signifieth in the Scriptures *labium blanditiarum* (a lip of flatteries), and *adulationum* (of fawnings); and because all flattery deceiveth, therefore both the Seventy and St. Jerome rightly rendered *labia dolosa* (deceitful lips), or *subdola* (crafty). In the Greek there is added *mala* (evil things), *in corde et corde locuti sunt mala* (with a double heart have they spoken evil things), but that word is neither in the Hebrew, nor in the more correct Latin copies.

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. MAY THE LORD DESTROY ALL DECEITFUL LIPS, AND THE TONGUE THAT SPEAKETH PROUD THINGS.**

It is a prophetic imprecation: for by way of imprecation he foretelleth that it shall come to pass, that all who deceive shall be deceived, and while they think that they gain much by their frauds, shall lose all things, and shall themselves also perish for ever. That phrase, *and the tongue that speaketh proud things*, signifieth the tongue that boasteth itself on account of its deceits and frauds, as is plain from the verse following. In the Hebrew and the Greek the conjunction *et* (and) is not found; from which we understand that here there are not reproved two

<!-- p. 64 (PDF 75) -->

kinds of men, the deceitful and the proud, but the deceitful only, who nevertheless trust in their deceits, and therefore make much of their deceitful lips, and speak magnificently of them. Therefore the Latin edition, which hath the conjunction, is to be brought back to the same sense, and is conveniently brought back, as we have explained.

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. WHO HAVE SAID: WE WILL MAGNIFY OUR TONGUE; OUR LIPS ARE OUR OWN; WHO IS LORD OVER US?**

He explaineth why he joined the tongue that speaketh proud things with the deceitful lips, namely, because the deceitful trust most of all in their tongue, so that they believe they need no other thing, and ought not to be subject to any lord. "Who have said," saith he, namely with themselves in their own mind, "we will magnify our tongue," that is, let us magnify our tongue, ascribing to it all our goods, which we gain by it. From the Hebrew some render *linguam nostram roborabimus* (we will strengthen our tongue); for the verb גבר *gabar* signifieth to be *weighty* and *strong*, but it is taken also for any greatness whatsoever, and therefore נגביר *nagbir*, which is found in this place, is rightly rendered *magnificabimus* (we will magnify); for among the Latins also a weighty man is referred not only to the weight of bulk, but also to the greatness of virtue. *Labia nostra a nobis sunt* (our lips are from us)[^3] is ambiguous in the Latin, but from the Hebrew and the Greek it is certain that the sense is: our lips are with us, they make for us, they fight on our side. And so the Prophet goeth on to explain the confidence which the deceitful have in their lips, as in the best instrument for prevailing over other men. Therefore he addeth that they say: "Who is Lord over us?" as if he said: We acknowledge no superior, since by the instrument of the tongue we subject all others.

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. BY REASON OF THE MISERY OF THE NEEDY, AND THE GROANS OF THE POOR, NOW WILL I ARISE, SAITH THE LORD.**

The Prophet hath taught that we must not trust in men: now he teacheth that we must trust in God, whose promises are most faithful; and he teacheth this poetically, by bringing in God himself speaking, and promising his help to the humble and afflicted. "By reason of the misery," saith he, "of the needy," who are deceived and afflicted by the deceitful, whence also they must needs groan; I will not put off my help, but "now will I arise," as from sleep, and will be present with them.

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. I WILL SET HIM IN SAFETY; I WILL DEAL CONFIDENTLY IN HIS REGARD.**

He explaineth what he will do when he hath arisen: "I will set," saith he, "in safety," that is, I will place them in safety, I will establish them in safety, that they may always stand safe. "I will deal confidently in his regard" (*in eo*),[^4] that is, no man shall hinder me, I will deal boldly and freely in this matter. The Greek word παῤῥησιάσομαι expresseth in this place confidence and freedom, and boldness. From the Hebrew St. Jerome rendered: *Ponam in salutari auxilium eorum* (I will set their help in safety). But I know not what he followed in this matter; for the Hebrew word יפיח לו *iapiach lo* can signify *he shall blow upon him*, or *he shall ensnare him*, or *he shall breathe favourably upon him*. But the interpreters seem to have read in the first person אפיח לו *aphiach lo*, *I will blow upon him*, or *I will breathe favourably upon him*, that is, upon every one of the poor I will breathe and show favour; and because "the Spirit breatheth where he will, and where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is liberty," therefore perhaps they rendered, "I will deal confidently," that is, I will freely breathe and show favour.

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. THE WORDS OF THE LORD ARE PURE WORDS: AS SILVER TRIED BY THE FIRE, PURGED FROM THE EARTH REFINED SEVEN TIMES.**

The Prophet now teacheth that the promise of the Lord, set forth in the two verses above, is not like the promises of deceitful men, but is most true and most faithful. "The words of the Lord," saith he, "are pure" (*casta*, chaste), that is, clean and pure, as the Hebrew word soundeth, not painted, not feigned, sincere, faithful. "Silver tried by the fire," that is, the words, or promises, of God are as silver most true in sound, weight and whiteness, such as is silver proved in the fire, and not only proved in the fire, but also *probatum terræ* (proved to the earth),[^5] that is, to the peoples of the earth, namely approved by men skilled in metals; lastly "refined seven times," that is, not once, but oftentimes purged from dross by melting (as the Hebrew word expresseth). In the Hebrew that phrase, *purgatum terræ* (purged of the earth),[^6] is very obscure; St. Jerome rendered it *separatum a terra* (separated from the earth); others, *a Domino terræ* (by the Lord of the earth), namely *refined seven times*; others, *in vase sive in camino* (in a vessel, or in a furnace); but since the word בעליל is found only in this place, there is no reason why we should not rest in the interpretation of the Seventy Interpreters, to whom the Hebrew tongue was most thoroughly known. For whatsoever others may say, it will be divination rather than interpretation.

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. THOU, O LORD, WILT PRESERVE US: AND KEEP US FROM THIS GENERATION FOR EVER.**

He gathereth from what hath been said that God will do what he hath promised: Thou, saith he, according to thy promises, "wilt preserve us," that is, wilt keep us; for here there is no word of saving, or of salvation, neither in the Greek nor in the Hebrew; but simply of preserving and keeping. In the Hebrew it is תשמרם, *servabis ea* (thou wilt preserve them), namely thy words, and wilt keep us; but the Seventy read תשמרנו *thishmerennu*, *servabis nos* (thou wilt preserve us), and the change was most easy, because there is the greatest likeness between ם, which signifieth *them*, and נו, which signifieth *us*; but both senses are very good.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. THE WICKED WALK ROUND ABOUT: ACCORDING TO THY HIGHNESS, THOU HAST MULTIPLIED THE CHILDREN OF MEN.**

As if some one asked: What shall become of the wicked, while thou wilt preserve us? He answereth: the wicked meanwhile, while we are at rest under thy wings, walk round

<!-- p. 65 (PDF 76) -->

about, ever going about after the goods of this world, and never coming to the end of their desires; and there will always be wicked men going about, while this world lasteth; because "according to thy highness, thou hast multiplied the children of men," and "the number of fools is infinite," *Eccles.* i. At the end of this Psalm all the editions seem to disagree among themselves. The Hebrew hath word for word: *In circuitu impii ambulabunt, dum exaltantur vilitates filiorum hominum* (The wicked shall walk round about, while the vilenesses of the sons of men are exalted), that is, while the vilest men are exalted and reign, then the wicked run about everywhere. But the Greek hath thus: *In circuitu impii ambulant, secundum altitudinem tuam sollicitudinem gessisti de filiis hominum* (The wicked walk round about; according to thy highness thou hast had care of the children of men). But the Greek and the Latin are easily reconciled; for God, according to his highness, hath had the greatest care of the human kind, and therefore, lest it should perish, he hath multiplied it above all other kinds; hence the Latin Interpreter gave the sense, when he said *multiplicasti* (thou hast multiplied). And because in a great multitude perfection belongeth to few, therefore "the number of fools," as we have cited from Solomon, "is infinite," and therefore the wicked are always and everywhere found walking round about. The Hebrew and the Greek do not seem able to be reconciled, unless the Seventy read otherwise; and perhaps it is not improbable that they read דלות *dallotha*, or דלית *dillitha*, not זלות, as we now have it: for by the change of a single letter there is made another word, which signifieth *exaltare* (to exalt), or *suscipere* (to uphold), as the Seventy rendered in the first verse of Psalm xxix: "I will extol thee, O Lord, for thou hast upheld me." Yet the sense is always true, and one reading doth not contradict the other, although they say different things.

[^1]: Translator's note: Bellarmine does not number the title, and divides the Psalm into nine verses. The Douay-Rheims Bible counts the title as verse 1 and also has nine: its verses 2, 3, 4 and 5 are his 1, 2, 3 and 4; its verse 6 holds his 5 and 6; and its 7, 8 and 9 are his 7, 8 and 9.

[^2]: Translator's note: the Douay-Rheims has "with a double heart" for the Vulgate's *in corde et corde*, which is the very explanation Bellarmine gives; the Latin words are therefore kept here.

[^3]: Translator's note: the Douay-Rheims has "our lips are our own". Bellarmine speaks of the ambiguity of the Latin words, so they are kept.

[^4]: Translator's note: the Douay-Rheims takes *in eo* of the poor man ("in his regard"). Bellarmine takes it of the thing ("in this matter").

[^5]: Translator's note: the Douay-Rheims has "purged from the earth" for the Vulgate's *probatum terræ*. Bellarmine takes the words otherwise, as "proved to the earth", so the Latin is kept in the commentary.

[^6]: Translator's note: so the print, *purgatum terræ*, where the verse has *probatum terræ*.
