BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM X.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*Unto the end. A psalm for David.*[^1]

Of the title it hath been spoken in the foregoing Psalms. The argument of this Psalm is an exhortation to confidence in the time of calumnies, and of other persecutions. David speaketh in the person of any just man whatsoever, foreseeing in a manner that word of the Apostle: "All that will live godly shall suffer persecution," II *Tim.* iii.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. IN THE LORD I PUT MY TRUST: HOW THEN DO YOU SAY TO MY SOUL: GET THEE AWAY FROM HENCE TO THE MOUNTAIN LIKE A SPARROW?**

It is the voice of the just man, who is assailed by the temptation of despair, that he should forsake his calling on account of the multitude of calumniators: "In the Lord," saith he, "I put my trust," who is everywhere, and is almighty; "how then do you say to my soul," that is, why do you endeavour to persuade me. For we have already said above that it is a Hebrew phrase, *animæ meæ* (to my soul), for *mihi* (to me). Now he speaketh either to the devils that tempt him, or to the inward suggestions themselves, that is, the thoughts sent in by the devil. "Get thee away from hence to the mountain like a sparrow," that is, forsake thy calling, and the company

<!-- p. 60 (PDF 71) -->

[Margin: There is no place where there are not dangers and temptations.]

of men, and go away to a place where there are no dangers, no temptations. For sparrows, when they fear the fowlers, fly to the tops of the mountains, to which the fowlers cannot climb. Such mountains, in respect of temptations, there are none, save in the imagination of men, who, when they are grievously tempted, imagine that by a change of place that trouble can be avoided; and they who despair choose even violent death itself, to which they flee as to a mountain. But the just man is constant, and forsaketh not his place, knowing that the temptations of the devil are everywhere, and that the help of God is everywhere. In the Hebrew it is: *Transmigra in montem vestrum, passer* (Get thee away to your mountain, O sparrow); but the word *chem*, which signifieth *your*, the Seventy read *cha*, which is *like*; and this without doubt is the truer and clearer reading. For St. Jerome also so rendered it. Therefore after the times of St. Jerome this place seemeth to have been corrupted in the Hebrew, by the fault of the copyists.

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. FOR, LO, THE WICKED HAVE BENT THEIR BOW; THEY HAVE PREPARED THEIR ARROWS IN THE QUIVER; TO SHOOT IN THE DARK THE UPRIGHT OF HEART.**

This is the reason why one should flee to the mountain, that is, why the calling should be forsaken through the excessive fear which the temptation suggesteth, namely, because evil men continually persecute the just, whether by calumnies, or in some other way. Now he compareth calumnies, or other persecutions, to arrows which are shot in the dark, to signify that those calumnies, or persecutions, not only strike grievously, but also can scarcely be guarded against. In the Hebrew it is: *Paraverunt sagittas suas in nervo* (They have prepared their arrows upon the string); and according to this reading, *intenderunt arcum* (they have bent their bow) and *paraverunt sagittas in nervo* (they have prepared their arrows upon the string) are the same thing. But the Seventy rendered: *Paraverunt sagittas in pharetra* (They have prepared their arrows in the quiver), to signify that the sinners have not only bent the bow, to shoot an arrow once, but have also filled the quiver with arrows, that they might be able to shoot the oftener. And although the Hebrew word *iether* signifieth a string and a cord, and no place is found in the dictionaries where it is put for a quiver, yet the authority of the Seventy Interpreters is enough for us to believe that it can be taken for a quiver also. Where the Latin copy hath *in obscuro* (in the dark), and the Hebrew אפל *ophel*, *in darkness*, in the Greek it is *in obscura luna* (in a dark moon); but in the thing itself there is no difference. For a dark moon signifieth a night that lacketh the brightness of the moon, whether the moon be covered with clouds, or be in conjunction with the sun, or near to the sun. These two verses could also have this sense: "In the Lord I put my trust: how then do you say to my soul: Get thee away from hence to the mountain like a sparrow? For, lo, the wicked have bent their bow; they have prepared their arrows in the quiver; to shoot in the dark the upright of heart." That is, it is not now permitted to be upright of heart, since on every side snares are laid for the upright of heart: therefore either one must withdraw to an inaccessible mountain apart from men, which cannot be done; or one must yield to custom, and forsake the way of justice. But to this temptation the just man answereth: "In the Lord I put my trust," and therefore neither will I go away to an uninhabitable mountain, nor will I forsake the way of justice.

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. FOR THEY HAVE DESTROYED THE THINGS WHICH THOU HAST MADE: BUT WHAT HAS THE JUST MAN DONE?**

The Prophet confirmeth, by an apostrophe to God, that it is true that sinners unjustly persecute the just. As if he said: It is true, what I have heard of the impiety of sinners. For sinners "have destroyed the things which thou hast made" (*quæ perfecisti*, which thou hast perfected),[^2] that is, thy most perfect laws, and also thy counsels, and all thine ordinances, which thou hast set among thy people, in that they not only do not render good for evil, as thou commandest, but render evil for good, calumniating and persecuting the just without any cause. "For what has the just man done?" that is, the just man hath given them no occasion, but they hate the just without cause. The Hebrew word which the Seventy rendered *perfecisti* (thou hast perfected) is very obscure. St. Jerome rendered it *leges* (laws), others *fundamenta* (foundations), others *retia* (nets). To me it seemeth likely that the Seventy Interpreters did not read, as we now have it in the Hebrew, השתות *hashathoth*, which hath the form of a plural noun, but read *hashatthotha*, which signifieth *thou hast set*, from the verb שתת in the conjugation *hiphil*; for the letters are altogether the same, and only one point is added under the last letter. For we commonly observe this in the Psalms, that the Seventy read somewhat otherwise than they read who added the points.

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. THE LORD IS IN HIS HOLY TEMPLE, THE LORD'S THRONE IS IN HEAVEN.**

He beginneth to give the reason why he said that he trusteth in the Lord, and feareth not the snares of men of iniquity: namely, because the Lord of all sitteth in heaven as judge, whence he seeth all things, and hath all men subject to him: "The Lord," saith he, "is in his holy temple," understand, *sitteth*. Now he calleth the holy temple of God the highest heaven, which is a temple not made with hands. He signifieth the same in clearer words, when he saith: "The Lord's throne is in heaven," that is, the Lord hath in heaven a seat, or throne. Now it is a Hebrew phrase, *Dominus in cœlo sedes ejus* (The Lord, in heaven his seat), for: the seat of the Lord is in heaven, or: the Lord hath his seat in heaven.

[Margin: The holy temple of God is heaven.]

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. HIS EYES LOOK ON THE POOR MAN: HIS EYELIDS EXAMINE THE SONS OF MEN.**

From that most high throne, from which nothing can be hidden, God looketh on the poor: therefore no evil can befall men without God knowing it, or without his permitting it, which is the greatest consolation of the just. That which followeth declareth further the providence of God. For God not only seeth men, but in seeing he discerneth, and judgeth between the good and the evil, and all their works. In the Hebrew there is not *in pauperem* (on the poor man), but simply, *oculi ejus*

<!-- p. 61 (PDF 72) -->

*respiciunt* (his eyes look); but the Seventy added *in pauperem* (on the poor man), for the sake of explanation. Moreover, for *interrogant* (they question)[^3] the Hebrew hath *probant* (they try), but the meaning is the same. For God is said by a metaphor to question, or to try, that is, to prove, or to examine, when he perfectly discerneth and judgeth, which men oftentimes cannot do, save by questioning and examining. That phrase, *palpebræ ejus interrogant* (his eyelids question), signifieth nothing else but that his eyes perfectly discern: for since the Psalms are written poetically, they therefore abound in many figures. And so the eyelids are put for the eyes, and the eyes for the mind; and to question, for to know exactly, as if he had first most exactly questioned and examined.

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. THE LORD TRIETH THE JUST AND THE WICKED: BUT HE THAT LOVETH INIQUITY HATETH HIS OWN SOUL.**

God not only knoweth exactly the just and the wicked, but also rewardeth or punisheth them according to their deserts. And therefore "he that loveth iniquity hateth his own soul," that is, himself, because on account of iniquity he is most grievously punished. From the Hebrew it can also be rendered, *diligentem iniquitatem odit anima ejus* (him that loveth iniquity his soul hateth), that is, God hateth those who love iniquity, and so St. Jerome rendered it. Both senses are true, and the Hebrew words admit of both. It is a wonderful sentence, and a most elegant one: "He that loveth iniquity hateth his own soul." For he that loveth iniquity seemeth to love his own soul, that is, himself, because he sinneth in order to satisfy himself, and yet in truth he hateth his own soul, and destroyeth it. This is what the Lord saith, *John* xii, 25: "He that loveth his life (*animam suam*, his soul) shall lose it," that is, he that loveth himself wrongly, truly hateth himself.

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. HE SHALL RAIN SNARES UPON SINNERS: FIRE AND BRIMSTONE AND STORMS OF WINDS SHALL BE THE PORTION OF THEIR CUP.**

This is the reason why men of iniquity hate their own souls, because the Lord shall rain, or, as it is more expressly read in the Hebrew, *pluere faciet super peccatores laqueos* (he shall make it rain snares upon sinners), that is, in this life he shall send snares in the greatest abundance, like drops of rain, upon sinners, that is, he shall suffer them to be entangled daily in more and greater sins, striking them with blindness, and delivering them up to a reprobate sense, which is a dreadful and most grievous punishment; then in the other life, "fire and brimstone and storms of winds," that is, most cold and most violent winds in hell, shall be the portion of the cup, that is, their portion and inheritance. It is to be observed in this place that by the name of the cup is understood the inheritance, as also often elsewhere in Scripture, as in that place: "The Lord is the portion of my inheritance and of my cup," where the same thing is understood to be said by two words, and therefore he addeth: "It is thou that wilt restore my inheritance to me." Now the inheritance is called a cup, because, as the cup at table, at least at the paschal supper, was divided among the guests; whence is that word of *Luke* xxii, 17: "Take, and divide it among you": so the inheritance is divided among the sons of the same father.

[Margin: The cup in Scripture is taken for the inheritance.]

The same inheritance is also sometimes called *pars* (part), or *portio* (portion), as: *Pars mea Dominus* (The Lord is my part); and elsewhere: *Portio mea Dominus* (The Lord is my portion); sometimes *pars hæreditatis* (the portion of the inheritance), as in the place cited, *Dominus pars hæreditatis meæ* (The Lord is the portion of my inheritance): where it is not signified that the Lord is a part of the inheritance, so that another part is to be sought elsewhere, but it is signified that the Lord is the part which hath fallen to him from the division of the inheritance, so that the inheritance, and the portion of the inheritance, are the same thing: so also the cup, and the portion of the cup, are the same thing, that is, that portion which is due to one from the division of the cup. And fitly to the sons of the devil, to whom the Lord saith, *John* viii, 44: "You are of your father the devil," there is assigned the inheritance which the devil possesseth, that is, the horrible punishment signified by the fire, and brimstone, and storms of winds.

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. FOR THE LORD IS JUST, AND HATH LOVED JUSTICE: HIS COUNTENANCE HATH BEHELD RIGHTEOUSNESS.**

This is the true reason why God so severely punisheth the wicked, because he is just in himself: "For God is light, and in him there is no darkness," I *John* i; "and he loveth justice" (*justitias*, justices, in the plural), that is, just works in all others, whom he created to his own image. He repeateth the very same thing, saying: "His countenance hath beheld righteousness." For by *æquitatem* (righteousness) in this place is understood uprightness, as the words sound, the Hebrew ישר and the Greek ὀρθότης; and uprightness is as it were a declaration of justice. For he doth not here treat of the justice which is one of the four cardinal virtues, which is a particular justice setting the mean in the dealings of men; but of universal justice, which embraceth all the virtues, and is a certain uprightness of the whole man, of which the charity of God and of our neighbour is as it were the summary; for "the end of the commandment is charity," I *Tim.* i; and: "He that loveth, hath fulfilled the law," *Rom.* xiii, 8. Now that phrase, *vidit vultus ejus* (his countenance hath beheld), doth not signify a simple seeing, but a seeing of approval, according to that word of the first Psalm: "The Lord knoweth the way of the just." It is therefore the same thing, he hath loved justice, and he hath beheld righteousness, that is, "his countenance," that is, his eye, hath approved uprightness.

[^1]: Translator's note: Bellarmine does not number the title, and divides the Psalm into eight verses. The Douay-Rheims Bible counts the title as verse 1 and also has eight: its verses 2, 3 and 4 are his 1, 2 and 3; its verse 5 holds his 4 and 5; and its 6, 7 and 8 are his 6, 7 and 8.

[^2]: Translator's note: the Douay-Rheims has "which thou hast made" for the Vulgate's *quæ perfecisti*. Bellarmine's comment turns on the Latin word, which means "which thou hast perfected", so the Latin is given beside the Douay-Rheims wording.

[^3]: Translator's note: the Vulgate has the same verb in verses 5 and 6 (*interrogant*, *interrogat*). The Douay-Rheims renders it "examine" in the first and "trieth" in the second. Bellarmine's comment turns on its first meaning, "to question", so it is so rendered in the commentary.
