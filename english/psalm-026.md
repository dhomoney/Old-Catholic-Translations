BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XXVI.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*The Psalm of David before he was anointed.*

[Margin: The anointing of David was twofold.]

In the Hebrew there is nothing for a title but *David*; the rest was added by the Seventy, that we might understand that this Psalm was composed by David before he began to reign, namely while the persecution of Saul lasted. For although David had been anointed with oil by Samuel, when he was yet a boy; yet that anointing was private, not public, and was used rather to mark out, than to consecrate a king. But afterwards, Saul being slain, David was anointed king publicly and solemnly, as is plain from Book II of *Kings*, chap. ii. And of this later anointing the title of this Psalm is to be understood. Now this Psalm seemeth to have been written next after the one above, David being still in exile and tribulation; but because after the prayer of the preceding Psalm he had been filled with great consolation of spirit, and had known by God's revealing that he was called to a certain greater kingdom than earthly ones are; he began to sigh with unspeakable desire for the heavenly kingdom, and to despise all temporal things, whether adverse or prosperous. The same Psalm befitteth all the elect, who are already anointed with the anointing of grace, and marked out for the heavenly kingdom, and sigh with the whole devotion of their mind for the anointing of glory, by which they truly begin to reign.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. THE LORD IS MY LIGHT AND MY SALVATION, WHOM SHALL I FEAR?**[^1]

Tribulation bringeth darkness, prosperity light and clear weather: for tribulation troubleth and confoundeth the mind, so that it seeth not easily what needeth to be done, and thence it is provoked to impatience, or to some other sin. But if God scatter the darkness by a light sent in from heaven, straightway the mind perceiveth that the tribulation is momentary and light, which in the shadow of night struck great horror into it, and at the same time perceiveth that, God protecting it, the tribulation not only cannot hurt, but will even wonderfully profit. David, having experienced this benefit of God, singeth in his own person and that of all the elect: "The Lord is my light and my salvation, whom shall I fear?" as if he said: Ignorance and infirmity made me fearful in tribulation, but since the Lord hath now enlightened my mind, and hath made me to see plainly that no temporal calamity is grievous, or lasting, and hath healed my infirmity with the ointment of divine love, I fear no man: for truth casteth out darkness, and "charity casteth out fear," as Blessed John saith, I *John* iv.

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. THE LORD IS THE PROTECTOR OF MY LIFE: OF WHOM SHALL I BE AFRAID?**

He addeth another argument, by which he persuadeth himself that no fear is henceforth to be admitted. The Lord, saith he, not only enlighteneth and healeth, but forsaketh not him that is enlightened and healed, but protecteth him continually with the shield of his providence and benevolence. "Of whom" therefore "shall I be afraid?" "If God be for us, who is against us?" as the Apostle saith, *Rom.* viii; if a king, guarded by a band of mortal soldiers, feareth not, why should the servant of God fear, guarded by the protection of the almighty and immortal Emperor? St. Martin, in Sulpitius: "I," saith he, "protected by the sign of the cross, not by shield, or helmet, will pierce the ranks of the enemies in safety"; he was, to wit, of those who could say: "The Lord is the protector of my life: of whom shall I be afraid?"

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. WHILST THE WICKED DRAW NEAR AGAINST ME, TO EAT MY FLESH.**

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. MY ENEMIES THAT TROUBLE ME, HAVE THEMSELVES BEEN WEAKENED, AND HAVE FALLEN.**

He describeth the effect of the protection of God, and putteth the past for the future, after the manner of the prophets, to mark the certainty of the things. The sense therefore is: By the protection of God it shall come to pass, that when they shall draw near against me, as one prostrate and cast down, who desire to hurt me, and as dogs, or hungry lions long to be filled with my flesh; then these very men who trouble me, and are my enemies, shall be made weak, and shall fall, and so by their endeavour they shall hurt not me, but themselves. That this is most true, the examples not only of David, but of Christ and the martyrs, and indeed of all the saints show. In the Hebrew and Greek there is, *tribulantes me, et inimici mei* (they that trouble me, and my enemies); the Latin Interpreter leaveth out that *et* (and), but the matter is of small moment. For *infirmati sunt* (have been weakened), St. Jerome rendereth *impegerunt* (have stumbled); but the Hebrew word כשלו signifieth both.

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. IF ARMIES IN CAMP SHOULD STAND TOGETHER AGAINST ME, MY HEART SHALL NOT FEAR.**

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. IF A BATTLE SHOULD RISE UP AGAINST ME, IN THIS WILL I BE CONFIDENT.**

He addeth to the things he had said, that he may show that he hath the fullest hope and confidence in God, that he feareth not only not single enemies, but not even whole camps, and those not resting in their tents,

<!-- p. 152 (PDF 163) -->

but fighting in battle array. That phrase, *in hoc ego sperabo* (in this will I be confident), is variously expounded, because both in the Hebrew זאת *zoth*, and in the Greek ταύτῃ, the word is of the feminine gender. St. Augustine expoundeth, *in this petition*; Theodoret, *in this hope*; Euthymius, *in this my heart*. In Greek καρδία is of the feminine gender, but the Latin Interpreter seemeth to have rendered most rightly, *in hoc ego sperabo*. For he considered that the Hebrews lack the neuter gender, and use the feminine gender in its place: and therefore that זאת *zoth* doth not signify, in *this hope*, in *this petition*, or in *this heart*, but absolutely, *in this*, that is, in this thing, namely, that God is my light, and salvation, and protection, as was said above. And so the Greeks set ταύτῃ, not because they lack the neuter gender, but because they would render word for word what was in the Hebrew; but the Latin looked not so much to the word as[^2] to the signification.

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. ONE THING I HAVE ASKED OF THE LORD, THIS WILL I SEEK AFTER; THAT I MAY DWELL IN THE HOUSE OF THE LORD ALL THE DAYS OF MY LIFE.**

It is strange why the Latin Interpreter did not render, *unum petii a Domino* (one thing I have asked of the Lord); for in this place also there is in the Hebrew the feminine gender for the neuter. He would therefore in rendering this word imitate the Greek interpreter, and render word for word. Yet the sense is: *Unam petii*, that is *unum petii* (one thing I have asked), or, *unam rem petii* (one matter I have asked). Most understand this place of the house of the Lord, where the ark of God was, and the propitiatory, and will have this to be the sense, *one thing I have asked of the Lord, this will I seek after*, that I may return from this exile, and that it may be allowed me to be continually in the tabernacle of the Lord, where the ark of God is, and the propitiatory, etc. But certainly to me the opinion of St. Augustine, who expoundeth it of the heavenly house, seemeth not only true, but also literal. For David saith not, that he desireth to dwell near the house of the Lord, but in the very house of the Lord: now it is known that David never dwelt in the house of the Lord, but in his own palace, which was far distant from the tabernacle, especially before the ark was carried to mount Sion; and he could surely, if he had wished, when he was king, have dwelt beside the tabernacle of the Lord. Moreover this place is like that which is in *Ps.* lxxxiii: "Blessed are they that dwell in thy house, O Lord: they shall praise thee for ever and ever," which certainly befitteth none but the dwellers in the house of God, which is in heaven. Lastly David, a holy and perfect man, would never ask any temporal thing with so great affection, as to say: "One thing I have asked, this will I seek after," as if nothing else were to be asked. The Prophet therefore explaineth in this place the true root of confidence in God, and why he feareth not any temporal evil: for the root is burning charity toward God: for he who ardently loveth the highest and eternal good, maketh nothing of all temporal things. "One thing," saith he, "I have asked of the Lord, this will I seek after," that is, I desire nothing temporal, I make no great account of the loss of all other things, so only I may deserve to have

[Margin: The Prophet speaketh of the eternal house of God.]

one thing; therefore for this one thing am I solicitous, this one thing I have asked, this one thing I will ask, namely that I may dwell in the house of the Lord, not for a time, but "all the days of my life," that is, as long as the life of the saints with God shall last, which without doubt shall have no end. It is very properly said, *that I may dwell in the house of the Lord*: for here on earth, although we be friends, nay sons of God, yet we do not dwell, but walk with God; nor do we rest in a house, but in a tent.

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. THAT I MAY SEE THE DELIGHT OF THE LORD, AND MAY VISIT HIS TEMPLE.**

[Margin: Beatitude consisteth in the divine vision.]

He teacheth why he desireth to dwell in the house of the Lord, namely because there is perfect felicity. Many Latin copies have, *ut videam voluntatem Domini* (that I may see the will of the Lord); but they have been rightly corrected in the edition of Sixtus V and Clement VIII, and there is set, *ut videam voluptatem Domini* (that I may see the delight of the Lord), as the most correct manuscript copies had. Now by *voluptas* (delight) in this place is not to be taken the act of delighting, but the object, that is beauty, comeliness, sweetness. For the Hebrew word נעם properly signifieth *beauty*; and the Greek word τερπνότης can signify *delectation* and *delight*, which sense the Latin Interpreter took, but it signifieth also comeliness and beauty, and for beauty Theodoret took that word in this place. Wherefore the Latin word, *voluptas*, if it is to agree with the Hebrew and Greek, is to be taken for *objective delight*, that is for beauty and comeliness. Which the very word of *seeing*, or *contemplating*, also requireth: for we do not properly see the act of delighting, but the delightful thing. And so St. Jerome rendereth: *Ut videam pulchritudinem Domini* (That I may see the beauty of the Lord); and St. Augustine readeth: *Ut contempler delectationem Domini* (That I may contemplate the delectation of the Lord), and expoundeth by *delectation* the delightful beauty. Yet if any one would altogether take delight in this place for the very act of taking delight, he ought not to understand by *the delight of the Lord* the delight which the Lord enjoyeth, but that which is received from the Lord, that is from the vision of the beauty of the Lord, and so the sense would come to the same. Now holy David in this place hath expressed felicity altogether properly; which according to the judgment of the more solid school consisteth in the noblest act of the most excellent of all the powers toward the highest object, that is in the intellectual vision of God, from which unspeakable delight and delectation followeth. And that which is added, *et visitem templum ejus* (and may visit his temple), St. Augustine readeth, *et protegar a templo ejus* (and may be protected from his temple). In which reading I know not what copies he followed: for the Hebrew and the Greek have nothing that can be referred to protection. The Hebrew word בקר properly signifieth *to seek, to look into, to survey with the eyes*, especially when the day is dawning: whence בקר *boker* with the Hebrews signifieth *the morning*, because then first, the darkness being driven away, we seek out and survey the things which lay hidden. So also the Greek word ἐπισκέπτεσθαι signifieth *to consider, to attend, to visit*. The sense therefore is: that I may see

<!-- p. 153 (PDF 164) -->

the beauty of the Lord, and then may visit that new light, that is, may survey with my eyes and seek out that whole heavenly temple of God, and all things that are in it. For in truth after the vision of God, nothing more pleasant can be met with, than the sight of that heavenly country, and of that divine house, in which are all the blessed, which is deservedly called a temple, since it is not a profane house, or city, but most sacred, since in it nothing profane is done, but the sacrifice of praise and jubilation is continually offered.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. FOR HE HATH HIDDEN ME IN HIS TABERNACLE; IN THE DAY OF EVILS, HE HATH PROTECTED ME IN THE SECRET PLACE OF HIS TABERNACLE.**

He giveth the reason why he so boldly asketh to dwell in the house of the Lord, and to see his beauty, because he hath already begun to taste that the Lord is sweet, and already holdeth the pledge of his love. As if he said most briefly: I dare to ask glory, because I have received grace. Now all the words are metaphorical, for David was not properly hidden in the tabernacle of the Lord, when he was sought by Saul; but the sense of these words is: in the time of the present life, in which the days are evil, God hath defended and protected me with so singular a providence, as if he had placed me in the inmost and most hidden part of his tabernacle. And from this so great grace and benevolence of God I conceive the hope, that I shall one day come to the house of the Lord, which one thing I ask and seek after. Now the same thing is signified in both parts of the verse; for *to be hidden in the tabernacle* is not *to be put in any part whatever of the tabernacle*, but *to be placed in the secret place of the tabernacle*, where one may be protected from all evils. In the Hebrew and Greek that phrase, *in die malorum* (in the day of evils), is joined with the first part of the verse, *abscondit me in tabernaculo suo in die malorum* (he hath hidden me in his tabernacle in the day of evils). But in the Latin copy it is joined with the second part, in this manner: *In die malorum protexit me in abscondito tabernaculi sui* (In the day of evils he hath protected me in the secret place of his tabernacle). It is all one where the pointing is put, since the sense is the same: yet the former pointing is found also in the books of the Fathers, Augustine and others, and perhaps in the Latin copy the pointing serveth not so much the understanding as the chant.

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. HE HATH EXALTED ME UPON A ROCK: AND NOW HE HATH LIFTED UP MY HEAD ABOVE MY ENEMIES.**

By another metaphor he signifieth the same thing, namely that he was protected and defended by God with a singular providence, as if he had been placed in a most strong and most lofty citadel. There is a like metaphor in Isaias, chap. xxxviii:[^3] "He," namely the just man, "shall dwell on high, the fortifications of rocks shall be his highness." And so the sense is: "He hath exalted me upon a rock," that is, he hath set me in a place lofty and fortified. "And now he hath lifted up my head above my enemies," that is, from this, that I now stand in a place most safe and most high, it hath come to pass, "that my head is lifted up above my enemies," that is, that I am above and the conqueror of my enemies. Now by these metaphors is not only described the protection and defence of the just man, whom all the devices of all his adversaries can in nothing hurt, according to that of St. Peter, I *Epist.* chap. iii: "And who is he that can hurt you, if you be zealous of good?" but there is also shown the manner, in which the just man cometh to this security: for the manner is the ascent of the mind by contemplation to God and things eternal. For he who earnestly meditateth things eternal, and ardently loveth God, is already set upon a most high and most strong rock, so that nothing can hurt him, since all earthly things have now become vile to him.

[Margin: Verse 11.]

**11. I HAVE GONE ROUND, AND HAVE OFFERED UP IN HIS TABERNACLE A SACRIFICE OF JUBILATION: I WILL SING, AND RECITE A PSALM TO THE LORD.**

In the Hebrew the first word of this verse is joined with the one above, and for *circuivi* (I have gone round), there is read *circuitibus meis* (in my goings round), so that the whole sentence is: *Exaltavit caput meum super inimicos meos circuitibus meis* (He hath lifted up my head above my enemies in my goings round); or, as St. Jerome rendereth, *qui sunt in circuitu meo* (who are round about me). But the Seventy Interpreters did not read סביבותי *sebibothai*, as it now is in the Hebrew, and signifieth *in my goings round*, but סבותי *sabbothi*, that is *I have gone round*, a single little letter being taken away, and the points changed. The Prophet therefore, exalted by contemplation above all earthly things, breaketh forth into wonder at the works of God, and the praise of the almighty maker. "I have gone round," saith he, that is, I have surveyed in mind all the works of God, which are in heaven and on earth, "and have offered up in his tabernacle a sacrifice of jubilation," that is, in this vast tabernacle of God, which is heaven, whither I have ascended by contemplation, with a loud voice, proceeding from great wonder, I have praised God, which praise is a sacrifice most pleasing to him. For so we read elsewhere: *Offer to God the sacrifice of praise*; and again: *The sacrifice of praise shall glorify me*. Nor only, saith he, have I offered up a sacrifice of jubilation, but I will also henceforth go on continually to do the same, "I will sing, and recite a psalm to the Lord."

[Margin: Verse 12.]

**12. HEAR, O LORD, MY VOICE, WITH WHICH I HAVE CRIED TO THEE: HAVE MERCY ON ME AND HEAR ME.**[^4]

He returneth to that which he had said: "One thing I have asked of the Lord, this will I seek after." And in this one petition he asketh to be heard, burning, to wit, with an ardent desire of seeing the beloved. "Hear," saith he, "O Lord, my voice, with which I have cried to thee," namely, that voice with which I cried: "One thing I have asked of the Lord, this will I seek after," etc. "Have mercy on me," who bear this exile hardly, "and hear me."

[Margin: Verse 13.]

**13. MY HEART HATH SAID TO THEE: MY FACE HATH SOUGHT THEE: THY FACE, O LORD, WILL I STILL SEEK.**

[Margin: Verse 14.]

**14. TURN NOT AWAY THY FACE FROM ME; DECLINE NOT IN THY WRATH FROM THY SERVANT.**

These things need not so much explanation, as pious meditation and imitation. "My heart hath said to thee,"

<!-- p. 154 (PDF 165) -->

that is, my desire speaketh to thee. "My face hath sought thee," that is, my inward eyes, which are in the face of my soul, seek thy beauty, they loathe all other things. "Thy face, O Lord, will I still seek," that is, this shall always be my care, to seek thy sight, not only that in the other world I may see thy countenance with open face, but also that in this world I may be busy about this one thing, that thou look upon me, and by looking enlighten and inflame me. "Turn not away thy face from me," that is, cease not, I pray, from looking upon me, lest at once the light be darkened, and charity grow cold. "Decline not in thy wrath from thy servant," that is, suffer me not to fall into sins, lest perchance thou depart from me in anger. Rightly in this place St. Augustine observeth that chaste fear, not servile, is expressed in these words. For servile fear desireth the master to be away, lest he punish, and therefore would not say: Decline not, but go and decline; but chaste fear, which truly loveth the beloved, feareth nothing more, than that the beloved depart. In the Greek text, after those words: *My heart hath said to thee*, there is added: *Dominum requiram* (I will seek the Lord); but those words are not in the Hebrew, nor are they read by Jerome and Augustine, although they are read by Theodoret and Euthymius. In the Hebrew there is not the pronoun *te* (thee), where we read: *Exquisivit te facies mea* (My face hath sought thee); but absolutely, *exquisivit facies mea* (my face hath sought): yet it is understood, and therefore it was added by the Greek and Latin interpreters.

[Margin: The difference between servile and chaste fear.]

[Margin: Verse 15.]

**15. BE THOU MY HELPER, FORSAKE ME NOT; DO NOT THOU DESPISE ME, O GOD MY SAVIOUR.**

Because he had said: "Decline not in thy wrath from thy servant," considering that by his own strength he cannot avoid the sins, for which we incur wrath, he crieth to God, that he cease not to help. In the Hebrew there is: *Adjutorium meum fuisti* (Thou hast been my help), but the Seventy Interpreters seem not to have read היית, that is *thou hast been*, but היה *heie*, which signifieth *be thou*: for there is the greatest likeness between these words, if the points be removed, which in the time of the Seventy Interpreters had not yet been set to them. Now the sense is always true and to the purpose; for if we read: "*Thou hast been* my helper, forsake me not," the sense will be: Because hitherto thou hast always helped me, help me henceforth also; but if we read: "*Be thou* my helper, forsake me not," it will be a repetition of the same prayer, which expresseth great affection. Now in those words: "Forsake me not; do not thou despise me," the Latin Interpreter seemeth to have inverted the order: for in the Hebrew and Greek text *ne despicias* (do not despise), or *ne abjicias me* (do not cast me away), is before *ne derelinquas me* (forsake me not). And for that *ne despicias*, the Greeks have a very expressive word, ἀποσκορακίσῃς, which in Euthymius is derived either from *scoria* (dross), or from *corvi* (crows), and signifieth *to be cast forth*, as dross is cast forth, or as carcasses are cast forth to the crows. The just man therefore asketh the help of God, that he may not sin, and, if perchance he shall have sinned, not to be cast forth from the face of God, as dross is cast forth, or not to be cast forth to the crows of hell, and not only not to be cast forth, or despised, but not even simply to be left and let go, but to be kindly cared for and healed; for therefore he addeth: "O God my Saviour." For it is not the part of a Saviour to despise and forsake the wretched, but to save and to cure.

[Margin: Verse 16.]

**16. FOR MY FATHER AND MY MOTHER HAVE LEFT ME: BUT THE LORD HATH TAKEN ME UP.**

[Margin: A Hebrew phrase to be noted.]

A great reason is given, why it is just that God should help, namely because none is more loving than he. The Hebrew phrase is to be noted, by which the Prophet addresseth God in the third person for reverence' sake; for he saith not: "But thou hast taken me up"; but: "But the Lord hath taken me up." There is a like phrase in *Gen.* chap. xxxi, verse 35, where Rachel saith to her father: "Let not my lord be angry that I cannot rise up before thee"; and Book III of *Kings*, chap. i, verse 27, Nathan saith to David: "Is this word come out from my lord the king, and hast thou not told me thy servant who should sit on the throne of my lord the king after him?" That phrase therefore, *the Lord hath taken me up*, is the same as if it had been said: *But thou, O Lord, hast taken me up*, or *hast gathered me*, as the Hebrew word יאספני properly signifieth. Now there is in these words a certain figure most fit to explain the benefit of God; for David was not a child, to need the help of parents, nor was it a great matter at that age to be left by parents, who might have been rather a burden than a help. The sense therefore of these words is: I am like an infant newly born, whom father and mother have left lying on the ground, and exposed to every injury: but thou, O Lord, hast most kindly gathered and cherished me, so cast away and desolate. For truly if any one consider the frailty of human nature, and the power of the invisible enemies, and how greatly we need the grace of God in all our actions, he will in no wise be able to deny, that we are most rightly compared to infants neglected by their parents. Now holy Ezechias understood this matter so well, that he compared himself not to an infant, but to the young of a swallow, *Isa.* xxxviii: "I will cry," saith he, "like a young swallow."

[Margin: Verse 17.]

**17. SET ME, O LORD, A LAW IN THY WAY, AND GUIDE ME IN THE RIGHT PATH, BECAUSE OF MY ENEMIES.**

Because he had compared himself to a little infant, whom God had gathered, he now rightly asketh that he teach him to walk; he asketh therefore the grace of fulfilling the divine commandments, which grace he is wont to ask most frequently in the whole psalter; nor without cause, since it is the way to that heavenly house of God, which alone he professed a little before that he desireth with his whole heart. For *legem pone mihi, Domine, in via tua* (set me, O Lord, a law in thy way), in the Hebrew there is, *doce me viam tuam* (teach me thy way); or, as St. Jerome rendereth, *ostende mihi viam tuam* (show me thy way). In the Greek there is one word, νομοτίθησον, which according to its ety-

<!-- p. 155 (PDF 166) -->

mology signifieth *to set a law*, as the Latin Interpreter rendered. But it is taken also for *to teach, to instruct, to show oneself a teacher*; therefore in this place, *set me a law in thy way*, signifieth, *instruct me concerning the way*, that is concerning thy law: which we read in another Psalm, *Ps.* xxii:[^5] "Shew, O Lord, thy ways to me, and teach me thy paths." And, as we say elsewhere, the Prophet asketh not the knowledge alone of the law, but the inward unction, by which he may be inwardly persuaded by the Holy Ghost, that the law of God is to be kept. The following words signify almost the same, *and guide me in the right path*, that is, lead me by the way of thy commandments, which is truly the right path, that is most just, but narrow: unless it please better that by that phrase, *teach me thy way*, the inward light be asked; by that phrase, *and guide me in the right path*, the pious affection of fulfilling the divine commandments. What is added, *because of my enemies*, or, as St. Jerome rendereth, *propter insidiatores meos* (because of them that lie in wait for me), signifieth that the grace of God is very necessary in this pilgrimage, because of the multitude of enemies both visible and invisible, who lie in wait for us everywhere, to lead us away from the right way of the divine law,[^6] and bring us to the pathless and broken places of vices.

[Margin: The words of God very necessary in this pilgrimage.]

[Margin: Verse 18.]

**18. DELIVER ME NOT OVER TO THE WILL OF THEM THAT TROUBLE ME; FOR UNJUST WITNESSES HAVE RISEN UP AGAINST ME; AND INIQUITY HATH LIED TO ITSELF.**

He goeth on in the same petition: for he asketh not to be delivered over to the soul (*in animam*), that is, the will and desire of the enemies, chiefly the invisible ones. There is a like phrase, *Luke* chap. xxiii: "But Jesus he delivered up to their will." For *voluntati eorum* (to their will), and *in voluntatem eorum* (into their will) are the same; but the former is a Latin phrase, the latter a Hebrew one. And so St. Jerome rendereth, *ne tradideris me animæ tribulantium me* (deliver me not to the soul of them that trouble me). The following words, *for unjust witnesses have risen up against me*, are referred by many to the false testimonies, which were spoken against David: which indeed I do not disapprove, but the sense seemeth to me to cohere better with the preceding and following words, and with the argument of the whole Psalm, if we refer these words to the temptations of devils, or of men, who by false promises, or threats, endeavour to lead the just man to impatience, or other sins, according to that of *Ps.* cxviii, 85: "The wicked have told me fables: but not as thy law." In the Hebrew there is, *insurrexerunt in me* עדי שקר, *testes falsitatis* (witnesses of falsehood) or *mendacii* (of lying); therefore that phrase, *testes iniqui* (unjust witnesses), is to be taken for *false witnesses*, who act unjustly, while they thrust false things upon us for true, to drive us to iniquity. What is added, *et mentita est iniquitas sibi* (and iniquity hath lied to itself), is not so much a translation, as an explanation of the Hebrew words; for in the Hebrew there are but two words ויפח חמס, which words St. Jerome rendereth, *et apertum mendacium* (and an open lie). Others render, *et loquens rapinam* (and one speaking rapine). But the Seventy Interpreters translated most acutely and most truly: for that word ויפח, although from the verb יפה it signify *to speak*, whence the Moderns render *loquens* (speaking); yet it can be derived from the verb פוח, which signifieth *to be ensnared*, whence is the noun פח, *a snare*. And that word, חמס *chamas*, although it can signify *rapine* and *a lie*, yet is most frequently taken for *iniquity*. The Seventy Interpreters therefore understood that here is signified iniquity itself, ensnared by itself, namely while unjust men, or devils, have stretched the snares of falsehood for the just man to their own destruction. Which Daniel said in other words, chap. xiii, to those wicked elders, the accusers of Susanna: "Thou hast lied against thy own head." David therefore saith: *Unjust witnesses have risen up against me*, thrusting forward falsehood and lies, *and iniquity is ensnared*, that is, *but iniquity hath lied to itself*, that is, it shall hurt no man, but its own author.

[Margin: Verse 19.]

**19. I BELIEVE TO SEE THE GOOD THINGS OF THE LORD IN THE LAND OF THE LIVING.**

He giveth the reason, why iniquity hath lied to itself. For I, saith he, in spite of all my enemies, "believe," that is, I surely trust, "that I shall see the good things of the Lord," that is, those good things, which are good even to the Lord himself, which make him blessed, which alone are true goods, and that "in the land of the living," that is, in that region, where death hath no place. In the Hebrew there is at the beginning of the verse one word לולא *lule*, which some render *nihil* (nothing),[^7] and St. Jerome better, *autem* (but): *Ego autem credo videre* (But I believe to see), etc. It was left out by the Seventy Interpreters, because it was not of great moment as to the sense.

[Margin: Verse 20.]

**20. EXPECT THE LORD, DO MANFULLY, AND LET THY HEART TAKE COURAGE, AND WAIT THOU FOR THE LORD.**

He concludeth by an apostrophe to himself, that patience and confidence in God are to be followed, as if he said: Therefore, my soul, because thou desirest to dwell in the house of God, because thou hast so many pledges of the divine love, because thou believest to see the good things of the Lord in the land of the living, fail not in tribulations, nor look back to earthly consolations, but *patiently expect, be strengthened in the Lord, do manfully*, while the evil days pass, and the good days come. In the Hebrew there is word for word, as St. Jerome rendereth, *exspecta Dominum, confortare, et roboretur cor tuum, et exspecta Dominum* (expect the Lord, be strengthened, and let thy heart be made strong, and expect the Lord). Likewise in the Greek there are the same words in the beginning and in the end. But the Latin Interpreter delighteth in variety; therefore at the beginning he set, *exspecta Dominum* (expect the Lord): and in the end, *sustine Dominum* (wait thou for the Lord). Patience joined with confidence addeth strength, and strength added increaseth patience and confidence. He saith therefore: "Expect the Lord," that is, patiently and with confidence expect the help, and consolation of the Lord. Hence "do manfully," or be made strong: but be made strong not with outward arms, but "let thy heart take courage," stir up thy mind, go on boldly; and hence, much more trust,

and with patience "wait for" the comforter, and helper, "the Lord," who without doubt, since he is faithful and just, "shall surely come, and shall not be slack," as is said, *Hab.* ii.
