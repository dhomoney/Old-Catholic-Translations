BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XLII.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*A psalm for David.*

This Psalm, which is without a title among the Hebrews, and among the Greeks and Latins hath nothing in the title except *A psalm for David,* and is as it were an appendix, or rather an epitome, of the foregoing Psalm, we shall declare very briefly. David therefore prayeth God for deliverance from his enemies, and at the same time comforteth himself with the hope of future beatitude.

<!-- p. 278 (PDF 289) -->

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. JUDGE ME, O GOD, AND DISTINGUISH MY CAUSE FROM THE NATION THAT IS NOT HOLY: DELIVER ME FROM THE UNJUST AND DECEITFUL MAN.**[^1]

David seemeth to have been oppressed, either by king Saul, or grievously tempted by devils, from whom, since he could have no recourse to men, he appealeth to God as judge. He saith therefore: "Judge me, O God," that is, be thou, O God, my judge; for I have none to whom I may flee for justice, but to thee. In the Greek it is clearer, κρῖνόν μοι, *judica mihi* (judge for me), that is, be thou judge for me. "And distinguish my cause from the nation that is not holy," that is, decide the cause which is brought against me by the nation that is not holy. In the Hebrew and Greek there is a fair allusion of the noun to the verb: for in Hebrew there is ריבה ריבי *riba, ribi;* and in Greek δίκασον τὴν δίκην, as if we should say, *litiga litem meam* (sue my suit), or *judica judicium meum* (judge my judgment); and because the words are general, there can be a twofold sense in these words: one, that he asketh of God to be not only judge, but also advocate, as if he should say: "Judge me," and, "sue my suit;" the other, that he declareth what he said, "Judge me," by the words following, "distinguish my cause," that is, take cognizance of the cause, and judge my judgment, pronouncing a just sentence in my favour. Now he calleth *the nation that is not holy* men of iniquity, cruel, and without mercy, as the Hebrew word showeth, which properly signifieth a man without mercy, and it appeareth also from what followeth: "Deliver me from the unjust and deceitful man;" that is, so judge my cause, that by this judgment thou mayest deliver me from the unjust and deceitful man, that is, from any unjust and deceitful man whatsoever, or at least from some certain unjust and deceitful man, who was the chief of the nation that is not holy, by whom he was afflicted.

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. FOR THOU ART GOD MY STRENGTH: WHY HAST THOU CAST ME OFF? AND WHY DO I GO SORROWFUL WHILST THE ENEMY AFFLICTETH ME?**

This verse is almost the same with the twelfth verse of the foregoing Psalm,[^2] and hath the same sense, that is, since thou, O God, "art my strength," and I trust in thy help alone, whence cometh it to pass that thou seemest to have cast me off, and that I, as one cast off from grace, go sorrowful, "whilst the enemy afflicteth me?" It is therefore a certain familiar expostulation, proceeding from confidence in God, wherein he complaineth that he is so permitted by God to be afflicted, as if God had cast him off from his friendship.

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. SEND FORTH THY LIGHT AND THY TRUTH: THEY HAVE CONDUCTED ME, AND BROUGHT ME UNTO THY HOLY HILL, AND INTO THY TABERNACLES.**

[Margin: Christ is light and truth.]

From this verse we understand that David was a spiritual man, as was said in the foregoing Psalm, and that he was solicitous for deliverance not so much from temporal evils, as from the dangers of sins, and of the loss of eternal life. For he saith: "Send forth thy light and thy truth," that is, grant me the light of thy grace and mercy, and thy truth and faithfulness: for they "shall conduct me" in this perilous pilgrimage, and "shall bring me unto thy holy hill," where the heavenly Jerusalem is, "and into thy tabernacles," that is, into thy very house, where there are many mansions, and many tabernacles for thy elect. By the light and the truth St. Augustine understandeth Christ, who is light and truth. But that pronoun *ipsa* (they) seemeth to stand in the way, when it is said, *ipsa me deduxerunt* (they have conducted me), which is found expressly in the Hebrew and Greek text in the plural number. Moreover this manner of speaking is like to that which is found in *Ps.* lvi: "God hath sent his mercy and his truth," and it is familiar to David to understand by light grace, or mercy, as *Ps.* lxvi: "May God have mercy on us, and bless us: may he cause the light of his countenance to shine upon us, and may he have mercy on us." But those words, *ipsa me deduxerunt, et adduxerunt* (they have conducted me, and brought me), are in the Hebrew copy in the future, *ipsa me deducent, et adducent* (they shall conduct me, and shall bring me); and although the Seventy Interpreters and the Vulgate edition have them in the past, yet one tense seemeth to be put for another, as Euthymius hath noted. For often the prophetical Scripture explaineth the things which are to come by the past tense, to show the certainty of the things.

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. AND I WILL GO IN TO THE ALTAR OF GOD: TO GOD WHO GIVETH JOY TO MY YOUTH.**

He explaineth what he will do on the holy hill and in the tabernacle of God, namely that he will do what the rest do who dwell in the house of God, whose office it is to offer up the sacrifice of praise: "Blessed, saith the same David, *Ps.* lxxxiii, are they that dwell in thy house, O Lord: they shall praise thee for ever and ever." "I will go in, saith he, to the altar of God," to offer up, that is, the sacrifice of praise: for all who enter that house straightway become priests, as may be understood from those words of theirs: "Thou hast made us to our God a kingdom and priests," *Apoc.* v. Nor will I go in to the altar only, saith he, but I will also go in "to God himself," that is, I shall appear before God himself, as one brought into his inmost chamber, "to God, I say, who giveth joy to my youth," that is, who giveth joy to my new youth. For in our country "our youth shall be renewed like the eagle's," as is said in *Ps.* cii, and as the Apostle saith, *Ephes.* iv: "We shall all meet unto a perfect man, unto the measure of the age of the fulness of Christ." St. Jerome rendered the word גילי *ghili* by *exultationem* (rejoicing), for which the Seventy rendered

<!-- p. 279 (PDF 290) -->

*juventutem* (youth). But this word is found very rarely in the Scriptures; and as on the authority of St. Jerome we may believe that it signifieth *rejoicing,* so on the authority of the Seventy Interpreters we may believe that it signifieth *youth.* But that which is found in *Dan.* i, גלכם *ghilchem,* doth not signify, as some have written, *youth,* but *likeness of age.* For Saint Jerome put for that word *coævis vestris* (your equals in age), and on his authority Sanctes Pagninus in his *Dictionary* taught that גיל *ghil* signifieth *likeness.* No man therefore can blame the Seventy Interpreters, or the Vulgate edition.

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. TO THEE, O GOD MY GOD, I WILL GIVE PRAISE UPON THE HARP: WHY ART THOU SAD, O MY SOUL? AND WHY DOST THOU DISQUIET ME?**

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. HOPE IN GOD, FOR I WILL STILL GIVE PRAISE TO HIM: THE SALVATION OF MY COUNTENANCE, AND MY GOD.**

He explaineth more clearly what sacrifice he will offer at the altar of God, when he shall have come into the tabernacle not made with hands, eternal in heaven. "I will give praise to thee, saith he, upon the harp," that is, I will praise thee by confessing thy mercies, thy justices, in fine all thy wonders, and I will praise not with the mouth only, but also in work; for then most excellently, and without any discord or disagreement, shall song and "harp" agree, that is, voices and works, which two in this pilgrimage seldom agree perfectly. Moreover the Apocalypse also maketh mention of the harp, when it describeth the praises of the Saints in heaven, *Apoc.* v: "Having every one of them harps in their hands;"[^3] and chap. xiv: "And the voice which I heard, was as the voice of harpers, harping on their harps." The things which follow were explained at the end of the foregoing Psalm.

[^1]: Translator's note: the print has *AD HOMINE* in this verse, for *AB HOMINE*; the commentary quotes it rightly.

[^2]: Translator's note: in Bellarmine's division of the foregoing Psalm the like words stand in verses 12 and 13.

[^3]: Translator's note: Bellarmine has *Habentes singuli citharas in manibus*; the Vulgate, *Apoc.* v, 8, has *habentes singuli citharas*, without *in manibus* (in their hands).
