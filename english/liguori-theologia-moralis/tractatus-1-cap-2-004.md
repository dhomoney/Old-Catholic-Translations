20. A doubtful conscience is that which suspendeth assent for either side of the doubt, and remaineth wavering and hesitating. Now a doubtful conscience is divided into the practically doubtful and the speculatively doubtful. But doubt is divided into negative and positive. It is *negative*, when on neither side do probable reasons occur, but only light ones. It is *positive*, when for either side, or at least for one, there is a grave motive, sufficient to form a probable conscience, though with fear of the opposite; therefore a positive doubt almost always coincideth with a

<!-- p. 8 (PDF 106) -->

[Left column]

probable opinion, of which we shall speak *in the chapter following*.

21. Next, doubt is divided into speculative and practical. A *speculative* doubt is that by which a man doubteth of the truth of a thing, for example whether some war be just or unjust; whether to paint on a feast day be a servile or a liberal work; whether baptism with distilled water be valid or null, and the like. But a *practical* doubt is that by which it is doubted of the moral goodness of a thing, for example whether it be lawful for me to serve as a soldier in such a doubtfully just war; whether to paint on this feast day; whether to baptize this child with distilled water. The true therefore must always be distinguished from the lawful; for a speculative doubt, though obliquely, and rather by consequence, regardeth the lawful, yet directly and principally the speculative regardeth the true, but the practical regardeth the lawful.

22. These things being laid down, we say, I, that it is never lawful to work with a practically doubtful conscience; and in case a man doth work, he sinneth, and indeed with a sin of the same species and gravity as that of which he doubteth; because he who exposeth himself to the danger of sinning already sinneth, according to that: *He that loveth danger shall perish in it* (1). Wherefore if he doubteth whether it be mortal, he sinneth mortally.

23. What if a man know that something is evil, but doubteth whether it be mortal or venial, and worketh with such a doubt? Some judge that he sinneth gravely or lightly, according as the object of the sin is in its species grave or light. So Vasquez, Sanchez, etc. Others, with Azor, Bonacina, Castropalao, etc., think that he always sinneth gravely. Others lastly, with Navarrus, Valentia, Granado and very many others, hold with sufficient probability that he sinneth only venially, if that man adverteth not at all, not even confusedly, to the danger of sinning gravely, nor to the obligation of examining the matter, and provided that the object be not certainly of itself a grave sin: I would add, provided also that the man be of a timorous conscience.

24. He therefore who is practically doubtful concerning some action ought certainly

[Right column]

first to lay aside the doubt by a certain principle, or a reflex one, concerning the moral goodness of that action, according to that which we shall expound in the dissertation on the use of probable opinion; provided that the man be sufficient to form for himself a practically certain conscience, and then he can work. But if he be insufficient (suppose because he is rude), he is bound to consult his parish priest or confessor, or some other godly and learned man, and to act according to the counsel. But he who should be doubtful, yet, all things weighed, should advert that the doubt is empty, can well (as Collet, the Continuator of Tournely, rightly writeth (2)) lay aside his doubt without other reflection, and so carry out the action; for then, if the malice of it is not known, that ignorance is altogether involuntary, as being one which cannot be overcome by study, as the Angelic Doctor teacheth.

25. We say, II, that it is lawful to work with a speculatively doubtful conscience, whenever he who worketh judgeth practically, by other reasons or reflex principles, that his action is certainly morally good. For (as Bishop Abelly wisely reasoneth) the reasons by which we judge of the truth of a thing, namely of the probability or of the doubtfulness of some opinion, for example that this war is probably or doubtfully just, are one thing; those by which we judge of the moral goodness of the action, to wit that in this war, the prince commanding, it is lawful for a subject to fight, are another, according to the doctrine of St. Augustine brought above (3). Now that from reflex principles a morally certain conscience concerning the moral goodness of the action can well be formed, will clearly appear below from the dissertation on probable opinion, which we shall bring at the end of this tract (4).

that second principle: *The condition of the possessor is the better.* For as often as a doubtful law doth not at all bind a man, the man certainly remaineth loosed and free from the obligation of the law, and in that case he can lawfully use his liberty, which he truly possesseth when the law is doubtful; for St. Thomas saith: *That is called lawful which is forbidden by no law* (1). Now as to this second principle, though some endeavour to weaken it, saying that it holdeth only for the forum, or only in the matter of justice, yet I know not how it can be denied in all things. The question can only be, whether in any moral doubt the law is in possession, or liberty. The antiprobabilists say that the law is always in possession; but we say that sometimes the law is in possession, sometimes liberty, namely when the law is not yet promulgated. This (I say) is the question, but no man can deny the principle, that the condition of the possessor is always the better. In the case therefore in which the law is in possession, we must stand for it; but if liberty is in possession, we must stand for liberty. Now to discern for which side possession standeth in doubts, it must be seen for which presumption standeth. And presumption standeth for that side which is not itself bound to prove the fact, but transferreth the burden of proving it to the other: *For a fact is not presumed, unless it be proved;* which is another principle, approved by law 2, *de probationibus*. In doubt therefore a fact is not presumed, but must be proved. But if the fact is certain, suppose if a marriage hath certainly been entered into, and it is doubted whether it was duly entered into, another principle ought to be kept: *In doubt every fact is presumed rightly done.* Or: *In doubt that is presumed done which by law was to be done.* Or: *We must stand for the validity of the act.*

27. From the foregoing it is inferred, 1, that if a law is doubtfully made, or doubtfully promulgated, it obligeth not, because possession standeth not for it, but for liberty.

[Right column]

The same must be said, if it is doubted whether in a promulgated law some burden be comprehended or not; for then we are not bound to fulfil it; because for that part of which the law is doubtful, the law is not in possession. But on the contrary, if a law is certainly made and certainly promulgated, and thereafter a doubt ariseth whether it be abrogated or revoked or dispensed, it must be observed; because then it is in possession. So also in doubt whether a law of itself just hath been accepted, it ought likewise to be observed; because it must be presumed that it was received, from that other principle: *In doubt that is presumed done which by law was to be done.*

28. It is inferred, 2, that what hath been said of a law must be said also of a vow; for a vow is like a particular law which a man imposeth on himself. Hence if a man doubteth whether he hath made a vow, he is not bound to fulfil it, as commonly teach Cabassutius (2), Suarez (3), Sanchez (4), Anacletus (5), the Salmanticenses (6), with others innumerable. So likewise he who doubteth whether in a vow made something be comprehended or not, is not bound to the vow as to the part of which he doubteth: for as to that part the vow is not in possession, as is had from chap. *Ex parte* 18, *de censibus*, where, when certain men had made certain vows, and afterwards doubted whether they were bound to the greater or to the less of the promise made, it was decreed that they were bound only to the less; and therefore the Gloss there noteth: *In doubts it is free to follow what shall have pleased more.* But on the contrary, if a man is certain of the vow and doubteth of the satisfaction, he is indeed bound to the vow, because then the vow is in possession.

29. Yet several authors ask,[^1] if a man judgeth with probability that he hath already satisfied the vow, whether he be bound notwithstanding this to fulfil it. Several authors deny it, namely Roncaglia (7), the Salmanticenses (8), with Laymann, Lugo (9) and others; because (as they say), since the obligation of the law is in that case doubtful, the possession of the law also becometh doubtful. 30. But if a man had made a vow, and afterwards had already performed the work promised, yet unmindful of the obligation contracted by the vow, with great probability he is not bound to perform that work again, as teach Suarez (5), Laymann (6), Sanchez (7), Bonacina (8), with Azor, Lessius and others commonly. Yet this holdeth whenever he who vowed can certainly judge that, if he had been mindful of the vow, he would have applied that work for the satisfaction of it: which certainly is commonly to be presumed; for every man hath the general will first to satisfy a burden due by obligation, and thereafter to add works of supererogation.

31. It is inferred, 3, that a subject, although he is not bound to render obedience to a superior commanding in those things which are certainly unlawful, is yet bound in those of which it is doubtful whether they be lawful or not; because in doubts we must stand for the superior, who possesseth the power of commanding, of which in doubt he cannot be despoiled; so commonly Cajetan (9), the Continuator of Tournely (10) and several others, with St. Antoninus, St. Bonaventure, Cabassutius, Soto, etc. But Soto, the Continuator of Tournely (11),

[Right column]

Lessius, Sanchez, the Salmanticenses, etc., most commonly limit this, whenever the thing commanded should be very difficult and irksome, namely if the subject by obeying would have to expose himself or another to the danger of grave spiritual or temporal damage. These things we shall expound more fully in treating of the particular precepts, *book 4, chap. 1, no. 47.*

32. It is inferred, 4, that a youth doubting whether he hath completed his twenty-first year is not bound to the fast. But one doubting whether he hath reached the sixtieth year, on the completion of which a man is with probability released from the obligation of the fast, as we shall say in *book 3, no.* 1036, is bound to the fast; because then the precept of the fast is in possession. The same must be said of him who doubteth of the age required for receiving a sacred order or a benefice; for then possession standeth for the precept. And the same must be said of him who is on the Saturday, and doubteth whether the hour of midnight hath passed; for then he cannot eat flesh, since the precept of abstinence is still in possession. But it is otherwise if a man doubteth of this on the Thursday, because then, after diligence used, he can lawfully eat flesh, since liberty is still in possession. So Laymann (12), Sanchez (13), Busembaum (14) and several others.

33. It is inferred, 5, that if after a marriage contracted in good faith a doubt ariseth concerning its validity, before inquiry into the truth he who doubteth cannot ask, but is bound to render (15), because the spouse who knoweth not of the doubt still possesseth the right of asking. But after inquiry he who doubteth can also ask; as Soto, Habert, Wigandt, Roncaglia, Sanchez, the Salmanticenses, etc., hold as more probable. *See the things to be said in book 6, nos. 903 and 904.*

35. But here it is asked, I, whether a possessor in good faith, a doubt arising, be bound to restore anything in proportion to the doubt. If the doubt is equal for and against, the common opinion, with Sanchez (4), against a few, is that the possessor is bound to nothing: *In a common thing the condition of the possessor is the better* (5).[^2] *In an equal cause the possessor ought to be held the stronger* (6). But what if the reasons against the possessor be stronger?

The *first opinion* saith that, although he have a probable reason for himself, yet he is bound in that case to restitution in proportion to that greater inclination, because the condition of the possessor is indeed the better in an equal doubt, but not in an unequal one. So Sanchez (7), with Coninck, Valentia and Ledesma, in the Salmanticenses (8).

Yet the *second opinion*, more common and more probable, teacheth that he is bound to nothing, unless it be morally established that the thing is another's. So Palaus (9), Lugo (10), Roncaglia (11), with Laymann, Cardenas, Dicastillo, Tamburini, Diana, Burghaber and others in La Croix (12), Mazzotta (13), Sporer (14), with Molina and St. Augustine (15), who saith: *He is most rightly called a possessor, as long as he knoweth not that he possesseth*

[Right column]

*what is another's.* Now as long as it is not certainly established (saith Sporer) that the thing is another's, so long the possessor knoweth not that he possesseth what is another's. The chief reason of this opinion is, because by possession in good faith a true right is acquired; for possession is thus defined by the jurists: *The right of holding to a thing not forbidden to be possessed.* Wherefore, as long as the ownership of the thing is doubtful, the possessor retaineth the right of holding to it; yet so that, before diligence is used to attain the truth, he is bound to keep the thing; but after diligence, the truth not being attained, he can consume it and even alienate it, the buyer of the things being warned of the doubt that lieth upon the thing. And on account of this chief reason Sanchez (16) changed the opinion defended elsewhere, and followed the second. Cardinal Sfondrati also (17) saith that one must not proceed against a possessor, save upon certitude. Hence that seemeth little probable which Roncaglia here addeth (18), namely that if the reasons favouring the possessor be only slightly probable, then he is bound to restore the thing, at least as to the greater part; for if the reasons against the possessor be so very probable as to ground a moral certitude against him, then he is bound to restore the whole thing; but if otherwise, he is bound to nothing.

36. It is asked, II: What if there be a probable reason against the possessor, and none for him?

The *first opinion* holdeth that if that probable reason begetteth a single opinative assent that the thing is another's, so that the possessor hath no assent that the thing is his own, then he is bound to restore. It is otherwise if he should still think with probability that the thing is his own. So Sanchez (1), citing Vasquez, Palaus (2), Renzi (3), Tamburini (4), Viva (5), who saith that when the possessor hath a probable reason against and none for himself, then he hath moral certitude that the thing is another's.

The *second opinion*, which Salas holdeth (6), saith that even when the reasons for the contrary side beget assent, provided they convince not, and the assent be not morally certain, but merely opinative, including the fear which is necessarily included in opinion, then the possessor can retain the thing, because possession outweigheth all reasons that convince not.

The aforesaid opinions seem able to be reconciled thus. It is certain that lawful possession giveth to every man a certain right of retaining the thing, until the right of another be established, as say most commonly Palaus, Laymann, Sporer and the others cited as above, with La Croix (7). And Viva himself (8) granteth it, saying that *possession in good faith begetteth a certain right,*

[Right column]

*which ought to prevail over the probable and not morally certain right of another.* The reason is, because lawful possession groundeth of itself a certain presumption of the justice of the possessor, as say Wigandt (9) and Lugo (10). Therefore even if there be a probable judgment against the possessor, and no probability stand by him, yet there is the presumption arisen from possession itself, which giveth him a certain right, which cannot be overcome save by the certain right of another through certain convincing reasons.

Wherefore the first opinion, saying that the possessor is bound to restore when he hath a contrary opinative assent, and none for himself, is surely to be understood of the case when the possession should be on some side weak, as being doubtful, or begun with doubtful faith, so that no lawful presumption seemeth to arise from it for the possessor; for then truly no probability would stand by him, and he would then have the single opinative judgment that the thing is another's.[^3] But it is otherwise, if he had a certain and lawful possession in good faith; because then possession alone, even if no other reason supported him, would afford him a certain right, which cannot be overcome save by certain opposite reasons, but not by a probable opinion, which necessarily includeth fear. And this Lugo clearly intimateth (11), where he saith that possession of itself begetteth a presumption prevailing for

37. It is asked, III: What if a possessor in good faith, a doubt arising, culpably neglect to use diligence, but afterwards the true owner can no longer be found? La Croix (2), Roncaglia (3) and the Salmanticenses (4), with Sanchez, Lessius, etc., say that then the possessor is bound to restore the thing in proportion to the doubt. But on the contrary Palaus (5), Bonacina (6), Tamburini (7), with Diana and Rebello, think it probable that the possessor, although he sinned mortally by omitting diligence, is yet then bound to nothing, since on the one side the damage done is doubtful, and on the other he still retaineth the right of possession acquired in good faith. But I judge it truer to say that such a possessor is bound to restore something (either to the owner, or to the poor, if the owner be uncertain). The reason is, because, since he hath culpably deprived the owner of the hope which the owner could have of the thing, and that hope was indeed able to be valued at a price, a certain damage hath now been done to the owner, who also certainly possessed that hope. Yet I think that restitution is not to be made according to the quantity of the doubt, so that, if the reasons on either side were equal, the thing or its price should be restored as to the half, but less, and perhaps much less; because that hope of the owner could not be valued at the half of the worth of the thing, but at much less,

[Right column]

given the equal probability of reason on the one side and the certain right of possession on the other, which favoured the possessor, and still favoureth him: for the possession of a thing is valued at much more indeed than the possession of a hope.

Now Tamburini noteth, I (8), with Villalobos and others, that if the possessor begin to doubt through probable reasons, he is indeed bound to inquire into the truth, but it is otherwise if he should doubt through reasons only slight. La Croix noteth, II (9), with Vasquez, Laymann, Palaus, Sanchez, Molina, etc., and Roncaglia thinketh it not improbable (10), that when the possessor (even one who began to possess with doubt) hath had the thing from a possessor in good faith, if afterwards diligence becometh impossible, he is bound to restore nothing; for he then lawfully succeedeth to every right of that one.

38. What, lastly, is to be said in doubt whether thou hast swallowed any food or drink: canst thou then receive the Eucharist? Sanchez (11), Roncaglia (12) and the Salmanticenses (13), with others, deny it; both because (as they say) one satisfieth not the certain law of not communicating without fasting by a doubtful satisfaction, and because this seemeth not to be free from grave irreverence. Yet others, as de Lugo (14), Castropalao (15), Laymann (16), Diana (17), La Croix (18), Sporer (19), Busembaum (20), with Sa, Cardenas, Medina, Gobat, Bossius and others, say that in such a doubt thou canst well communicate. Their reason is, because the law of fasting is not positive, so that fasting be commanded as a requisite absolutely necessary for communicating; but it is prohibitive, by which every man is forbidden to approach communion, if he hath taken any food or drink, lest earthly food be joined with the divine. For Lugo saith (21) that this is established from the second council of Braga (22), where it is forbidden *that any man, having first taken any food whatsoever, consecrate the oblation on the altar.* Likewise in the seventh council of Toledo (23)

But in doubt whether the case hath occurred in which the prohibition of the law is in force, he is not bound to abstain from communion, while his liberty is then still in possession; for, saith Lugo, he acteth not against the precept of the church, that no man communicate after food, who, examination made, knoweth not that he hath taken anything. Lawfully therefore, in doubt of the breaking of the fast, doth he communicate; for since communion would be forbidden him only in the case in which he had broken the fast, the liberty of communicating is well in possession, until the breaking be established. This reason seemeth not to be despised as trifling: at least in that case the law forbidding communion seemeth not certain, and as doubtful obligeth not, according to the things to be said in *no. 55 and following.* But to the second reason objected, that of irreverence, they answer that he is not judged irreverent who useth the right of his liberty, especially if he approacheth communion from devotion.

39. But whether the same is to be said, that he can communicate who doubteth whether he took food or drink after midnight. Lugo denieth it, because (as he saith), when a man knoweth that he hath eaten, and doubteth whether he ate after midnight, the burden of proving that he did not eat after midnight seemeth to lie on him. But, by the leave of so great a man, we say that the reason brought above, that the law is prohibitive, not positive, if it holdeth for the first case, holdeth also for the second;

[Right column]

for the prohibition is of communicating for those who have taken anything after midnight; if therefore, that they be unable to communicate, the fact must be proved that they have taken something, the fact must also be proved that they took it after midnight. For the rest, the other doctors cited above hold the affirmative opinion, to whom is added the learned Father Eusebius Amort (3), where, inquiring whether one who eateth in doubt whether the twelfth hour of the night hath already struck can communicate on the following day, he answereth thus: *He can communicate, because the condition of the possessor is the better, as long as presumptions to the contrary are not given so strong that he who worketh himself believeth it more probable that twelve hath already struck.*
