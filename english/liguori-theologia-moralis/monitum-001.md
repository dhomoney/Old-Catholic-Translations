## who is asked to read this preface for the understanding of the whole work

Since it is the chief aim of our least congregation of the Most Holy Redeemer to be occupied with missions, and since to the work of missionaries there is necessarily joined the office of directing the consciences of men by instructions and by confessions: therefore many years ago I purposed to deliver to the youth of our society a book treating of moral matters, which should hold the middle way between others either too rigid or too indulgent. I finished the work; but because it was too hastily committed to the press, that I might satisfy others, I did not satisfy myself; for many things in it either slipped out ill printed, or were set forth in a confused order. Wherefore, when I had perceived that these things needed a more diligent examination and also a clearer method, I applied my mind to a second edition, in which I took care to reduce all things to a better order, and to make the book more copious with most useful doctrines. Some opinions also (matters having in the course of time been recalled to a more profitable weighing), acknowledging myself a man, I have reformed. Nor did I blush at this, since St. Augustine did not blush to retract himself in many things; as Cajetan, Catharinus and Capreolus testify that St. Thomas also did, and the Angelic Doctor himself (Part III, quest. 9, art. 4) thus confessed: "although elsewhere I have written otherwise." For, as Tully said: "It is the part of a wise man to change his counsel." And elsewhere: "Never was it praised to abide in one opinion."

Now let it seem superfluous to no man that, after so many books handling moral science, I have undertaken this labour, as if I were doing what is already done: for when I had read very many authors, I found some who, indulgent beyond what is right to those who (as Isaias saith, chap. xxx) say: "Behold not for us those things that are right: speak unto us pleasant things," sew cushions under their head, that they may miserably rest in their sins: for it is not to be doubted that the Church of God feeleth much harm from the followers of these authors, since the greater part of men of laxer life runneth together to them. On the other hand I found others, who, accustomed to reject whatsoever doth not savour of extreme rigour, confound counsels with precepts, and burden consciences with new commandments, making no account of human weakness, and nowise mindful of that sacred canon (last chapter *On Transactions*) which admonisheth: "But in those things in which the law is not found expressed, thou shalt proceed with equity observed, always inclining to the more humane side, according as thou shalt see persons and causes, places and times to require." And in this manner they render the yoke of Christ the Lord, which is sweet, intolerable, and so shut up the way of salvation to many, according to that word of St. Ambrose: "There are also among us those who have the fear of the Lord, but not according to knowledge, laying down harder precepts, which the condition of man cannot bear" (*On Psalm* cxviii, Sermon 5). Both extremes indeed are most dangerous, for the first by relaxation openeth the broad way to destruction; the other (as the most learned Cabassutius saith in the *Theory of Law*, Preface to the Reader) driveth souls to ruin by a twofold path, namely by an erroneous conscience and by despair; since very many, having heard this more rigid doctrine, fall into mortal sins, either believing that there is deadly sin where there is none, or, deterred by the excessive difficulty, thinking it impossible that they can be saved in that manner, wholly cast away the care of their salvation.

Therefore I resolved to bring forth into the light this new work, which should hold the middle place between opinions too indulgent and too severe, and which should not be so diffuse that it could not easily be read, nor so brief that it should be wanting in many things. Thou wilt find arranged in it all the questions and moral matters which most serve for practice. And that I might choose in every question the opinions more conformable to truth, I spent no little labour; for through many years I turned over very many volumes of classic authors, as well of the rigid as of the indulgent opinion, which have latest (as I judge) come forth in public. But especially I bestowed sedulous pains in noting the doctrines of St. Thomas, which I took care to observe in their own sources. Moreover, in the more intricate controversies I also consulted learned men of the younger sort.

Besides, thou wilt find here, noted with diligent study in their proper places, the texts both of canon and of civil law that pertain to the matter. Likewise the condemned propositions, and (what is more useful) the more recent decrees of the supreme pontiffs, and chiefly the bulls and ordinances lately published by our most holy lord Pope Benedict XIV. Also I have here set forth very many things which I have learned rather by the exercise of missions and confessions than by the reading of books. Now that a just method might be kept, I judged that the *Medulla* of Hermann Busembaum should be set before (\*), not indeed that I approved all the opinions of that author, but only that I might follow his method, which among the methods of other authors seemed to me very well fitted for setting forth moral matters; then, that I might the more profit the beginners of our congregation, according to the end principally proposed to me, I endeavoured to write my appendices in a manner easier to be understood: for upon this I chiefly bent myself, that I should abound rather in clearness than in elegance of speech.

Now in the choice of opinions it was my great care always to set reason before authority; and before I gave my judgment, I was (unless I am deceived) wholly intent on this, that in each question I should hold myself indifferent, and should strip myself of all the soot of passion: which, kind reader, thou wilt be able sufficiently to know from this, that not a few opinions, which I had held in the former editions of this work, I have not hesitated to change in this last. For the rest, I have for the most part taken pains to set forth my own opinion, assigning to each opinion its just weight of greater, or equal, or lesser probability, as it seemed to my weakness, lest I should leave the reader in doubt, after the manner of some, who, only relating the opinions of others, make themselves not a little odious to those who read them. But where I did not find a convincing reason for one side, I did not dare to condemn the opposite, after the manner of others, who too easily reject opinions which many and grave authors defend, whose doctrines our aforepraised most holy father Benedict (far removed from those who too freely make light of them) mentioneth not without esteem in his works, elaborate and filled with learning of every kind, and often useth them; nay, he even deferreth not a little to their opinions, and, resting upon them, instructeth the faithful of Christ in many things, as may be seen in his bullarium.

For the rest, kind reader, I would have thee warned not to think that I approve those opinions because I do not reject them; for I shall sometimes faithfully set them forth with their reasons and patrons, that others according to their prudence may judge of what weight they are. Next, take notice that when I call any opinion *truer*, I then do not hold the contrary as probable, although I do not expressly condemn it as improbable. Moreover, when I call one of the opinions *more probable*, no judgment being given of the probability of the other, or when I use this phrase, *I dare not condemn*, I do not therefore mean to call it probable, but to remit it to the judgment of the more prudent. But if thou wouldst observe what system I myself judge should be held concerning the choice of moral opinions, see Book I, *On the Probable Conscience*, no. 55 and following. Farewell.

(\*) [Editor's note, Marietti 1879] The things which in the midst of the text of Hermann Busembaum are written within a parenthesis and begin with a capital letter are the words of St. Alphonsus.
