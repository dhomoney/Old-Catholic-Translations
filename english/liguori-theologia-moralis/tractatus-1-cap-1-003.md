## THE FIRST TRACT, OF CONSCIENCE

### THE AUTHOR'S NOTICE

[Left column]

Take notice, kind reader, that this first tract of conscience, by which the entrance to the whole of moral theology is opened, wrought out by me with special study, I have willed to set here for the easier instruction of my scholars. I say this, because thereafter thou wilt find all the other tracts of the work, from another author, marked with inverted commas, yet with my annotations adjoined. Now I chose to insert those tracts of another author, that I might follow the order of matters kept by him, because such an order, on account of the distinction and connexion of the matters, hath been generally reputed by many the best.

1. The rule of human acts is twofold: one is called remote, the other proximate. The *remote* or material rule is the divine law, but the *proximate* or formal is conscience; because although conscience ought in all things to be conformed to the divine law, yet the goodness or malice of human actions becometh known to us according as it is apprehended by conscience itself, as St. Thomas teacheth: *Human reason is the rule of the human will, by which its goodness is measured* (1). And more clearly in another place: *A human act is judged virtuous or vicious according to the good apprehended, to which the will is of itself borne, and not according to the material object of the act* (2). We shall treat first of the proximate rule, namely of conscience, then of the remote, to wit of laws.

2. Conscience is thus defined: *It is the judgment or practical dictate of reason, by which we judge what here and now is to be done as good, or avoided as evil.* Now conscience is called a *practical dictate*, to distinguish it from synderesis, which is the speculative knowledge of the universal principles for living well, namely: *God is to be worshipped: What thou wouldst not have done to thee, do not to another*, etc., as is had from St. Thomas (3).

3. Now conscience is divided into right, erroneous, perplexed, scrupulous, doubtful and probable. A *right* conscience is that which dictateth what is true; he therefore sinneth who worketh against it, since the apostle saith: *All that is not of faith* (namely of the dictate of conscience, as Estius and others explain) *is sin* (4),

Innocent III confirmeth it (1): *Whatsoever is done against conscience buildeth unto hell fire.* But an *erroneous* conscience is that which dictateth what is false as true. And this is either vincible or invincible. It is *vincible*, when, though it ought to be and can be overcome by him who worketh, either because he already adverteth to the error, or at least doubteth of the error, and adverteth at the same time to the obligation of overcoming it, yet he neglecteth to overcome it, as teach St. Antoninus (2), Navarrus (3), the Salmanticenses (4) and Suarez (5), with Sylvius, Cajetan and others, most commonly, from St. Thomas (6). Now the Salmanticenses (7) and Castropalao (8), with Azor, Suarez, Vasquez, Bonacina, etc., and Wigandt (9) note that there is no need that the diligence to be used for overcoming the error be the greatest, but that it sufficeth that it be common and ordinary. But it is *invincible*, when it cannot morally be overcome, since no thought nor doubt of error cometh into the mind of him who worketh, not even confusedly, while he worketh, or while he placeth the cause of the action, as shall be more fully explained *in book 2, tract on sins*, where the advertence required for sin shall be treated of.

4. Hence we say, 1. He who hath a vincibly erroneous conscience always sinneth, whether he work according to it, or against it. For he sinneth in acting against it, by choosing the evil which he judgeth to be evil: and he sinneth in acting according to it, because, since he ought to overcome the error, and can, by not laying it aside he worketh rashly.

5. We say, 2. He who hath an invincibly erroneous conscience not only sinneth not in working according to it, but is also sometimes bound to follow it. The reason of both is: he sinneth not, because although the action be not right in itself, yet it is right according to the conscience of him who worketh; and he is bound sometimes to work according to it, if conscience, which is the proximate rule, so suggesteth to him that he must act.

7. Franzoia objecteth (11) that an evil action can never be the cause of merit, and bringeth for himself St. Thomas, who (12) saith thus: *Good is caused by an entire cause, but evil by single defects.* And therefore, that that to which the will is borne be called evil, it sufficeth either that it be evil according to its own nature, or that it be apprehended as evil. But that it be good, it is required that it be good in both ways. But it is answered that the holy doctor speaketh here of good taken absolutely and simply, and not of good relatively and by accident, according as it is invincibly apprehended by conscience, which is the proximate rule of acting, according to that which the same Angelic Doctor teacheth (as we have related above), saying: *A human act is judged virtuous or vicious according to the good apprehended, to which the will is borne, and not according to the material object of the act* (13). 8. It is asked, 1. Whether there can be an invincibly erroneous conscience concerning the precepts of the law of nature. It is here briefly answered, that there can be concerning conclusions mediate and remote from the first principles, but not concerning immediate and proximate conclusions; as would be, to take away another's goods against the owner's will, to slay the innocent, etc. It is the common opinion, which we shall prove below, chiefly by the authority of St. Thomas (2).

9. It is asked, 2. Whether there can be an invincibly erroneous conscience in him who desireth to commit some evil, for example fornication, judging by error that the mere desire of fornicating is not mortal, if fornication follow not in deed. Sanchez and Cardenas affirm this as more probable, saying that such a one sinneth only materially, because although he willeth that evil object, yet he invincibly believeth that his desire doth no injury to God. Yet I could never judge this opinion probable; for I have never been able to understand how he who deliberately willeth to do an action by which he knoweth God to be offended, can inculpably believe that he offendeth not God, while he efficaciously desireth to perform that by which he certainly knoweth that he is turned away from God. But thou wilt say: How shall this man sin formally by that desire of his, if he is ignorant of its malice? It is answered: Though it be granted that he is ignorant of the malice of that inward act,

yet he certainly knoweth the malice of the outward act; if therefore he willeth to perform it, already known as evil, how shall he be excused from sin? All indeed discern by the very light of nature that they are bound to obey their Creator; when therefore a man deliberately willeth to do that which he knoweth to be forbidden him by God, he necessarily knoweth at the same time that he doth ill: and although he sin not then reflexly, thinking that only the outward act is sin; yet in the act as exercised and in fact he already sinneth, willing to deny to God, at the time when he thinketh to consummate the sin, the obedience due. Thou wilt urge: Many rustics therefore confess not these evil desires, because they believe that they then only sin, when they consummate the sin outwardly. I answer: these are rather falsely deceived in believing that they are not bound to confess sins which they consummate not; but a prudent confessor ought to judge that, when they consented to the consummating of the sin, they truly and formally sinned, turning themselves away from God by an evil will. From all these I judge the contrary opinion, with other learned modern writers, to be little probable.

10. Let us go on to discuss the other kinds of conscience. A *perplexed* conscience is that by which a man, set in the midst of two precepts, believeth that he sinneth, whichever part he choose; for example, if a man could by perjury save the life of an accused in court, and were on the one side straitened by the precept of religion not to forswear himself, and on the other (led by error) by the precept of charity towards his neighbour, and were not able to resolve himself, it is asked what he ought to do in this case. It is answered: If he can suspend the action, he is bound to defer it, until he consult wise men: but if he cannot suspend it, he is bound to choose the lesser evil, avoiding rather the transgression of the natural law, than of the human, or the positive divine law. But if he cannot discern which is the lesser evil, whichever part he choose, he doth not

11. Let us treat here last of the *scrupulous* conscience, of which it behoveth us to speak at greater length. A scrupulous conscience is that which for light motives, without reasonable foundation (for a scruple is an empty apprehension), often feareth sin where in truth there is none. The signs of a scrupulous conscience are these: 1. Obstinacy of judgment, by which the scrupulous man refuseth to obey the counsels of wise men, consulteth divers, but resteth in the judgment of none; nay, the more he heareth, the more perplexed he becometh. 2. Frequent change of judgment from light motives; whence ariseth inconstancy in acting, and disturbance of mind, especially in outward operations, for example in the celebration of Mass, in the recitation of the hours, or in the administration or reception of the sacraments. 3. To have impertinent reflections on many circumstances which were or could have been present in the action. 4. To fear sin in all things; and to remain stuck in mind against the judgment of wise men, and even his own, and therefore never to be content with one assurance of the confessor, but to ask him again and again of the same act, whether in acting according to the counsel given his conscience can be free from fault.

12. Now the remedies to be applied for scrupulous persons of this kind are these. After the confessor hath already perceived from the signs above alleged that the penitent is scrupulous, let him prescribe to him: 1. That he greatly cultivate the virtue of humility, for scruples often take their rise from the vice of pride. 2. That he beware of reading books which stir up scruples, and avoid the conversation of the scrupulous. 3. That he tarry not long in the examination of conscience, especially concerning those things in which he is most troubled. 4. That he flee idleness, from which the mind is often filled with empty apprehensions. 5. That he earnestly commend himself to God, to obtain help to obey the precepts

Hence it will greatly profit for the calming of consciences tossed with scruples, that the confessor set before their eyes these excellent authorities of wise men, which I here subjoin. Natalis Alexander (1) saith: *Now that scruples ought to be despised, when the judgment of a prudent, pious and learned director is added, and that we must act against them, is plain from the chapter Inquisitioni, de sententia excommunicationis, where it is said: But if any man hath a conscience from light credulity... he may lawfully act at the counsel of his pastor.* St. Antoninus confirmeth this from John Gerson, who (2) writeth thus: *They who refuse to believe the counsel of superiors and prudent men for laying aside such scruples, and for acting against them, err in many ways. Many of the simple are deceived in this, not knowing how to distinguish between those things which the superior portion of the soul suffereth, and those which the inferior suffereth without the assent of the superior.* Hence he bringeth that of blessed Bernard, who said to a certain scrupulous disciple: *Go, and celebrate on my faith.* But some one will say, would that I had a St. Bernard! but now, while I look upon the middling wisdom of my superior, I dare not commit my conscience to him. St. Antoninus answereth from the same Gerson: *Whosoever thou art that sayest so, thou errest; for thou hast not committed thyself into the hands of a man because he is lettered, because he is pious, etc., but because he is set over thee. Wherefore obey him, not as man, but as God... Beware at the last, lest while thou seekest security, thou fall into the grievous hole of rashness.* Hence St. Philip Neri (3) spoke thus to his penitents: *Let them who desire to advance in the way of God submit themselves to a learned confessor, whom they are to obey as God. 13. Wherefore let the confessor earnestly take care to persuade penitents troubled with scruples, that he walketh altogether safe who resteth in the counsels of his director and complieth in all things in which evident sin appeareth not; for then he obeyeth not man, but God himself, who saith: *He that heareth you, heareth me; and he that despiseth you, despiseth me* (8). But on the contrary let him inculcate that he committeth himself to great danger of his salvation, who refuseth to yield obedience to the precepts of his confessor; for then he exposeth himself to the danger of losing not only peace of heart, devotion and progress in virtue, but also his mind (for how many scrupulous persons, not resting in obedience, have fallen into madness!), likewise the health of the body: nay, what is worse, even of suffering

14. Moreover let the prudent confessor take pains to prescribe to such penitents general rules rather than particular ones; for by particular rules the scrupulous are scarce ever able to resolve themselves to work; because they always doubt whether that rule prescribed can hold for the case occurring, which will often seem to him different from the past case conferred of with the confessor. Hence Concina rightly saith (9): *Having once received the rules of direction, they* (the penitents) *ought not importunately to go to the director, and vex him with most troublesome questions: but by the benefit of the rules which they have received they ought to drive away scruples.*

15. Now the scrupulous are for the most part troubled by scruples in three ways. Some are straitened by evil thoughts, to which they often fear that they assent. Others on account of past confessions, with which they doubt that they have ever satisfied. Others on account of the fear of sinning in every operation which they are about to do. Hence, 1. on the penitent who is afraid that he giveth assent to every evil thought (for example against faith, chastity or charity), let the confessor enjoin that he wholly despise such scruples, affirming that not evil thoughts, but only evil consents are sins. And in this matter let him not omit always to use that rule wisely delivered by the doctors, namely that they who are of a timorous conscience, unless they know with moral certainty that they have consented to grievous sin, are to be judged free from sin; for, as Father Alvarez saith, it is impossible for sin to enter into a soul abhorring it, without being clearly known by her. 16. II. On the penitent who is always anxious about past confessions, because he feareth that he hath failed in integrity or in sorrow, if he hath already at another time made a general confession, or for some notable time hath made his confessions diligently, on him let him enjoin that he think no more of past faults, nor speak a word of them in confession, unless he can swear that he certainly committed those mortal sins, and moreover never confessed them. Nay, Azor, Bonacina, Becanus, Coninck, Laymann and others teach that sometimes a man may be so straitened with scruples, that although it seem to him certain that he hath not told some things, he is still not bound to confess them. Wigandt confirmeth this, writing to the purpose: *The scrupulous man is by no means to be heard, who would repeat his confessions; for granted that he hath not confessed some sins, yet on account of his grave harm, and on account of the danger of sticking perpetually in so great anxiety, he is excused from the integrity of confession.* And in this let the confessor press vehemently, that the penitent obey him: but if he refuse to obey, let him chide him, deprive him of communion, and as far as he can beat down his hardness. With the scrupulous who obey we must deal gently; but with those who fail in obedience the greatest rigour and austerity is to be used: for, bereft of this anchor of obedience, they can never be healed.

18. Nor doth it matter that he worketh with a scrupulous conscience, or with actual fear of sinning; for by the common and true opinion, which Concina (3), Roncaglia (4), Anacletus (5), St. Antoninus (6), the Salmanticenses (7), with Navarrus, Cajetan, Bonacina and very many others teach, the scrupulous man so acting sinneth not at all. The reason is, because a scrupulous conscience, or a dictate of the mind arising from scruples, cannot take away the assent concerning the moral goodness of the action before formed from the judgment of the confessor or in another way; for, as Father Concina saith: *Doubt suspendeth assent, but not so a scruple, as being distinct from doubt. Scruples therefore are certain little clouds of the mind, which can somewhat obscure the judgment of the understanding, yet can in no way stifle it.* And this holdeth especially, if obedience to the confessor urgeth to act against scruples, where fault is not evidently perceived, as is had from *chap. Inquisitioni 44, de sententia excommunicationis*, where the Pontiff declareth him secure, who following the counsel of his pastor casteth away scruples; for thus it is there said: *At the counsel of his pastor (the conscience of light and rash credulity being cast out) he may lawfully act.* Nor doth the text alleged by some one stand in the way (8), where the Pontiff prescribed to one having too scrupulous a conscience, that he should not ascend to the higher orders, unless that conscience were first laid aside. 19. Hence for practice it is to be concluded, that the scrupulous man must always have obedience before his eyes, that, stoutly driving away the fear of his scruples, he may work freely. Nor is there need that in every particular act he form this judgment, namely that he ought to contemn scruples by the precept of the confessor; for it is enough that he act against the scruple from a judgment before formed; because on account of past experience that judgment existeth virtually or habitually in his conscience, though obscured by darkness. So much the more because, when the scrupulous man worketh in that confusion, he certainly worketh not with a formed and deliberate conscience, which is indeed required to constitute sin, as John Gerson rightly writeth (1) in these words: *A formed conscience is, when after discussion and deliberation it is judged by a definitive sentence of reason, that something is to be done, or avoided; and to act against it is sin.
