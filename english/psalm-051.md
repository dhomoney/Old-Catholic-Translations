BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM LI.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*Unto the end, understanding for David, when Doeg the Edomite came and told Saul:* "David went to the house of Abimelech."[^1]

The title of the Psalm is very well known from the history of I *Kings,* chap. xxii. Now the argument is a reproof of Doeg the Edomite, who calumniated David and Achimelech the priest before king Saul, and was the cause of the perdition of many men.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. WHY DOST THOU GLORY IN MALICE, THOU THAT ART MIGHTY IN INIQUITY?**[^2]

[Margin: The variety of reading is reconciled.]

David beginneth with a question, that the reproof may be the more effectual, as M. Tullius began against Catiline: "How long, I pray, wilt thou abuse our patience, Catiline?" "Why dost thou glory," saith he, "in malice, thou that art mighty in iniquity?" for Doeg the Edomite gloried that by his accusation he had destroyed the priest of the Lord, and all his family. For Saul, when he had heard from this Doeg the Edomite that David had been received as a guest by Achimelech the priest, was kindled to so great wrath, that he commanded not only Achimelech the priest to be slain by Doeg himself, but also eighty-five other priests, who were with him; and then he smote with the edge of the sword the priestly city, slaying men and women, children and sucklings, sheep also, and oxen, and asses. Behold how great evils sprang from that calumniator, so that he is truly called "mighty in iniquity." But a difficulty occurreth from the disagreement of the Hebrew, Greek and Latin copies. For St. Hilary and St. Augustine read: *Quid gloriatur?* (Why doth he glory?) But our reading: *Quid gloriaris?* (Why dost thou glory?) is favoured by the Hebrew text and by St. Jerome, not only in his version, but also in his *Commentary.* Moreover in the Hebrew and Greek text a point is set after the word *potens es* (thou art mighty): *Quid gloriaris in malitia, qui potens es.* (Why dost thou glory in malice, thou that art mighty.) But our reading is favoured by St. Hilary and by St. Jerome in his *Commentary,* although St. Augustine and Euthymius read as it is now had in the Hebrew and Greek text. It appeareth therefore that among the ancients there were various pointings. Lastly, in the Hebrew copy there is *misericordia Dei* (by the mercy of God), for that which is in the Greek, ἀνομία, *iniquitas* (iniquity); for so to the word it is read in the Hebrew: *Quid gloriaris in malitia, qui potens es misericordia Dei?* (Why dost thou glory in malice, thou that art mighty by the mercy of God?) But the Hebrew word חסד *chesed* signifieth not only *misericordiam* (mercy), but also *opprobrium* (reproach), as is manifest from *Lev.* chap. xx, verse 17, and from *Prov.* chap. xiv, verse 37.[^3] Therefore in this place it ought not to have been rendered *misericordia Dei,* but *opprobrio Dei* (by the reproach of God), because he slew a multitude of priests, which redounded to the reproach of God. The Seventy Interpreters rendered ἀνομίαν, *iniquitatem* (iniquity), because that deed of Doeg the Edomite was most iniquitous: for, as is read I *Kings* xxii, no others dared to put forth their hands against the priests, even at the king's command, save this Doeg the Edomite, who yet knew best of all the innocence of the priests, as an eyewitness of all the things.

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. ALL THE DAY LONG THY TONGUE HATH DEVISED INJUSTICE: AS A SHARP RAZOR, THOU HAST WROUGHT DECEIT.**

He amplifieth in wondrous ways that calumnious denunciation of Doeg the Edomite; and first he saith that it was not a sudden denunciation, but one long meditated: "All the day long," saith he, "thy tongue hath devised (*cogitavit*) injustice," that is, thou wast continually thinking how thou mightest unjustly denounce, and at length, an occasion being offered, thy tongue brought forth that with which it had long before been in travail: for although thinking pertaineth to the mind, yet David poetically attributeth this to the tongue, as if the tongue itself were so evil, that even when silent to the ears of men,

<!-- p. 346 (PDF 357) -->

yet within itself it spoke by thinking. Then he addeth that the deception was most swiftly wrought by him, as a sharp razor cutteth most readily and most easily: for the Prophet elegantly joineth slowness of meditation with swiftness of execution. For truly Doeg the Edomite, when occasion was given, most swiftly accomplished what he had long before devised. For in very few words he altogether persuaded Saul that Achimelech the priest had conspired with David, which was a most grievous deceit and imposture; he saith therefore: "As a sharp razor, thou hast wrought deceit," that is, as a sharp razor most easily cutteth hairs, so thou most easily didst deceive Saul. By *deceit* may also be understood the deceitful and fraudulent slaying of the priests, which Doeg wrought by killing the whole offspring of the priests even to children and sucklings, as a razor shaveth off utterly all hairs, though the very finest. Now it is called a deceitful slaying, because the priests were not called to the king as enemies, so that they might have feared that they were to be slain, but they were circumvented, and perished unawares.

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. THOU HAST LOVED MALICE MORE THAN GOODNESS: AND INIQUITY RATHER THAN TO SPEAK RIGHTEOUSNESS.**

[Margin: A pernicious lie is iniquity.]

He declareth from what root that calumnious denunciation came forth, and saith that it came not forth from ignorance, or from some chance, but from the evil will of him who always loved evil rather than good, and a lie rather than truth. "Thou hast loved," saith he, "malice more than goodness," that is, thou delightest rather to hurt thy neighbour than to do him good. "Iniquity rather than to speak righteousness," that is, it delighteth thee rather to speak a lie than the truth: for the Hebrew word שקר *sheker* properly signifieth *mendacium* (a lie); but because a pernicious lie is a certain iniquity, therefore the Seventy Interpreters rendered ἀδικίαν, *iniquitatem* (iniquity), to declare that a pernicious lie is here treated of: so also David himself, when he ought to have opposed truth to a lie, yet opposed justice, saying, "than to speak righteousness," because he would show that the lie of that Doeg the Edomite was not an idle lie, or an officious one, but plainly pernicious and unjust. Moreover, by *righteousness* (*æquitatem*) is to be understood *justice,* as the Hebrew word צדק *tsedek* soundeth, and not that which the word *æquitas* (equity) properly soundeth among the Latins. But Doeg seemeth not to have uttered any lie, when he saith: "David went to the house of Achimelech." I answer that there was a lie in those words according to the intention of the speaker and of the hearers: for he would signify that Achimelech had been a partner of the conspiracy of David, and in that sense his words were taken by king Saul.

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. THOU HAST LOVED ALL THE WORDS OF RUIN, O DECEITFUL TONGUE.**

He explaineth yet more what he called "a lie and iniquity;" and saith that it was a truly pernicious lie, since it was the cause of the perdition of very many innocent men. "Thou hast loved," saith he, "all the words of ruin (*præcipitationis*)," that is, thou hast loved all the words by which innocent men might be cast headlong into ruin and perdition: the Hebrew word בלע *balah* signifieth *deglutire* (to swallow), *absorbere* (to swallow up), *perdere* (to destroy), and the like. Therefore that word, *præcipitationis,* ought to be expounded of a casting headlong into the sea, or into a pit, that they may be swallowed up, or devoured; or *præcipitationis* is put for *ruinæ* (of ruin) and *perditionis* (of perdition): now it is certain from I *Kings,* chap. xxii, that on account of the words of Doeg the Edomite a whole city perished. But that, *lingua dolosa* (deceitful tongue), is variously read by the authors: for St. Hilary and St. Augustine read *linguam dolosam* (a deceitful tongue, in the accusative), as Theodoret also readeth, and as the Greek copies now have; and if it be so read, the sense will be: *Thou hast loved all the words of ruin, a deceitful tongue,* that is, thou hast loved all the words of ruin, thou hast loved, I say, a deceitful tongue. But St. Jerome, although in the Hebrew he rendereth *linguam dolosam,* yet in the *Commentary on the Psalms* readeth *in lingua dolosa* (in a deceitful tongue), as Euthymius also readeth, though he is not silent on the variety of the Greek copies, in some of which there is in the accusative *linguam dolosam;* and if any man read so, the sense will be: *Thou hast loved all the words of ruin in a deceitful tongue,* that is, by the instrument of a deceitful tongue. Our copies seem to be taken in the vocative, that the sense may be: O *deceitful tongue,* thou hast loved all the words of ruin, that is, thou delightest always to speak pernicious words; and our reading seemeth to agree well enough with the Hebrew text, where the word is put simply without any sign of an oblique case.

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. THEREFORE WILL GOD DESTROY THEE FOR EVER: HE WILL PLUCK THEE OUT, AND REMOVE THEE FROM THY DWELLING PLACE: AND THY ROOT OUT OF THE LAND OF THE LIVING.**

[Margin: The just retribution of the impious.]

He foretelleth that the sin of Doeg the Edomite shall not be unpunished, but that for the temporal destruction of the priests, of which he was the cause, everlasting ruin is to be rendered to him. "Therefore," saith he, "will God destroy thee for ever," that is, for this thy sin God will destroy thee by the roots in this world, and in the world to come, so that thou remain destroyed for eternity; by which words eternal desolation in this world, and eternal damnation in the world to come, seem to be signified;[^4] and this is the just retribution of the impious, that they who destroy others should themselves also perish miserably. Then what he had said in general he explaineth by parts, saying: "He will pluck thee out, and remove thee from thy dwelling place: and thy root out of the land of the living," that is, in this manner he will utterly destroy thee, because he will punish thee with exile and with childlessness, so that first thou lose thy wealth and thy country, and then die in exile and without children. "He will pluck thee out," saith he, "and remove thee," that is, he will make thee remove from thy dwelling place, and so thou shalt wander an exile and a vagabond. "And thy root he will pluck out of the land of the living," that is, he will root thee out of the land with all thy posterity: for children are like roots, because by them the father is rooted and established in the land. And since

<!-- p. 347 (PDF 358) -->

by the land of the living the heavenly country may be signified, eternal damnation also is indicated in this place, as we said above. In the Hebrew text, for *evellet te* (he will pluck thee out), there is יתחך *iachtheca,* which word St. Jerome rendereth *terrebit te* (he will terrify thee). But St. Jerome derived that word from חתת *chathath,* which is *terrere* (to terrify). But the Seventy Interpreters derived it from חתה *chathah,* which is *capere* (to take) or *carpere* (to pluck), which is the same as *evellere* (to pluck out).

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. THE JUST SHALL SEE AND FEAR, AND SHALL LAUGH AT HIM, AND SAY: BEHOLD THE MAN THAT MADE NOT GOD HIS HELPER.**

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. BUT TRUSTED IN THE ABUNDANCE OF HIS RICHES: AND PREVAILED IN HIS VANITY.**

From the punishment of the impious informer this will follow, that many are instructed. "The just shall see and fear," that is, just and prudent men will consider this fall, and will shudder. "And shall laugh at him, saying: Behold the man," etc., that is, and they will deride him, because he acted most foolishly, since he would not trust in God, who is most mighty, and placed his hope in frail riches, which are most easily lost. "Prevailed in his vanity," that is, they will deride the same man, because he strove to prevail not in true strength and true power, but in snares and lies most full of vanity: for that word, *prevailed,* signifieth not that he truly prevailed at last, but that in his own opinion he believed that he could prevail: for although for a time he seemed to prevail against the innocent, yet the issue will show that he succumbed, not prevailed, when "the just shall stand with great constancy against those that have afflicted them," as is said *Wis.* v.

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. BUT I, AS A FRUITFUL OLIVE TREE IN THE HOUSE OF GOD, HAVE HOPED IN THE MERCY OF GOD FOR EVER, YEA FOR EVER AND EVER.**

He concludeth the Psalm, showing that he hath entered on an altogether contrary way. "I," saith he, "am like a fruitful olive tree in the house of God," that is, I shall not be plucked out, nor rooted out, as Doeg, a dry tree, but I shall be rooted more and more, as a beautiful olive tree, ever green, and bearing fruit; and because I am such, therefore "I have hoped in the mercy of God for ever, yea for ever and ever," that is, I have hoped that I shall be helped by God for ever, yea for ever and ever. Where he wonderfully opposeth himself to Doeg the Edomite; him he compareth to a dry tree, but himself to a green olive tree; of him he saith that he is to be plucked out even from his dwelling place, of himself he saith that he is to be rooted in the house of God; of him he saith that he trusted in his riches, but of himself, in the mercy of God.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. I WILL PRAISE THEE FOR EVER, BECAUSE THOU HAST DONE IT: AND I WILL WAIT ON THY NAME, FOR IT IS GOOD IN THE SIGHT OF THY SAINTS.**

[Margin: Things to come are most certain with God and in the knowledge of the Prophets.]

He giveth thanks for a thing to come as if it were past, because things to come are most certain with God and in the knowledge of the Prophets. "I will praise thee," saith he, "for ever," that is, I will laud thee always, because thou hast done this judgment, that thou mightest confound and destroy him that trusted in his riches, and comfort and build up him that hoped in thee. "And I will wait on the name," that is, and always henceforth I will hope in thee; for to hope in God and to wait on God are the same, and the name of God is put for God himself. "For it is good in the sight of thy Saints," that is, with good reason will I hope in thy name, because thy name is sweet to thy Saints, who have tasted its sweetness.

[^1]: Translator's note: so the print in the title. The Vulgate and the Douay-Rheims Bible have Achimelech, as Bellarmine himself has everywhere below.

[^2]: Translator's note: Bellarmine does not number the title, and divides the Psalm into nine verses; the Douay-Rheims Bible has eleven, and its verses 1 and 2 are the title. His 1 to 5 are its 3 to 7; his 6, its 8 and the beginning of its 9; his 7, the rest of its 9; his 8 and 9, its 10 and 11.

[^3]: Translator's note: so the print. *Prov.* xiv has thirty-five verses; the place meant is verse 34, *miseros autem facit populos peccatum* (but sin maketh nations miserable).

[^4]: Translator's note: the print has *significare* (to signify); it is translated as *significari* (to be signified), which the nominatives require.
