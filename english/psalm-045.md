BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XLV.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*Unto the end, for the sons of Core, for the hidden.*

Some of the more recent Interpreters will have this Psalm to have been written in thanksgiving for a victory of David gained over his enemies by the help of God; but it is strange that such an argument came into the mind of none of the ancient Fathers. For St. Ambrose, St. Jerome, St. Augustine, St. Basil, St. John Chrysostom, Theodoret and Euthymius with great agreement teach that the Psalm is to be understood of the deliverance of the Christian Church from the persecutions of the unbelievers. And this explanation can be confirmed from the title of the Psalm; for that, *for the hidden,* showeth openly enough that the Prophet speaketh not of a thing past, but of one to come, and of such a kind that in the time of David it was most hidden, and could not be known save by God revealing. Therefore the present Psalm is rightly subjoined to the Psalm above: for in that was foretold the exaltation of the Church by reason of the spiritual nuptials with Christ the Lord, the King; but in this is foretold persecution, and deliverance from persecution; which deliverance is chiefly to be, as St. Basil thinketh, in the consummation of the world.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. OUR GOD IS OUR REFUGE AND STRENGTH: A HELPER IN TROUBLES, WHICH HAVE FOUND US EXCEEDINGLY.**[^1]

[Margin: The soldiers of Christ overcome temptations in two ways.]

The soldiers of Christ overcome no less by fleeing temptations than by enduring them. When there is need to flee, God is the safest refuge; when there is need to endure, God is strength (*virtus*), and fortitude: and at both times "he is a helper in troubles:" for he both helpeth by affording a refuge, and helpeth by giving the victory. Now that, *which have found us exceedingly,* signifieth that the tribulations were most grievous which arose at the beginning against the Church, and that they rushed in suddenly and unlooked for: for, as is read in *Acts* ii and iii, after the ascension of the Lord, and the coming of the Holy Ghost, the Church was being built up and was growing in Jerusalem with great peace and tranquillity. "Continuing daily, saith St. Luke, with one accord in the temple, and breaking bread from house to house, they took their meat with gladness and simplicity of heart, praising God, and having favour with all the people, and the Lord increased daily together such as should be saved." But a little after there began a most vehement persecution, the Apostles being scourged, Stephen stoned, and all the disciples scattered, except the Apostles. In the Hebrew text is read: *Adjutor in tribulationibus inventus est nimis* (A helper in tribulations he hath been found exceedingly), that is, God hath been found in tribulations a helper very mighty. Each sense is very good; but as to the truth of the reading, we must cleave to the Seventy Interpreters, who without doubt read that Hebrew word, נמצה *nimsah,* a little otherwise; else they would never have translated *invenerunt* (have found), unless they had been very unskilled. St. Basil noteth that many have in their mouth the words of this verse: "God is our refuge and strength," but that very few pronounce them from true affection, which is gathered thence, that to overcome tribulations they flee with so great ardour to human defences, or even to diabolical arts, as though they held the help of God for nothing. But on the contrary St. David therefore saith: "Our God is our refuge and strength," because he trusted very little indeed in the might of men, or in arms, or in industry.

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. THEREFORE WE WILL NOT FEAR, WHEN THE EARTH SHALL BE TROUBLED; AND THE MOUNTAINS SHALL BE REMOVED INTO THE HEART OF THE SEA.**

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. THEIR WATERS ROARED AND WERE TROUBLED: THE MOUNTAINS WERE TROUBLED WITH HIS STRENGTH.**

[Margin: The moral exposition.]

These two verses are most obscure, but we follow the holy Fathers Basil and Chrysostom. Therefore from this, that the people of God said: "God is our refuge and strength," it concludeth that it will be fearless, even if the earth and the sea be moved up and down, and change their seats with a horrible sound. "Therefore, saith the people of God, we will not fear, when the earth shall be troubled," that is, when haply the earth shall be troubled, or, even if the earth be troubled never so much, "and the mountains shall be removed into the heart of the sea," that is, even if the mountains themselves, which seem so founded and rooted by God that they are judged to be immovable, be shaken, and toss, and be cast headlong into the deep sea, that is, if it be so, if these things be done, "we will not fear," because God almighty is our refuge and our strength. "Their waters roared and were troubled," that is, we will not fear, even if from the removing of the mountains into the heart of the sea the waters of the sea resound and be troubled. Now he saith *eorum* (their), that is, of the seas, because where we have *in cor maris* (into the heart of the sea), in the Hebrew and Greek text there is *in corda marium* (into the hearts of the seas); but our Interpreter changed the plural number into the singular number in the word *maris,* and left the plural number in the pronoun *eorum,* and thence arose the obscurity. "And the mountains were troubled," that is, even if the mountains themselves, removed from the land into the sea, be troubled, namely their stability being lost, with his strength, in the power and might of God. For it is God who alone can trouble the earth, and remove the mountains into the heart of the sea, and make the sea itself and the mountains tremble, according to that of *Ps.* lxxvi, 17: "The waters saw thee, O God, and they were afraid, and the depths were troubled." And *Ps.* ciii: "He looketh upon the earth, and maketh it tremble;" and *Isa.* li: "I am the Lord thy God, who trouble the sea, and the waves thereof swell." And so in these verses is declared the very great trust of the people of God, which will not fear, even if the world fall in ruin; and at the same time is intimated the very great power of God, who can trouble and move all the elements: as in truth he will trouble and move them in the consummation of the world, when "there shall be great earthquakes in divers places, and by reason of the confusion of the roaring of the sea and of the waves men shall wither away for fear," *Luke* xii;[^2] and then the people of God not only will not fear, but will look up, and "lift up its head," as is said in the Gospel, "because its redemption shall be at hand," *Matt.* xxiv.[^3] All these things can be understood figuratively, so that by the earth be understood earthly men; by the mountains, not only the earthly, but also the mighty and proud, such as were the great kings, the enemies of the Church; and by the sea, the bitterness of the tribulation and punishments in which at the last day all the wicked shall be wrapped. And so the earth shall be troubled, when "the wicked lovers of the earth shall be troubled with terrible fear,"[^4] as is said *Wis.* v; "and the mountains shall be removed into the heart of the sea," when kings once great, who persecuted the Church, shall be sunk into the depth of the abyss; and then the waters of the seas shall roar and be troubled, when the last tribulation to come shall in a dreadful manner trouble the wicked, and their kings, with the strength of God, because

then it shall appear how great is the power of the wrath of God against sinners.

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. THE STREAM OF THE RIVER MAKETH THE CITY OF GOD JOYFUL: THE MOST HIGH HATH SANCTIFIED HIS OWN TABERNACLE.**

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. GOD IS IN THE MIDST THEREOF, IT SHALL NOT BE MOVED: GOD WILL HELP IT IN THE MORNING EARLY.**

He showeth now why the people of God will not fear, when the earth shall be troubled, and the mountains shall be removed into the heart of the sea; and he saith that the reason is, because instead of the abundance of bitterness, which shall trouble the wicked, there shall be given to the Church an abundance of delight, which shall make it joyful; and instead of the instability of the mountains, which shall be removed into the heart of the sea, there shall be given to the Church perpetual stability, because God will be in the midst thereof. "The stream of the river (*fluminis impetus,* the rush of the river), saith he, maketh the city of God joyful," that is, the people of God will not fear, when the earth shall be troubled, because the waves of the bitter sea shall not rush in upon the Church; but the sweet and clear waters of the river with a great rush, namely with great plenty, and abundance of delight, shall make it joyful. In the Hebrew there is נהר פלגיו *nahar pelagav, fluminis rivi* (the streams of the river), or *divisiones* (the divisions), for that which we have from the Greek, *fluminis impetus;* but they are not contrary: for streams of waters can flow both with a rush, and without a rush: now the Seventy Interpreters would declare that those streams flow not slackly and thinly, but with great fulness and a rush, to signify the vehemence of the sweetness which overfloweth in the city of God; as in another *Ps.* xxxv it is said: "Thou shalt make them drink of the torrent of thy pleasure." The Prophet addeth: "The most High hath sanctified his own tabernacle," as if he said: Therefore doth God make his city joyful, because he hath chosen it, and sanctified it for a tabernacle to himself, and would have it to be his own proper habitation, according to that of *Apoc.* xxi: "Behold the tabernacle of God with men, and he will dwell with them, and they shall be his people." "God is in the midst thereof, it shall not be moved;" this is opposed to the instability of the earth and of the mountains, as if he said: The city of God will not fear lest haply it be removed into the heart of the sea: for "God is in the midst thereof," that is, God departeth not from it, but is most present with it, as being "in the midst," in the inmost parts, in the heart of it, and therefore this city shall not be moved, but shall be made firm with eternal stability. And he concludeth, showing whence and when these things shall be, saying: "God will help it in the morning early," that is, gladness and stability shall be given to the city of God, because God will help it at the rising of the day, that is, at the beginning of the day of eternal felicity. For Scripture compareth the time of unbelief to the darkness of night, the time of faith to the dawn, the vision of God to the brightness of day. Blessed Paul, *Rom.* xiii: "The night is passed, and the day is at hand." And Blessed Peter, II *Pet.* i: "You have[^5] the more firm prophetical word, whereunto you do well to attend, as to a light that shineth in a dark place, until the day dawn, and the day star arise in your hearts." And the Spouse in *Cant.* chap. ii: "Till the day break, and the shadows retire." And the prophet Malachias, *Mal.* iv: "Unto you that fear my name the Sun of justice shall arise." And with this meaning agreeth aptly enough the ancient reading which St. Augustine hath, and of which St. Ambrose maketh mention, and which the Church embraceth in the ecclesiastical office: *Adjuvabit eam Deus vultu suo* (God will help it with his countenance), that is, he will help it with the greatest and most mighty help of all, when he shall show it his countenance, which shall then be, when the Sun of justice shall manifestly show himself to it, and shall begin that day desired and expected by all the Saints from the beginning of the world; of which the Apostle saith: "I know whom I have believed, and I am certain that he is able to keep that which I have committed unto him, against that day," II *Tim.* i.

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. NATIONS WERE TROUBLED, AND KINGDOMS WERE BOWED DOWN: HE UTTERED HIS VOICE, THE EARTH TREMBLED.**

He foretelleth in proper words what he had before foretold in figurative, namely the destruction of the enemies of the Church, whence universal and perpetual peace shall follow. What therefore he had said before of the earth and the mountains, he now saith more openly of the nations and kingdoms. "Nations, saith he, were troubled," because their destruction draweth near; "and kingdoms were bowed down," that is, cast down from their height, and laid prostrate even to the ground. "He uttered his voice," namely God thundered from heaven, "and the earth trembled (*mota est*)," that is, it quaked, or the earth was melted and made desolate: for the Hebrew word מוג *mug* is *to melt* and *to be dissolved.* Now what is here said of the destruction of the earthly kingdoms was foretold more clearly by Daniel, chap. ii, where it is said that "the kingdom of Christ shall consume all kingdoms, and itself shall stand for ever;" and it was explained by the Apostle, I *Cor.* xv, in those words: "Afterwards the end, when he shall have delivered up the kingdom to God[^6] and the Father, when he shall have brought to nought all principality, and power, and virtue."

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. THE LORD OF ARMIES IS WITH US: THE GOD OF JACOB IS OUR PROTECTOR.**

In all these ruins of nations and kingdoms the people of God will not fear, because it will be able to say: "The Lord of armies (*Dominus virtutum,* the Lord of powers)," that is, of armies (*exercituum*), "is with us; our protector," that is, he who hath undertaken to defend us, "is the God of Jacob." Now he is called *the Lord of powers,* that is, of armies, because all the angels, who are very many and most mighty, obey him at a nod, according to that of *Ps.* cii: "You that are mighty in strength, and execute his word." Nor are the angels only the soldiers of God, but all created things whatsoever: for "fire, hail, snow, ice, stormy winds fulfil his word," as is said in *Ps.* cxlvii,[^7] and as is said in *Ps.* cxviii: "All things serve him." And so two arguments are set down in this verse, by which it is most openly shown that the people of God can, and ought to, fear nothing: the first is, because the most mighty Lord, for whom all things fight, is the helper of this people. The other, because this same Lord not only can,

<!-- p. 305 (PDF 316) -->

but also will help his people, since he would be called the God of Jacob, that is, the God of this people, which is called Jacob from that holy patriarch, most dear to God, of whose offspring he vouchsafed to take flesh.

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. COME AND BEHOLD YE THE WORKS OF THE LORD: WHAT WONDERS HE HATH DONE UPON EARTH, MAKING WARS TO CEASE EVEN TO THE END OF THE EARTH.**

He exhorteth all nations to think upon the wonderful works of God, and that above all which shall be in the last time, when, all enemies being taken away, or rather all being made subject under the footstool of the feet of Christ, there shall be an end of all wars, and God alone shall reign, none any more resisting, or rebelling. Which kingdom we look for and ask, when we daily say in the Lord's prayer: "Thy kingdom come." "Come, saith he, and behold," that is, draw near by faith, and contemplate with the eyes of the mind, and consider "the works of the Lord, what wonders he hath done upon earth," that is, consider the works which God will do (for the past is here put for the future after the manner of the prophets) upon the earth, so admirable, and astonishing, that they may be named prodigies (*prodigia*). Now he will do these wonders "making wars to cease even to the end of the earth;" for it is truly wonderful that wars could be taken away out of the whole earth even to its uttermost ends, and so taken away that they cannot be renewed for ever. From the Hebrew St. Jerome translateth, *quantas posuerit desolationes in terra* (how great desolations he hath made in the earth), for that which we read, *quæ posuit prodigia super terram* (what wonders he hath done upon earth): and the sense is the same; for from the desolation of the whole earth, the just being carried over into heaven, and the wicked thrust down into hell, shall arise the ceasing of wars in the whole earth. Yet the Hebrew word שמות *shammoth* signifieth both, *desolations* and *things to be wondered at,* or *prodigies:* for the word שמם *shamam* signifieth *to wonder* and *to be made desolate;* wherefore the Seventy Interpreters rightly translated *prodigia,* since the Hebrew word signifieth that, although it can also signify desolations.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. HE SHALL DESTROY THE BOW, AND BREAK THE WEAPONS: AND THE SHIELD HE SHALL BURN IN THE FIRE.**

He declareth what he said, *making wars to cease;* and saith that this shall be, because the Lord will destroy all arms, as well those which are used in striking, such as are bows and lances, as those which soldiers use for their own defence, such as are shields: now without arms wars cannot be waged. In the Hebrew for the word *arma* (weapons) there is in particular חנית *chanith, hasta* (a spear), or *lancea* (a lance); but the Seventy chose rather to translate *arma,* because without doubt the Prophet would by the name of bow and lance signify all offensive arms; and so the sense is, *he shall destroy the bow, and break the rest of the weapons.* But for the word *scuta* (shields) there is in the Hebrew *currus* (chariots), and indeed the sense is the same, because both shields and chariots of war, and all the like instruments of war, are foretold to be burnt: yet it is to be known that the Hebrew word עגלות *hagaloth* signifieth *chariots,* because chariots are round; for that word properly signifieth *roundness.* Because therefore shields also are round, and shields are better joined with lances and bows than chariots, therefore the Seventy prudently translated *scuta.* These two verses some expound of the temporal peace which was in the time of Augustus, or of Constantine; but they agree far better with the eternal peace which shall be at the end of the world, when the Church shall cease to be militant, and shall begin to be triumphant, namely all her enemies being on every side subdued.

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. BE STILL AND SEE THAT I AM GOD; I WILL BE EXALTED AMONG THE NATIONS, AND I WILL BE EXALTED IN THE EARTH.**

[Margin: The contemplation of divine things requireth a mind free from perturbations.]

A little before the Prophet had said: "Come and behold ye the works of the Lord." Now, that he may show how one must come, if any man would understand the works of the Lord, he saith: "Be still (*vacate,* be at leisure) and see," and, that he may the more persuade, he bringeth in God himself speaking and exhorting: "Be still and see that I am God." For the contemplation of divine things requireth a mind free from the tumult of worldly perturbations; now the root of all perturbations is covetousness (*cupiditas*): for from the desire of riches, of pleasures, of honours, and of the like things, arise most troublesome occupations, which leave no rest at all to a man so affected. Hence Jeremias saith of the contemplative man: "He shall sit solitary, and hold his peace, because he hath lifted himself above himself,"[^8] *Lam.* iii; and the Lord commandeth, *Matt.* vi: "But thou when thou shalt pray, enter into thy chamber, and having shut the door, pray to thy Father." For what it is to pray to the Father, the door being shut, he taught us by his own example, who for the most part, when about to pray, went up into the mountains, and prayed alone, namely removed from all the noise of cares and businesses. But, as we said, the chief leisure ought to be from the concupiscence of earthly things: for he who desireth nothing of those things which are seen, even if he be much occupied in the help of his neighbours, will easily recollect his mind, when he will, and will be still, and will see that the Lord himself alone is God, he the beginning and the end, he the whole hope of the faithful on earth, and their true felicity in heaven. For David also was most occupied in governing the kingdom, and St. Gregory (as also very many other holy Pontiffs) in governing the pontificate; and yet they were lifted up in most high contemplations, because they bore the wings of the mind free from the mire of concupiscence. The Apostle himself also was pressed with the solicitude of all the Churches, and was exercised with the labour of his hands to seek his living; and yet because he was most free from worldly desires, he not only was still, and saw, but was even rapt to the third heaven, and heard secret words, which it is not granted to man to utter. But on the contrary there are many men idle, as to the occupations of businesses; but because they are carnal, and entangled in worldly desires, they know not how to be at leisure for God. He saith therefore: "Be still," that is, seek a holy leisure, and bring a mind

[Margin: Who and how great is God?]

pure and tranquil, and see, thinking most attentively, "that I am God," that is, I am God alone, and no created thing, however great and lofty it seem, is God; I alone am that which is; I alone am he "of whom are all things, by whom are all things, and in whom are all things," *Rom.* xi; I alone, without whom you can do nothing, and are nothing, and in whom you can do all things, and truly are. "I will be exalted among the nations, and I will be exalted in the earth," that is, when I shall have done the wonders which were spoken of above, I shall appear lofty before all nations, and before the whole world, so that every knee shall bow, of those that are in heaven, on earth, and under the earth. For at the end of the world there shall be none at all who dareth to despise God; but all, either willing or unwilling, shall acknowledge his supreme empire, and shall be made subject to him. St. Basil and some others mystically interpret this exaltation of the exaltation of Christ on the cross, so that the sense is: "Be still and see that I," who seem to be man, as other men, yet truly "am God, and I shall be exalted among the nations, and in the earth," hung upon the cross for ignominy and punishment, in the eyes of the wicked. But truly by this very cross I shall be exalted in all nations, and in all the earth, because I will draw all things to myself, and to the yoke of my faith and obedience even the highest kings themselves with their peoples shall bow their necks. In the Hebrew there is, *cessate et videte* (cease and see), which can be understood of ceasing from worldly businesses and desires, as we said, and we judge that to be the literal and proper exposition: yet the opinion of those is not to be rejected who will have an exhortation to the wicked to be contained in these words, so that the sense is: Since those things are to be which were explained a little before, *cease now* at length from the sin of unbelief, and from the persecutions of the godly, *and see* with the eyes of faith *that I am God,* and your idols are not gods; for I alone at the end of the world *shall be exalted among the nations, and in all the earth.*

[Margin: Verse 11.]

**11. THE LORD OF ARMIES IS WITH US: THE GOD OF JACOB IS OUR PROTECTOR.**

He concludeth the Psalm with the repetition of the seventh verse, that he may show that the affection of the godly was stirred up again and renewed by that exhortation of God.
