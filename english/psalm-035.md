BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XXXV.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*Unto the end, for the servant of God, David.*

Of this title it hath been spoken in the explanation of Psalm IV, where *unto the end* is first read. The sense therefore is: A Psalm inspired to David the servant of the Lord, which is to be sung continually unto the end, and which pertaineth to Christ, who is the end of the law and of the Prophets. The argument is, that the malice of certain men is well-nigh immeasurable, but the mercy of God, and his justice, much greater.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. THE UNJUST HATH SAID WITHIN HIMSELF, THAT HE WOULD SIN: THERE IS NO FEAR OF GOD BEFORE HIS EYES.**[^1]

[Margin: The two first roots of sin.]

The Prophet describeth the two first roots of sin, of which one is in the will, which determineth to do evil; the other in the understanding, which thinketh not on the fear of the Lord who forbiddeth sin. "The unjust, saith he, hath said within himself, that he would sin," that is, with himself, in his heart, the unjust hath determined to sin, that is, in his heart he hath consented to sin. "There is no fear of God before his eyes," that is, he therefore chose sin in his heart, because with the eyes of the mind he thought not on the fear of the Lord who seeth all things. Where fear is put for the object of fear, that is, he thought not on God as just, strong, seeing all things: from which thought he would without doubt have conceived fear of so great a power. For as he who feareth a man dareth not do evil outwardly in his sight: so he who feareth God dareth not think it inwardly, because God searcheth hearts also. As to the words, the three first Hebrew words might thus be translated to the letter, *dictum prævaricationis impii* (the saying of the transgression of the wicked); but that word, נאם, although it properly signifieth *dictum* (a saying), yet is taken in many places for the verb *dixit* (hath said); and that, פשע, which signifieth *prævaricationem* (transgression), the points which have been set to it by the Rabbins being changed, may be read פושע *poseuh,* that is *prævaricator* (a transgressor), or *injustus* (unjust); so also that, לרשע, which St. Jerome translateth, *impii* (of the wicked), the points being changed, will signify *ad delinquendum* (to sin), or *ut delinquat* (that he may sin), as the Greek and Latin copies have. And this without doubt is the true reading, which is confirmed by the letter ל *lamed,* which is the sign of a verb of the infinitive mood, and not of a noun in the genitive case. And that, *in semetipso* (within himself), is in the Hebrew, *in medio* לבי *cordis mei* (in the midst of my heart); but the Hebrew writing seemeth corrupted by the change of one letter: for St. Jerome himself also translateth, *cordis ejus* (of his heart), not *cordis mei.* And so it seemeth that we should read לבו, that is *corde suo* (his own heart).

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. FOR IN HIS SIGHT HE HATH DONE DECEITFULLY, THAT HIS INIQUITY MAY BE FOUND UNTO HATRED.**

In this verse the Prophet proveth what he said, that the unjust hath not the fear of the Lord. "For, saith he, in the sight of God himself he hath done deceitfully" with God himself and with all men, so grievously, "that his iniquity may" not "be found" with God unto pardon, but "unto hatred," which surely he would never have done, if he had feared God. For who would dare to sin in the sight of the judge, if he feared the judge? The Hebrew and Greek words can make another sense, namely, that the unjust dealeth deceitfully when he seemeth to wish to return to his heart, and to confess his sins; for he doth not seek out his sins, while he searcheth the secrets of his conscience, that he may truly hate them, but only that he may deceive God, or men, and this sense St. Augustine followeth. The Hebrew words sound thus: *Quoniam dolose egit adversus eum in oculis ejus ad inveniendam iniquitatem ejus, ad odiendum* (For he hath dealt deceitfully against him in his eyes, to find his iniquity, to hate). And the Greek have thus: *Quoniam dolose egit in conspectu ejus, invenire iniquitatem suam, et odisse* (For he hath dealt deceitfully in his sight, to find his own iniquity, and to hate). Now although the latter sense is true, and can be fitted to the words of the Prophet, yet the former sense is more universal,

<!-- p. 222 (PDF 233) -->

and is better joined with what goeth before, and agreeth more with the Latin Vulgate edition, and can without difficulty be accommodated to the Hebrew and Greek words. For that which is said in the Hebrew copy, *ad inveniendum iniquitatem ejus ad odium* (to find his iniquity unto hatred), maketh this sense: That God may find his iniquity to hate it, that is, not to spare, but to punish. And that which is said in the Greek copy, *invenire iniquitatem suam, et odisse,* may also be read, *invenire iniquitatem ejus, et odisse* (to find his iniquity, and to hate), that is, that God may find his iniquity, and not pardon, but hate, and punish it; for the Greeks have no distinction between *ejus* (his) and *suam* (his own), as we Latins have; accordingly that αὐτοῦ, which St. Augustine readeth *suam,* our Interpreter readeth *ejus.*

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. THE WORDS OF HIS MOUTH ARE INIQUITY AND GUILE: HE WOULD NOT UNDERSTAND THAT HE MIGHT DO WELL.**

The Prophet hath said that the wicked man dealeth deceitfully, now he addeth that he also speaketh deceitfully, and will afterwards add that he also thinketh deceitfully, that on every side may be made manifest the notable perversity of him who feareth not God. "The words, saith he, of his mouth" are conformable to his deeds, namely unjust, yea so unjust that they are nothing else but "iniquity and guile;" whatsoever he speaketh tendeth to this, either to hurt openly, or to deceive. "He would not understand that he might do well," that is, neither can he excuse his fault by pretending ignorance: for he was ignorant wilfully, since he cared not to learn the law of justice, either by searching it out of himself, or by asking of others, because he had determined to do evil: that he might do evil, he despised the knowledge of living well.

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. HE HATH DEVISED INIQUITY ON HIS BED, HE HATH SET HIMSELF ON EVERY WAY THAT IS NOT GOOD: BUT EVIL HE HATH NOT HATED.**

In backward order he hath described first the unjust works, then the words of iniquity, now at last he describeth the evil thoughts and affections; for although out of the heart, as is in the Gospel, *Matt.* xiii,[^2] proceed evil words and works, yet we, from the evil works and words which we see and hear, come to know the evil thoughts and wills, which we cannot see and hear. "He hath devised iniquity, saith he, on his bed," that is, the works and words of iniquity he did not commit or pour forth by chance or unawares, but long before meditated them in the secret of his bed. "He hath set himself on every way that is not good: but evil he hath not hated," that is, while he meditated in his heart, and divers counsels presented themselves, as it were ways to work by, he approved all the evil counsels, and so began to enter upon every way that is not good, because his will is corrupted, and therefore he hated not evil, but rather loved it, not because it is evil, but because it seemed profitable. That, *omni viæ non bonæ* (on every way that is not good), is put for *omni viæ malæ* (on every evil way), as though he said: No good counsel pleased him; but on the contrary he chose to follow all evil counsels, and so *astitit viæ non bonæ* (he set himself on a way not good), that is, he stood in a way not good.

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. O LORD, THY MERCY IS IN HEAVEN, AND THY TRUTH REACHETH, EVEN TO THE CLOUDS.**

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. THY JUSTICE IS AS THE MOUNTAINS OF GOD, THY JUDGMENTS ARE A GREAT DEEP.**

[Margin: Mercy and truth and justice are infinite in God.]

The Prophet passeth to the other part of the Psalm, and showeth that the malice of some men is indeed great; but that greater is the goodness in God, which consisteth in his mercy and justice. And of mercy he saith: "Thy mercy is in heaven," that is, so great is thy mercy, that it reacheth from earth to heaven, and filleth all things: which is had more clearly in *Ps.* cvii: "Thy mercy is great above the heavens." To mercy he joineth truth, that is, faithfulness, which causeth that to be kept which is mercifully promised; of which it is said in *Ps.* cxliv: "The Lord is faithful in all his words." "And thy truth reacheth, even to the clouds," that is, mercy reacheth even unto heaven with its companion truth, which itself also is extended "even to the clouds," that is, even unto the heaven in which the clouds are. Nor is justice less in God, by which he rendereth to every one according to his works. For "thy justice, saith he, is as the mountains of God," that is, it is great after the manner of the highest mountains, which sometimes surpass the clouds. For great things are called things of God, as in another place, *Ps.* lxxix: "As the cedars of God."[^3] To justice he joineth judgments, which are the acts of justice, and saith: "Thy judgments are a great deep," that is, profound and unsearchable, even as a most deep gulf, which is called *abyssus* (the deep), cannot be pierced by the eyes. By all these bodily similitudes, of the height and depth of the divine mercy and justice, as also of truth and of judgments, nothing else is signified but that the mercy and justice of God are greater than can be perfectly understood by us, even as with bodily eyes we reach not to the things which are above the clouds, or which are beneath the earth.

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. MEN AND BEASTS THOU WILT PRESERVE, O LORD: O HOW HAST THOU MULTIPLIED THY MERCY, O GOD!**

The Prophet now showeth that the mercy of God is very great, since he preserveth (*salvat*) men and beasts, that is, keepeth, nourisheth, filleth with bodily goods, not only men, that is, those who use reason; but also beasts, that is, those who after the manner of beasts are led by sense and appetite alone, whose malice he explained above. And truly infinite and astonishing is the mercy and kindness of God, who, when he might most justly destroy the wicked and blasphemers, and bring them to nothing, yet at that very time in which they blaspheme and curse God, and transgress all the commandments of God, God upholdeth them as it were with his own hands, beareth and feedeth them, filleth them with enjoyments, maketh his sun to rise upon them, and with rains from heaven watereth their fields

<!-- p. 223 (PDF 234) -->

and gardens. And that, *quemadmodum multiplicasti misericordiam tuam, Deus* (how hast thou multiplied thy mercy, O God), is of one wondering, as though he said: O how hast thou multiplied thy mercy, O God! For the Hebrew copy hath, מה יקר חסדך, *quam pretiosa est misericordia tua, Deus!* (how precious is thy mercy, O God!) Nor do the Greek copies disagree; and our Latin edition easily admitteth this sense. And the original text is the best commentary, when our version can be explained from it.

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. BUT THE CHILDREN OF MEN SHALL PUT THEIR TRUST UNDER THE COVERT OF THY WINGS.**

[Margin: The singular providence of God toward godly men.]

He hath spoken of mercy toward the wicked and carnal men, whom he judged fit to be called by the name of beasts. Now he speaketh of mercy toward the godly and spiritual, whom he calleth children of men: which may also be called justice in respect of the wicked, whom he hath justly decreed to be deprived of goods so excellent. He saith therefore: "But the children of men shall put their trust under the covert of thy wings," that is, the beasts indeed will have to be content with bodily safety, which alone also they have known, and alone have desired. But the children of men, as chickens under the wings of the hen, so they, gathered and resting under thy wings, O most loving God, shall hope for all good things from thee alone. By which words is signified a certain especial providence and singular benevolence of God toward godly men, and on the other side a certain supreme and most secure confidence of the godly toward God, such as is the care of the hen in cherishing her chickens, and the confidence of the chickens under the wings of the hen. Which similitude, since it is most apt, the same Prophet often useth, as *Ps.* lxii: "Under the covert of thy wings I will hope:[^4] my soul hath stuck close to thee;" and *Ps.* xc: "He will overshadow thee with his shoulders: and under his wings thou shalt trust." But how sweet it is, and how much to be preferred before all human pleasures, to be cherished under the wings of God, and to experience his fatherly, or motherly, loving-kindness, no man knoweth, but he that receiveth it.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. THEY SHALL BE INEBRIATED WITH THE PLENTY OF THY HOUSE; AND THOU SHALT MAKE THEM DRINK OF THE TORRENT OF THY PLEASURE.**

[Margin: Eternal felicity satisfieth.]

The protection under the wings of God is had in this world, where there is danger from the bites of kites and of wild beasts: but that which followeth shall be given us in the heavenly country; and the Prophet explaineth those unspeakable goods in the manner he can, that is, by bodily similitudes. And first indeed he taketh a similitude from the receiver himself: then from that thing which is received. The receiver himself is then content, when he is full, so that he desireth nothing more. This fulness, satisfying every appetite, is most aptly described in this place by drunkenness. For he who is greedy of wine is then at last truly satisfied, when he is drunk; for then he thirsteth no more for wine, but is dissolved in sleep. So therefore we also, who in this life are never satisfied, nor at rest, how much soever temporal goods increase, shall then at last be full, and satisfied, and thereby blessed, when we shall be inebriated with the plenty of the goods which are in the house of God; because then, every appetite being satisfied, we shall be dissolved into the sleep of everlasting rest. And he saith, *inebriabuntur ab ubertate* (they shall be inebriated with the plenty), and saith not, *inebriabuntur a vino* (they shall be inebriated with wine), because he would have us understand that drunkenness is not taken properly in this place, but as it signifieth full satiety. Then he bringeth a similitude from the thing itself which is given and received, saying: "And thou shalt make them drink of the torrent of thy pleasure." In a torrent there are three things: a very great multitude of waters coming down from the mountains; a sudden flood: for where a little before there was no water, suddenly a huge river is beheld; lastly the violence of the waters running down, and sweeping with them all things in their way. So will be the heavenly beatitude: there will be present a multitude of wisdom and knowledge coming down from that mountain of which it is said, *Ecclus.* i: "The word of God on high is the fountain of wisdom," that is, on the high mountain of the divinity is the fountain of wisdom, the Word of God: from which mountain and fountain a sudden flood ariseth in the Blessed. For we who in this life with great labour, and in much time, drew wisdom as it were drop by drop, there suddenly, and in a moment, God being clearly seen, shall abound with all knowledge, not only of created things, but also of all the attributes of the Creator himself; which abundance of wisdom and knowledge will sweep the mind with great violence to the love and fruition of the supreme good. For in our country it will not be free to love, or not to love God; to enjoy, or not to enjoy so great a good: but there will be a most happy necessity of cleaving to the supreme good, and of enjoying it with infinite sweetness by an inmost clasping.

[Margin: How shall we, when Blessed, draw wisdom?]

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. FOR WITH THEE IS THE FOUNTAIN OF LIFE; AND IN THY LIGHT WE SHALL SEE LIGHT.**

[Margin: God is the fountain of life.]

He giveth the reason why from the vision of God there will be in the Blessed so great a flood of wisdom and knowledge: namely because *with God is the fountain of life,* which is the same with the fountain of wisdom. God therefore, who is the fountain of wisdom, is by this very thing the fountain of life, because for the wise to be wise is to live: and because he is the fountain of life, by this very thing he is the fountain of being, because for the living to live is to be. And he is called the fountain of wisdom, of life, and of being, because he hath it not from another to be wise, or to live, or to be; but he himself is wisdom, is life, is being; and all other things, which in any manner are wise, live, or are, have this from him. And David useth the word fountain, that he may continue in the metaphor of the torrent, as though he said: Thou shalt make us drink of the torrent, because with thee is the fountain whence that torrent cometh down. And he saith: "With thee is the fountain of life," when it seemed that he ought to have said, *the fountain of wisdom,* because he would show that eternal life, promised to the just, and desired by all as the supreme good, consisteth in this supreme wisdom, according to the word of the Lord, *John* xvii: "Now this is eternal life: That they may know thee, the only true God, and Jesus Christ, whom thou hast sent." And he addeth: "In thy light we shall see light," that at length he may explain in clearer words what he had shadowed forth by metaphors. For "in thy light we shall see light,"

<!-- p. 224 (PDF 235) -->

signifieth nothing else but: In thyself, who art light, and the fountain of light, we shall see thee, the light inaccessible and unfailing. For now we see God, but in the glass of creatures; in our mind, which is the image of God, by reasoning, and passing in discourse from the works to the knowledge of the workman; lastly in faith, not in sight: but then we shall see God in himself, and, as the Apostle saith, I *Cor.* xiii, "face to face;" and, as blessed John speaketh, I *John* iii: "We shall see him as he is," not as he is represented in an image. And since, as the same John saith, I *John* i: "God is light, and in him there is no darkness," therefore it is rightly said, "in thy light," that is, in thy very divinity, which is light, not in figures and riddles, "we shall see light," that is thyself, "who art the true light, which enlighteneth every man that cometh into this world," *John* i. Rightly also do theologians gather from this place the light of glory, which they hold to be necessary for seeing God. For God *is* a certain *light,* but *inaccessible,* as blessed Paul saith, I *Tim.* vi; and therefore unless the mind be raised up, and strengthened by a certain gift of God, which they call the light of glory, it cannot fix its gaze upon that uncreated light: accordingly we shall indeed see the light, which is God himself, but *in his light,* that is, helped by the light of glory, which he giveth to those whom he vouchsafeth to admit to the vision of himself. But the former explanation seemeth more literal.

[Margin: The light of glory is necessary for seeing God.]

[Margin: Verse 11.]

**11. EXTEND THY MERCY TO THEM THAT KNOW THEE, AND THY JUSTICE TO THEM THAT ARE RIGHT IN HEART.**

The Prophet now teacheth that these great goods, of which he hath hitherto spoken, pertain to the just alone, whom a little before he called children of man, when he had called the wicked by the name of beasts. The Prophet indeed useth a form of speaking of the imperative mood, but with the prophets that form hath the force of the indicative mood, as we have often said: "Extend, saith he, thy mercy to them that know thee," that is, stretch forth this thy great mercy to them that know, that is, to those only who know thee with the knowledge of familiarity and friendship; who converse with thee, call upon thee, who hear thee in thy precepts, and whom thou hearest in their prayers: In which manner of speaking it is said in the Gospel, *Matt.* xxv: "Amen I say to you, I know you not." "And thy justice to them that are right in heart," that is, stretch forth, or hold out this mercy, which is at the same time a crown of justice, to them "that are right in heart," that is, to the just and godly, who have a heart right and conformable to thy uprightness, and therefore all thy commandments and thy judgments please them. For the Prophet asketh the same thing after his manner in different words: for they that know God with the knowledge of friendship are themselves the right in heart; and eternal felicity is mercy and justice: mercy indeed, because by the gift of grace we have been made just from wicked, and friends from enemies, and right in heart from crooked and awry, and workers of good works from workers of iniquity; but yet the same felicity is also a crown of justice, and the reward of good works: for, as is had in the second Council of Orange, *Can.* xvi:[^5] A reward is due to good works, if they be done; but mercy, which is not due, goeth before, that they may be done.

[Margin: Verse 12.]

**12. LET NOT THE FOOT OF PRIDE COME TO ME, AND LET NOT THE HAND OF THE SINNER MOVE ME.**

The Prophet, now anxious for himself, lest perchance he fall away from so great a good, prayeth God for the gift of perseverance, and especially against the vice of pride, to which great men are subject, such as he himself was. "Let not, saith he, the foot of pride come to me," that is, suffer not, I beseech thee, that the foot of the proud come near me, lest perchance their hand move me by word, or example, or other temptation, and cast me down from the state of grace into the hole of sin. By the name of the proud one and the sinner, whose foot and hand, that is, whose approach and power he feareth, is understood chiefly the devil, who is king over all the children of pride; and then also his ministers and attendants. It might also be thus expounded with St. Augustine: "Let not the foot of pride come to me," that is, let it not befall me that I begin to have the foot of pride, that is the affection of pride; "and let not the hand of the sinner move me," that is, and let not the working of the sinner lead me to consent to sin, and so either by my own vice, or by the temptation of others, cast me down from my state, and I fall.

[Margin: Verse 13.]

**13. THERE THE WORKERS OF INIQUITY ARE FALLEN, THEY ARE CAST OUT, AND COULD NOT STAND.**

[Margin: All perdition took its beginning from pride.]

He giveth the reason why he so greatly feareth for himself from pride: because, saith he, "there the workers of iniquity are fallen," that is, from "pride all perdition took its beginning," as Tobias saith, chap. iv; for both the first angel with his companions, and the first man with his wife, fell through pride, and by them sin entered into the world. And after they fell from justice to iniquity, they were cast out by God from the place of felicity, and thrown down into the depth of miseries. For "God resisteth the proud, but to the humble he giveth grace," as the Apostles say, Peter in *epist.* I, chap. v, and James, chap. iv. "And could not stand," in that place of felicity in which God had set them, that he might advance them to greater things, if they persevered in good.
