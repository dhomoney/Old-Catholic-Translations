BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XXXIII.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*For David, when he changed his countenance before Abimelech, who dismissed him, and he went his way.*[^1]

This title may be referred to two histories, which are found in I *Kings,* chap. xxi. The former history relateth that David, fleeing from the face of king Saul, came to Achimelech the priest, and asked of him loaves and arms, and did not make known to him his flight, but feigned that he had a secret business of the king, for which he made such haste. The latter history telleth that David fled for refuge to Achis the Philistine king, but that when the servants of that king had begun to stir up their lord to the death of David, then David began to feign madness and folly, and by that means escaped death. Theodoret referreth the title to the former history; but St. Basil, St. Augustine, St. Jerome, and Euthymius refer it to the latter. And this is the truer opinion: *first,* because in the former David did not change his countenance, but his office; but in the latter he changed his countenance, that he might seem to be what he was not. *Secondly,* because where we read: *Et dimisit eum* (who dismissed him), the Hebrew hath ויגרשהו, which signifieth, *et expulit eum* (and he drove him out); or, *et projecit eum* (and he cast him forth); or, *et repudiavit eum* (and he put him away), which in no wise agreeth with the former history, but only with the latter: for Achimelech the priest did not drive David out, but king Achis drove him out, as a madman and one raving. Whereby is refuted also the opinion of those who so understand the words *dimisit eum,* that David dismissed king Achis, or the priest Achimelech: for David did not dismiss, that is, drive out, but was driven out and dismissed. *Thirdly,* because in this Psalm David giveth thanks to God for having delivered him out of a great danger: but David suffered no danger with Achimelech, and a very great one with Achis. *Fourthly,* because in the title of this Psalm, according to the credit of the Hebrew and Greek copies, there is not *Achimelech,* as there ought to be if the title regarded the former history; but *Abimelech.* And so read and expound Basil, Augustine and the other ancients, Theodoret excepted. The Latin copies also of the Vulgate edition which I have seen have *Abimelech,* except the edition of Sixtus V, in which there is *Achimelech,* which seemeth to have been done by the fault of the copyists, or of the printers, since it is against all the copies, and it was easy to err by reason of the likeness of the words. And why Achis is called *Abimelech,* St. Basil giveth the reason: *Abimelech* was a name common to the kings of the Philistines, as Pharao was a name common to the kings of the Egyptians, and Cæsar to the emperors of the Romans. And St. Basil proveth that *Abimelech* was a name common to the kings of the Philistines from chap. xx and xxvi of *Gen.* Moreover for that which is said in the title, *immutavit vultum suum* (he changed his countenance), the Hebrew hath בשנותו את טעמו *beschanutho eth tahhemo,* that is, *immutavit sensum suum* (he changed his sense), which cometh to the same; for to change the sense is in this place to feign folly, and of a man of sense to make himself senseless, which David did by writhing his mouth and eyes, and by falling down, and by stumbling against the doors of the house after the manner of drunken men, and by letting forth abundance of spittle after the likeness of epileptics: for these things are related in I *Kings,* chap. xxi. The argument of the Psalm is to give thanks to God for the benefit of deliverance from that danger of imminent death, and on this occasion to exhort all to love God, and to hope in him. For the godly man understood that all that industry of his, which he used in feigning folly, would have profited little without the divine help and favour.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. I WILL BLESS THE LORD AT ALL TIMES, HIS PRAISE SHALL BE ALWAYS IN MY MOUTH.**[^2]

The Psalm is alphabetical: for the first verse beginneth with the first letter of the alphabet, the second with the second, and so of the rest; which seemeth to have been done that it might the more easily be committed to memory, and be often sung by the faithful. He beginneth therefore to give thanks with great affection, as though he said: At no time will I ever forget the benefit of this day, but for it: "I will bless the Lord at all times," that is, as long as I shall live; and repeating the same: "His praise, saith he, shall be always in my mouth." Therefore the word *always* doth not signify at every moment, every day, every night, as though nothing else were to be done; but it signifieth that this is to be done in its own place and time, but even to the end of life; nay, even to the end of the world these Psalms shall be sung, and so through others David shall bless God at all times. This place may also be understood spiritually, that the just praise God always, both when they receive benefits, and when they suffer scourges, according to that of holy Job, chap. i: "The Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away: blessed be the name of the Lord."

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. IN THE LORD SHALL MY SOUL BE PRAISED: LET THE MEEK HEAR AND REJOICE.**

Nor shall I alone, saith he, bless the Lord for this benefit at all times, but others also shall bless him: for whosoever shall hear this will praise me, that by so wonderful an art I eluded that king, and withal they will praise and bless God, who by that art delivered me from the danger of death. "In the Lord shall my soul be praised," that is, I shall be praised by all to whom the report of this thing hath come; but I shall be praised in the Lord, and thereby the chief praise will be referred to the Lord, who by his singular providence inspired me with good counsel, and helped, that it might obtain a happy effect. In the Hebrew the verb *laudabitur* (shall be praised) is of a certain conjugation which signifieth an action upon oneself, and so according to the property of the Hebrew word, *laudabitur anima mea* signifieth: My soul shall be praised by itself, that is, shall glory; and because the

<!-- p. 203 (PDF 214) -->

[Margin: It is not always a fault to glory, and to praise one's own deeds.][^3]

soul is put for the whole man, the sense will be: I myself for this deed will glory wonderfully, not in myself, but in the Lord, by whose protection and help I escaped the danger. We are admonished from this place that it is not always a fault to glory, and to praise one's own deeds, but then only, when either we glory in a thing which deserveth not praise, or we acknowledge not the primary author of all goods, God. "He that glorieth, saith the Apostle, II *Cor.* x, 17, let him glory in the Lord. For not he who commendeth himself, is approved, but he, whom God commendeth." That which followeth, "let the meek hear and rejoice," signifieth that the sharing of this gladness belongeth properly to those to whom like dangers are wont to be common, such as are mild and patient men: for they who are such are both often oppressed by the more powerful, and easily find by experience that God is their helper. "Let the meek, saith he, hear," the humble, those that fear God, those like me, what hath befallen me, "and rejoice," and bless the Lord. In the Hebrew these verbs are of the future tense, *audient, et lætabuntur* (they shall hear, and shall rejoice); but because the imperative among the Hebrews lacketh the third person, to express it the Hebrews use the third person of the future: therefore the Greek and Latin interpreters rightly translated, *audiant, et lætentur* (let them hear, and rejoice).

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. O MAGNIFY THE LORD WITH ME; AND LET US EXTOL HIS NAME TOGETHER.**

He turneth his speech to the meek themselves, whom he had bidden hear and rejoice, and exhorteth them not only to praise God each one by himself, but joined with him, and united together, to glorify the Lord. "O magnify the Lord with me," that is, let us confess that the Lord, who alone is truly great, is great; and him who alone is truly most high let us proclaim with our voice also to be most high; "and his name," that is, his knowledge and fame, or his power and majesty, let us extol before men. For it greatly pleaseth God that the faithful, not only apart in their chambers, but also joined together in the Churches, "with one mind, and with one mouth glorify him," as the Apostle warneth, *Rom.* xv. The words *in idipsum* (together), as we have often said already, signify nothing else but *together, as one, jointly,* etc.

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. I SOUGHT THE LORD, AND HE HEARD ME; AND HE DELIVERED ME FROM ALL MY TROUBLES.**

The Prophet now explaineth the cause why he willeth to bless the Lord at all times: namely because he hath found him by experience the best and most mighty deliverer. "I sought the Lord," that is, when I was pressed by straits, I fled for refuge to the Lord, I sought his help, I came to him with confidence, "and he heard me," with his wonted kindness and mercy, and the effect of that hearing showed itself in this, that "he delivered me from all my troubles," as though he would say: King Saul endeavoured to slay me, both with his own hand and by his guards, and by the protection of the Lord I escaped his hands; and fleeing, by reason of haste I could take neither arms nor food, and forthwith the mercy of the Lord was present, which provided me with loaves and arms by Achimelech the priest: a little after I fell through imprudence into the hands of Achis, king of the Philistines, but the same God inspiring, helping and protecting, I turned aside the danger by a wonderful and unwonted art. Therefore "from all the troubles" which have hitherto befallen me the most kind Lord and most loving father, God, "delivered me."

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. COME YE TO HIM AND BE ENLIGHTENED: AND YOUR FACES SHALL NOT BE CONFOUNDED.**

Here now beginneth a most beautiful and most effectual exhortation to the love and fear of God, and to the casting of all our care upon him. "Come ye to him," or, as the Hebrew word soundeth, *respicite in eum* (look ye upon him), that is, as many of you as are in tribulation, *come* by faith and prayer to God, *look* to his help; "and be enlightened," that is, receive the light of consolation and gladness, the sadness that cloudeth being cast away: for light signifieth gladness, according to that of *Ps.* xcvi: "Light is risen to the just, and joy (*lætitia*) to the right of heart." And this place may be thus expounded in a higher and mystical sense: "Come ye to him" by conversion, "and be enlightened" by the grace of justification; for the divine enlightening conferreth spiritual life; whence the Apostle saith, *Ephes.* v: "Rise thou that sleepest, and arise from the dead: and Christ shall enlighten thee;" and Christ himself saith, *John* viii, 12: "He that followeth me, walketh not in darkness, but shall have the light of life;" and in *Ps.* lv[^4] we read: "With thee is the fountain of life; and in thy light we shall see light;" where *the fountain of life* and *the fountain of light* are put for the same. Lastly, baptism is therefore called by the ancients *enlightening,* because by it men dead by sin are born again, and come from the darkness of death to the light of life. *Come ye* therefore *to him* by conversion and penance, and he will be turned to you, and with his countenance most bright, and most full of life, as being the uncreated sun and the fountain of life, he *will enlighten* and quicken you. "And your faces shall not be confounded," that is, come ye securely, fear not a repulse, he will hear and receive you; nor will he suffer your faces to be confounded with shame. For the face is said to be confounded when he who asketh obtaineth not, and therefore departeth with shame. So in III *Kings,* chap. ii, Bethsabee saith to king Solomon: "I desire one small petition of thee, do not put me to confusion" (*ne confundas faciem meam,* confound not my face). In the Hebrew, for *accedite ad eum, et illuminamini, et facies vestræ non confundentur* (come ye to him and be enlightened, and your faces shall not be confounded), there is, *respexerunt ad eum, et illuminati sunt, et facies eorum non confundentur* (they looked upon him, and were enlightened, and their faces shall not be confounded); but it is easy to see what the Seventy read: for the word הביטו *hibbitu, respexerunt* (they looked), they read *habitu, respicite* (look ye), as St. Jerome also read; and because the coming to God by conversion is a coming of the inward eyes, not of the feet,

<!-- p. 204 (PDF 215) -->

therefore *come ye* and *look ye* are the same, namely come with the eye of faith, turn the face, look upon God. So also the word נהרו may be the preterite of the indicative mood, and also of the imperative: for defective verbs are often inflected after the manner of perfect ones. Certainly not the Seventy only, but St. Jerome also translated that word by the imperative mood: and indeed St. Jerome translated, *confluite* (flow together), and the Seventy, *illuminamini* (be enlightened), because the verb נהר signifieth both *to flow together* and *to be enlightened;* but *be enlightened* cohereth better with the foregoing verb, *look ye,* and therefore the version of the Seventy Interpreters is commonly received. Lastly, the Seventy and St. Jerome did not read פניהם *facies eorum* (their faces), but פניכם *facies vestræ* (your faces). Therefore the whole disagreement of the Hebrew copies from the Greek and Latin ariseth from the points ill placed by the Rabbins, or from the less correct books which we now have.

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. THIS POOR MAN CRIED, AND THE LORD HEARD HIM: AND SAVED HIM OUT OF ALL HIS TROUBLES.**

He proveth by his own example that we must come securely to God in the time of tribulation. For, saith he, "this poor man," namely David, destitute of all help, so that he even begged bread of the priest, "cried" with faith and confidence, knocked with ardent prayer at the door of the divine clemency, "and the Lord" forthwith "heard him, and delivered him from all trouble."

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. THE ANGEL OF THE LORD SHALL ENCAMP ROUND ABOUT THEM THAT FEAR HIM: AND SHALL DELIVER THEM.**

He hath proved by example, now he proveth by reason, that we must come to God with all confidence. The reason is, because "the Angel of the Lord," to whom God himself hath given charge to keep just men, as is said in *Ps.* xc, when he seeth a man in danger, is forthwith present, and after the manner of a camp compasseth that man with his protection, that nothing may be able to hurt him. "The Angel of the Lord shall encamp" (*immittet,* shall send in), there is understood, *himself,* "round about them that fear him." In the Hebrew the sense is clearer: for so we read to the word, *castrametatur Angelus Domini in circuitu timentium eum, et eripiet eos* (the Angel of the Lord encampeth round about them that fear him, and shall deliver them). Wonderful is the power of the angels, that one angel supplieth the place of an army. Whence it followeth that there ought to be the greatest security and peace in the heart of them that fear God, who are fenced with such a guard.

[Margin: The wonderful power of the Angels.]

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. O TASTE, AND SEE THAT THE LORD IS SWEET: BLESSED IS THE MAN THAT HOPETH IN HIM.**

He goeth on in the exhortation, and because he had said: Come ye to him, and had proved by example and by reason that we must come to God in the time of tribulation, now he exhorteth to make trial, that they themselves also may learn by their own experience that the matter is so. "O taste, saith he, and see that the Lord is sweet," that is, make experiment, make trial, begin in very deed to cast all your care upon God, by cleaving to him, all other comforters being dismissed; "and see," that is, know that the Lord is sweet to all that cleave to him. And truly what sweeter thing can be imagined, than that a soul endowed with charity, a good conscience, a pure heart, an unfeigned faith, should rest in the bosom of the supreme good? Truly "blessed is the man that hopeth in him," that is, who hath peace with God, and leaneth upon him with sure hope. And as we expounded the words, *come ye, and be enlightened,* in a certain higher sense of the unwise,[^5] who are enlightened by justification: so that which is said here, *taste, and see,* may be expounded of them that are advancing, who after spiritual regeneration begin to be nourished and to grow, according to that of St. Peter in his first *epistle,* chap. ii: "As newborn babes, desire the milk, that thereby you may grow unto salvation: if so be you have tasted that the Lord is sweet;"[^6] where St. Peter citeth this place of the Psalm in that sense which we now follow. St. Paul also, chap. vi *to the Heb.,* joineth enlightening with tasting, when he saith: "It is impossible for those who were once illuminated, have tasted also the good word of God,"[^7] etc.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. FEAR THE LORD, ALL YE HIS SAINTS: FOR THERE IS NO WANT TO THEM THAT FEAR HIM.**

After the exhortation to make trial how sweet the Lord is, the Prophet exhorteth to the fear of the Lord, that is, to the keeping of the commandments, or, which is the same, to persevere in justice and the charity of God, which is the foundation of the confidence by which we come to God, and taste the sweetness of his benefits. Most rightly also are these words joined to those above according to another higher sense: for as by coming we begin, and by tasting we advance, so by fearing we are perfected: for servile fear is not treated of here, but chaste and filial fear, which belongeth to the holy and the perfect. "Fear, saith he, the Lord, all ye his saints:" for this fear includeth perfect charity, because he who loveth perfectly feareth most vehemently lest in anything he offend the beloved, and therefore most diligently conformeth himself to the will of God, and keepeth his word in all things; and he who so keepeth his word, "in him the charity of God is perfected," as St. John saith in his first *epistle,* chap. ii. Of this fear it is said, *Job* xxviii: "Behold the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom;" *Ecclus.* i: "To fear God is the fulness of wisdom;" and chap. xxv: "The fear of the Lord is above all things;"[^8] and *Isa.* xi, it is said of Christ: "And he shall be filled with the spirit of the fear of the Lord." Lastly Ecclesiastes, in the last chapter: "Fear God, and keep his commandments: for this is all man," as though he said: In this consisteth the whole perfection of man, and all his good in this life, that fearing God he keep all his commandments; and this the following words signify in the higher sense, "for there is no want to them that fear him," or, as the Hebrew word מחסור properly signifieth, *non est defectus timentibus eum* (there is no lack to them that fear him), that is, nothing is lacking to them that

[Margin: The commendation of the filial fear of God.]

<!-- p. 205 (PDF 216) -->

fear him: for this is to be perfect, that nothing be wanting to one. And indeed what can be wanting to the friend of God, whose are all things, since all things of friends are common? But if at any time temporal things seem to be wanting to most just men, yet in truth nothing is wanting to them, since patience is given them, which is far better than riches: nor is he properly said to lack riches who desireth not riches; for the mind ought to be rich, not the chest. But yet the same Prophet, or rather the same Holy Ghost, who by his words instructeth the wise, by the same words taken in a lower sense instructeth also the unwise, and exhorteth them to fear God, for there is no want to them that fear him, that is, because God supplieth temporal things to his servants, and doth not forsake them in the time of necessity. And there are in the Scriptures, and in the histories of the lives of the Saints, examples without number, by which it is proved that God by a wonderful providence is wont to provide for his servants those things which are necessary for life.

[Margin: The mind ought to be rich, not the chest.]

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. THE RICH HAVE WANTED, AND HAVE SUFFERED HUNGER: BUT THEY THAT SEEK THE LORD SHALL NOT BE DEPRIVED OF ANY GOOD.**

He explaineth the same by comparing the wicked with them that fear God, as though the Prophet said: Not only shall there be no want to them that fear God, but on the contrary there shall be great want to them that fear him not, although they were rich before. In the Hebrew, for *the rich,* there is כפירים *chephirim, leones* (lions), or *leunculi* (young lions): for the Prophet calleth the wicked rich lions by a metaphor, because they seize the things of others. He saith therefore that the wicked rich, God himself punishing them, have more than once been brought to extreme want. "The rich have wanted, and have suffered hunger," that is, they who were rich began to want and to suffer hunger, because riches are unstable, and deceitful, and exposed to many and divers dangers. "But they that fear the Lord shall not be deprived of any good," that is, they who have their hope placed not in riches, but in God, such as are they that fear God, these, although they be poor, shall not be deprived of any good, that is, no good shall be wanting to them: for it is a Hebrew phrase, and most frequent in the Scriptures, that in a negative proposition *non omne* (not every) is put for *nullum* (none). We should say, *nullo bono minuentur* (they shall be deprived of no good), for that which the Hebrews say, *non minuentur omni bono* (they shall not be deprived of every good). So in *Ps.* xlviii: "When he shall die he shall take nothing away" (*non sumet omnia,* he shall not take all things), that is, when he shall die, he shall take nothing; so in the *Apoc.,* chap. xxii: "And there shall be no curse any more" (*omne maledictum non erit amplius,* every curse shall be no more), that is, and no curse shall be any more. Moreover the words, *the rich have wanted, and have suffered hunger,* have also another sense more hidden, namely that they who love temporal riches, how much soever they abound in riches, always want and suffer hunger, because they always desire more than they have; "but they that seek the Lord," because they seek an infinite thing, which is greater than their desire, according to that of St. John, in his first *epistle,* chap. iii: "God is greater than our heart;" these "shall not be deprived of any good," because, cleaving to the supreme good, they have in it all goods.

[Margin: Verse 11.]

**11. COME, CHILDREN, HEARKEN TO ME: I WILL TEACH YOU THE FEAR OF THE LORD.**

After the Prophet hath exhorted men to the fear of the Lord, he teacheth what is the profit of this fear, and in what thing this fear is placed. "Come, saith he, to me," come to the school of the Holy Ghost, than which none more profitable[^9] can be found; "hearken to me," or rather to the Spirit of the Lord speaking by my tongue. For so David himself speaketh in II *Kings,* chap. xxiii: "The spirit of the Lord hath spoken by me and his word by my tongue." "I will teach you the fear of the Lord," that is, I will teach how great is the profit of the fear of the Lord, and what this fear of the Lord is, to which, as to the sum of all goods, I exhort you so often in the Psalms, and so vehemently.

[Margin: Verse 12.]

**12. WHO IS THE MAN THAT DESIRETH LIFE: WHO LOVETH TO SEE GOOD DAYS?**

In these words the Prophet explaineth the profit and end of the fear of the Lord, namely that the fear of the Lord bringeth forth true life and good days, that is, a most happy life, which the just foretaste in this world, while they have in their heart "the kingdom of God, which is justice, and peace, and joy in the Holy Ghost," *Rom.* xiv, and in the other world shall possess perfectly, when death shall be swallowed up in victory. "Who, saith he, is the man that desireth life?" as though he said: I promised that I would teach the fear of the Lord; now therefore I fulfil my promise, and say that the end of the fear of the Lord is that which all desire, but few attain, namely, the true and blessed life. But they who would attain this end must take the means, which I will presently add. Who therefore is he that desireth life? that is, they who say that they desire the blessed life, and yet enter not upon the way which leadeth to it, seem not truly and in earnest to desire the blessed life, but only a shadow and image of life; therefore I ask: Who is he that truly and in earnest desireth the true life? who truly loveth to see good days, happy and blessed days? The Hebrew hath it a little otherwise, *diligit dies ad videndum bonum* (loveth days to see good); and although the sense is the same, yet it may be that in the ancient copies there was in the plural number טובים *tobim, bonos* (good, of the days), that which is now טוב *tob, bonum* (good, the thing); and that the ancients read the word *ad videndum* (to see), not as a gerund, but as an infinitive: for the same word among the Hebrews maketh the infinitive and the gerund, a single letter being added at the beginning. For not only the Seventy Interpreters, but also St. Jerome, and St. Peter himself, in his first *epistle,* chap. iii, readeth *dies videre bonos* (to see good days), and not *dies ad videndum bonum.*

[Margin: Verse 13.]

**13. KEEP THY TONGUE FROM EVIL, AND THY LIPS FROM SPEAKING GUILE.**

[Margin: Verse 14.]

**14. TURN AWAY FROM EVIL AND DO GOOD: SEEK AFTER PEACE AND PURSUE IT.**

The holy Prophet now teacheth in what ways the

<!-- p. 206 (PDF 217) -->

fear of the Lord bringeth men to life, and good days, and he saith that the perfect keeping of the commandments of God, or the abstaining from all sin, of words, of works and of the heart, is the right way to life, according to that of the Lord, *Matt.* xix: "If thou wilt enter into life, keep the commandments." And this keeping of the law and flight from all sin proceedeth from the fear of God: therefore it is the fear of the Lord which, by the keeping of the law, maketh us arrive at true life and good days. "Keep thy tongue from evil," that is, beware lest thou offend God by sin of the tongue, by lying, forswearing, detracting, brawling, etc. And he beginneth with the sins of the tongue, because they are more frequent, and are more hardly avoided; for which cause St. James saith, chap. iii: "He that offendeth not in word, the same is a perfect man."[^10] "And thy lips from speaking guile;" he hath forbidden in general all sins of the tongue, but he willed also to forbid in particular pernicious lies, because they are much more grievous than the rest, and from them many others arise. "Turn away from evil and do good:" from the sin of the tongue he passeth to sins of work, and first he warneth that sins of commission be avoided, when he saith: "Turn away from evil," such as is murder, adultery, etc. Then he addeth: "And do good," that all sins of omission be avoided, such as are, when parents are not honoured, when God is not worshipped in his time, when alms, prayers, fastings, and the like good works are omitted. "Seek after peace and pursue it." Here at length he addeth that sins of the heart be avoided, angers, hatreds, envies, and the other depraved affections of the mind: that true peace and tranquillity in every business may be procured and held. And rightly doth the Prophet say: "Seek after peace," because it is the duty of a good man not so much to have peace with all, as to wish and seek it: for often by the fault of others peace cannot be obtained; therefore the Apostle also saith, *Rom.* xii: "As much as is in you, have peace with all men."[^11] And the prophet David in *Ps.* cxxi:[^12] "With them that hated peace I was peaceable." Nor only with others, but with ourselves also we cannot have perfect peace in this vale of miseries. "For I see, saith the Apostle, *Rom.* vii, another law in my members, fighting against the law of my mind." But although we cannot have perfect peace with ourselves, yet peace is to be sought, by taming the members with fastings, and by subjecting the flesh to the spirit, that at length it may learn not to fight, or to fight less, against the command of the mind. Lastly that peace is to be sought, with all the strength of the soul, which awaiteth us in the heavenly Jerusalem: for they who pant after that peace, as is meet, with their whole heart, easily contemn all temporal goods and evils, and thereby have even in this world peace with God, which alone the filial fear of the Lord regardeth.

[Margin: It is the duty of a good man to wish for peace, not to have it.]

[Margin: Verse 15.]

**15. THE EYES OF THE LORD ARE UPON THE JUST: AND HIS EARS UNTO THEIR PRAYERS.**

He proveth what he had said, that they who keep the commandments of God, and avoid sins, have life and good days. And the reason is, because the Lord perpetually looketh upon just men, and always heareth their prayers; for how shall they not have good days, who pass their life under an almighty keeper? For if the just know the evils that hang over them, and cry to God, they find his ears open and attentive; if haply they know them not, God watcheth for them, and delivereth them from very many evils, which the just themselves neither see nor understand: for to this end are "the eyes of the Lord upon the just," that he may keep them from the evils to which their own eyes reach not. Wonderful goodness of God! Whom would it not delight to love so good a Lord with the whole heart, and to fear him with filial affection? who, considering these things, would not cry with the Prophet: "Pierce thou my flesh with thy fear;" and in another Psalm: "Let my heart rejoice that it may fear thy name?" But the just are not always heard by God: nay, they are always heard; but if God doth not that which they ask, he doth it not for this cause, that it is not expedient for them. He is a physician, and if he heareth not, that the bitter potion be not taken, he heareth, that by that potion the disease be cured.

[Margin: The wonderful goodness of God.]

[Margin: Verse 16.]

**16. BUT THE COUNTENANCE OF THE LORD IS AGAINST THEM THAT DO EVIL THINGS: TO CUT OFF THE REMEMBRANCE OF THEM FROM THE EARTH.**

By a comparison of the wicked the Prophet heighteneth the benefit of the just: for as the eyes of the Lord watch over the just, that he may keep them, so also *the countenance of the Lord,* that is, the eye of the Lord (for countenance in the Hebrew is called פנים, from the verb פנה, which is *aspicere,* to look upon), watcheth *against them that do evil things,* that is, against men of iniquity, not that he may keep them, but *to cut off the remembrance of them from the earth,* that is, that they may be utterly rooted out and perish, not themselves only, but their sons, and grandsons, and all their posterity, until their remembrance be wholly abolished. This we do not see always come to pass, because either the wicked themselves come to a better mind before the time of vengeance come, or the sons and grandsons do not imitate the sins of their parents, or for some other cause, by the just judgment of God, punishments of this kind are deferred: but the Psalm singeth that which is wont for the most part to come to pass, and which is found in the first head of the Decalogue: "I am thy God, mighty, jealous, who visit iniquities unto the third and fourth generation in them that hate me," *Exod.* xx.[^13]

[Margin: Verse 17.]

**17. THE JUST CRIED, AND THE LORD HEARD THEM: AND DELIVERED THEM OUT OF ALL THEIR TROUBLES.**

He proveth what he said: "The eyes of the Lord are upon the just," etc., by the examples of the fathers which are read in the sacred histories, as of Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, Moses, Josue, Gedeon, and others; and perhaps also he foresaw in spirit and sang of the deliverance of Daniel from the den of lions, of the three children from the burning furnace, of Susanna from the death to which she had been condemned on a false charge, and of very many others. Nor are the examples of the Machabees against this verse, who were not delivered from torments and death, as neither are the examples of the apostles and martyrs, and of

<!-- p. 207 (PDF 218) -->

Christ himself, who unjustly endured most grievous punishments from enemies and persecutors. For these were most truly delivered from all tribulation, who, as the Church singeth, "by the short way of a holy death possess the blessed life." Most truly also were these heard when they cried to God, because God gave them something better than deliverance from temporal calamity: for he gave them the precious gift of patience, and for the same patience the crown of everlasting glory.

[Margin: In the hymn for many martyrs.]

[Margin: Verse 18.]

**18. THE LORD IS NIGH UNTO THEM THAT ARE OF A CONTRITE HEART: AND HE WILL SAVE THE HUMBLE OF SPIRIT.**

The Prophet declareth the manner in which God delivereth the just from tribulation, and he seemeth to explain in more words that which he said most briefly in *Ps.* ix:[^14] "I am with him in tribulation, I will deliver him, and I will glorify him," that is, I am with him by patience in this life, "I will deliver him" by the sleep of death, "and I will glorify him" by a glorious resurrection. So now he saith: "The Lord is nigh unto them that are of a contrite heart" (*tribulato corde,* of a troubled heart), that is, the just, who by reason of straits and persecutions are of an afflicted and troubled heart, God doth not forsake; but he is near at hand, supplying patience, and mingling heavenly consolation, that they may be able to bear bravely. Nor shall this last long, for a little after "he will save the humble of spirit," that is, those same men, humbled and afflicted in heart and spirit (for in the Hebrew there is דכאי רוח, *contritos spiritu,* the contrite of spirit) by reason of the grievousness of the tribulation, he will pluck out of every strait.

[Margin: Verse 19.]

**19. MANY ARE THE AFFLICTIONS OF THE JUST; BUT OUT OF THEM ALL WILL THE LORD DELIVER THEM.**

This belongeth to those last words of the verse above, *he will save the humble of spirit:* for it is the same as if he had said, *he will save the humble of spirit,* and will so save them, that although their afflictions are very many, yet out of them all without exception he will deliver them. "For God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes," as is said in the *Apoc.,* chap. xxi. Hence we are admonished that freedom from want, diseases, ignominy, persecutions, calumnies, oppressions is not promised to the faithful in this life; but that spiritual consolation is promised in the present time, and full and perfect deliverance in the time to come. In the Hebrew צדיק *tsaddik* is in the singular number, *multæ tribulationes justi, et de his omnibus liberabit eum Dominus* (many are the afflictions of the just man, and out of them all will the Lord deliver him), but in the sense there is no disagreement: for the Prophet did not mean to signify that the afflictions of one just man only are many; but of every just man, and thereby of all the just.

[Margin: Verse 20.]

**20. THE LORD KEEPETH THEIR BONES, NOT ONE OF THEM SHALL BE BROKEN.**[^15]

These things seem to belong to the glory of the resurrection, to which without doubt belongeth that which the Lord saith in the Gospel, *Luke* xxi: "But a hair of your head shall not perish;" for that may rightly be said not to have been broken, which immediately after the breaking is presented again whole, and stronger and fairer. Although therefore the bones and all the members of the just be scattered, and either devoured by wild beasts, or cast into the sea, or consumed by fire, yet the Lord keepeth them all in the bosom of his providence, and nothing at all of them shall perish, but all shall be restored whole and glorious in the resurrection. Others however expound this place of the inward bones, that is, of the fortitude of the soul, which in the saints, even in the greatest persecutions, the Lord keeping it, doth not fail.

[Margin: The resurrection of the dead in all]

[Margin: their members shall be entire.]

[Margin: Verse 21.]

**21. THE DEATH OF THE WICKED IS VERY EVIL: AND THEY THAT HATE THE JUST SHALL BE GUILTY.**

Lest the wicked should think that their torments and miseries also are ended in death, which atheists, or they who believe not the providence of God, or the immortality of the soul, falsely persuade themselves, the Prophet addeth: "The death of the wicked is very evil," namely because it will be the beginning of eternal torments, as the death of the Saints is precious, because it beginneth everlasting rest and glory. "And they that hate the just shall be guilty" (*delinquent*), that is, and they who vex the just man, and hold him in hatred, namely all the persecutors of the just, who think that they are conquerors and gain much when they oppress, spoil, and slay the just, at length "shall be guilty," that is, shall stray from the way of true felicity, and shall say that which we read in the book of *Wisdom,* chap. v: "Therefore we have erred from the way of truth, and the light of justice hath not shined unto us, we wearied ourselves in the way of iniquity and destruction, we have walked through hard ways, but the way of the Lord we have not known, what hath pride profited us? or the boasting of riches? those things are passed away like a shadow," etc. In the Hebrew, for the words *mors peccatorum pessima* (the death of the wicked is very evil), there is to the word, *interficiet impium malitia* (malice shall slay the wicked man). And although the sense is the same, and without doubt the death of those whom malice slayeth is very evil, because malice bringeth not temporal death only, but also everlasting, yet it is altogether credible that the Seventy Interpreters read otherwise, and therefore translated otherwise. The word תמותת *themutheth,* therefore, which is a verb, and signifieth *interficiet* (shall slay), they read *themuthoth,*[^16] which is a noun in the construct state, and signifieth *mors* (death). For in the Hebrew all the letters are the same, and only the points need be changed, which were added by the Rabbins, and often not rightly. And the noun *themuthath* being set down, the word following cannot be translated *impium* (the wicked man), but *impii* (of the wicked man), or *peccatoris* (of the sinner), and the third word cannot be rendered *malitia* (malice), but *mala* (evil), or *pessima* (very evil), although the Hebrew word signifieth both. Rightly therefore did the Seventy translate, *mors peccatorum pessima.* That which followeth, *et qui oderunt justum delinquent* (and they that hate the just shall be guilty), many translate from the Hebrew, *et qui oderunt justum, desolabuntur* (and they that hate the just shall be made desolate); but the Hebrew word signifieth both, *delinquere* (to offend) and *desolari* (to be made desolate), and is referred as well to the punishment as to the fault; but because the Seventy translated, πλημμελήσουσι, which verb signifieth *delinquere, peccare, errare* (to offend, to sin, to err), and St. Jerome rendered from the Hebrew *culpabuntur* (they shall be held guilty), we ought not to depart from this latter

<!-- p. 208 (PDF 219) -->

signification. Yet because *delinquere* may be taken for that which is to offend against another, or to hurt and to harm; and for that which is to offend against oneself, that is, to err from the mark, or to fail in the course, and to lose the prize, in this latter manner we took and expounded it a little before. For indeed there could be no doubt but that the wicked offend against just men, but there could be a doubt whether, in hurting the just, they hurt themselves more; and this the Prophet affirmeth, saying: "They that hate the just shall be guilty," that is, they who harm the just shall harm themselves more, because they shall err from the last end, which all men desire, and which alone maketh its possessors blessed and happy; and in this sense *delinquent* is the same as *desolabuntur:* for there is no greater desolation than to lose eternal life.

[Margin: Verse 22.]

**22. THE LORD WILL REDEEM THE SOULS OF HIS SERVANTS: AND NONE OF THEM THAT TRUST IN HIM SHALL OFFEND.**

The Psalm is concluded by foretelling to the just a lot contrary to that which hath been foretold to the wicked. "The Lord will redeem," from all bondage, and so from all evil, "the souls of his servants," when he shall have led them out of the prison of the body, and therefore the death of the just is very good, so that Balaam deservedly said: "Let my soul die the death of the just, and my last end be like to them," *Num.* xxiii.[^17] "And none of them that trust in him shall offend," that is, and they shall not err from the mark, they shall not fail in the course, they shall altogether arrive at the prize of heavenly felicity, "all" (*omnes*) who trust not in their own strength, but in God. Now it is to be observed in this place that hope of any kind whatsoever sufficeth not, as neither doth dead faith, for the obtaining of eternal life: but that in this place the attaining of beatitude is attributed to hope, because the speech is of the hope of the just, who fear and love God, which the apostle Peter calleth *a lively hope,* I *Pet.* i. This plainly is the hope and confidence which is born of patience, of good works, of the testimony of a good conscience, according to that: "Patience worketh trial; and trial hope," *Rom.* v;[^18] and that: "They that have ministered well, shall purchase to themselves a good degree, and much confidence in the faith which is in Christ Jesus," I *Tim.* iii; and that: "If our heart do not reprehend us, we have confidence towards God," *John* iii:[^19] this lively and perfect hope, I say, leadeth straight to the thing itself, that is, to everlasting glory, that what we have hoped for we may at length possess.
