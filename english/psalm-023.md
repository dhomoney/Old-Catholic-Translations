BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XXIII.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*A Psalm for David, on the first day of the week.*[^1]

In this title that part, *prima Sabbati* (on the first day of the week), is not in the Hebrew, but was added by the Greeks, perhaps because in the beginning of the Psalm mention is made of the creation of the earth, which was made on the first day of the week, that is, on the Lord's day, or because they were wont to recite this Psalm on the Lord's day. That, as some will have it, *prima Sabbati* was added because on that day the Lord rose again, seemeth not to me probable, since this Psalm manifestly proclaimeth not the resurrection, but the ascension of the Lord, and it is most well known that the ascension was made not on the first day of the week, but on the fifth. That this Psalm properly belongeth to the ascension, is witnessed by Cyprian, sermon *On the Ascension*; Jerome, epistle 142 *to Damasus*; Rufinus[^2] in the *Exposition of the Creed*; Gregory of Nyssa, John Chrysostom, Augustine, Leo, and others in the sermon *On the Ascension*.

[Margin: Why is this Psalm said to be of the first day of the week?]

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. THE EARTH IS THE LORD'S AND THE FULNESS THEREOF: THE WORLD, AND ALL THEY THAT DWELL THEREIN.**

The purpose of David is to show, that out of the innumerable multitude of men Christ alone, and a few others, few, I say, if they be compared with the multitude of the rest, shall enter into the heavenly and most blessed house of the Lord; wherefore, lest perchance the rest of men should be believed not to belong to God, but to have been created by some other principle, which the Marcionites and Manichees afterwards held, he setteth before these two verses, in which he teacheth that God is the Creator and Lord of the whole world, and of all things that are in it. "The earth is the Lord's and the fulness thereof," that is, and all things that are in it, and that fill it. Which is explained by the other part of the verse: "The world, and all they that dwell therein." For he declareth that, when he said: "The earth is the Lord's," he understood it chiefly of the habitable earth, which is properly called in Hebrew תבל *thebel*, and in Greek οἰκουμένη, that is, *the habitable world*; and when he said, *all the fulness thereof*, he declareth that he understood it especially of men, who dwell in it, not of the other things that are in it.

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. FOR HE HATH FOUNDED IT UPON THE SEAS; AND HATH PREPARED IT UPON THE RIVERS.**

He proveth that God is Lord of the world, and of all that dwell therein, because he himself made the earth, and made it to stand out above the waters, that it might be habitable. For, if it either wholly lacked waters, or were on every side covered with waters, it would be uninhabitable. "For he," saith he, "hath founded it upon the seas," that is, he made the world from the foundations, and thereby made it out of nothing, and made it "upon the seas," that is, that the surface of the earth might be higher than the surface of the sea, and likewise "hath prepared it upon the rivers," that is, he prepared the world for the habitation of men, and therefore made it above and higher than the rivers: for otherwise the sea and the rivers would overwhelm the earth, and would be higher. Now from this, that God made the earth habitable, it followeth that he is also Lord of all men, both because men were made out of the earth, and return into the earth; and because they inhabit the earth, not as lords, but as husbandmen appointed by God, to till the earth.

[Margin: God is Lord of all men.]

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. WHO SHALL ASCEND INTO THE MOUNTAIN OF THE LORD: OR WHO SHALL STAND IN HIS HOLY PLACE?**

Since all men are husbandmen, and servants of God, and all alike inhabit the earth, which is God's possession: "Who shall ascend into the mountain of the Lord?" that is, shall there be any one out of so great a number, and who shall he be, that is worthy to ascend to the place where God properly dwelleth? The heavenly house is called *the mountain of the Lord* (as we have often said elsewhere), which is truly lofty and holy, of which it is said in another place, *Ps.* cxiii: "The heaven of heaven is the Lord's: but the earth he has given to the children of men." They who expound this place of mount Sion, which was in Jerusalem, a city of Judea, seem able to rest on no solid foundation, since it is certain that that mountain was full of men of every kind, so that it seemeth plainly ridiculous to say of it: "Who shall ascend into the mountain of the Lord?"

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. THE INNOCENT IN HANDS, AND CLEAN OF HEART, WHO HATH NOT TAKEN HIS SOUL IN VAIN, NOR SWORN DECEITFULLY TO HIS NEIGHBOUR.**

He answereth that four conditions are required in him, who is to ascend the mountain of the Lord. The first is, that he be *innocent in hands*, that is, that he have done nothing evil; the second, that he be *clean of heart*, that is, that he have thought, or desired, nothing evil; the third, *that he have not taken his soul in vain*, that is, that he have not received life from God to no purpose, that is, that he have not only done, or thought, nothing evil, but also have thought, and done, all those good things, which are required for obtaining the end appointed him by

<!-- p. 134 (PDF 145) -->

God; *the fourth*, that he have not sworn deceitfully. That phrase, *who hath not taken his soul in vain*, some wrest to the oath, so that the following clause is an explanation of the former. And they will have it read, *qui non accepit in vano animam ejus* (who hath not taken his soul in vain), that is, God's; or, according to the points of the Rabbins, *qui non accepit in vano animam meam* (who hath not taken my soul in vain), so that God is understood to speak. But this exposition seemeth not probable, both because without cause the Rabbins will have it read, *animam meam* (my soul), since the Hebrew word hath the pronoun of the third person, not of the first, and St. Jerome rendereth in the third person; and because that reading, *animam meam*, is altogether new and unheard of, since the Latins and Greeks have hitherto understood in that word the reflexive pronoun, namely *animam suam* (his own soul); and lastly, because to take the name of God in vain, for that which is to swear falsely by God, is found in the holy Scriptures; but to take one's own soul, or the soul of God, in vain, for to swear falsely, is nowhere read. Others will have it that to take one's soul in vain is nothing else but to think upon and to covet vain things, that is, things temporal and perishing; but this was already comprehended in that phrase, *clean of heart*, and it seemeth forced and harsh. But the Prophet added that fourth condition, of the deceitful oath, that in that sin, which is one of the greatest, since it is alike against God and against one's neighbour, he might comprehend all the other sins, which are committed by the instrument of the tongue. And so he, who is worthy to ascend into the mountain of God, that is, into heaven, ought to be perfect on every side, so that he have never offended either in heart, or in word, or in deed, and on the other hand have done all the good things, which belonged to his duty. But these conditions agree to none other than Christ. For he alone it is, "who did no sin, neither was guile found in his mouth," as is said, I *Pet.* ii. Of the rest, "there is none that doth good, no not one," as David speaketh in *Ps.* xiii; and "all we like sheep have gone astray," saith Isaias, chap. liii; and the apostle Paul, *Rom.* iii, 23: "All have sinned, and do need the glory of God." Therefore rightly the Lord himself saith, *John* iii: "No man hath ascended into heaven, but he that descended from heaven, the Son of man who is in heaven." The rest are of the earth, earthly; he alone from heaven, heavenly, holy, innocent, undefiled, separated from sinners, and made higher than the heavens by the ascension. Yet the person of Christ alone was not to ascend into the mountain of the Lord, but he, and his body, which is the Church, which he washed in his blood, that he might present it to himself a glorious Church, not having spot or wrinkle, *Ephes.* v; therefore it followeth:

[Margin: No man, Christ excepted, hath all things necessary for attaining heaven.]

[Margin: The Church is to ascend into heaven together with Christ.]

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. HE SHALL RECEIVE A BLESSING FROM THE LORD, AND MERCY FROM GOD HIS SAVIOUR.**

"He," namely Christ, "shall receive a blessing from the Lord," that is, most plentiful good things for himself, and "mercy from God his Saviour," for his body, which is the Church, in respect of which also God is called his Saviour. For life everlasting in the kingdom of heaven is to Christ justice, to his faithful mercy; for although the just merit life everlasting condignly, yet their merits themselves spring from the mercy of God, and are most truly called the gifts of God. Whence in *Ps.* cii it is said: "Who crowneth thee with mercy and compassion"; and *Rom.* vi: "The wages of sin is death. But the grace of God, life everlasting." In the Hebrew there is, *justitiam a Deo salutari suo* (justice from God his Saviour), and so St. Jerome rendereth, according to which reading this whole verse would properly agree to Christ. Yet the Seventy, who rendered *misericordiam* (mercy), seem to have considered that justice in the Scriptures is sometimes taken for goodness, nay even expressly for mercy, as in *Ps.* cxi: "He hath distributed, he hath given to the poor: his justice remaineth for ever and ever." Whence is that in the Gospel, *Matt.* vi: "Take heed that you do not your justice before men, to be seen by them." And presently: "But when thou dost alms, let not thy left hand know what thy right hand doth." And so the Seventy in this place had regard not so much to the word, as to the thing signified.

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. THIS IS THE GENERATION OF THEM THAT SEEK HIM, OF THEM THAT SEEK THE FACE OF THE GOD OF JACOB.**

Here now the Prophet maketh manifest that that one innocent in hands, and pure of heart, who shall ascend into the mountain of the Lord, and shall receive a blessing and mercy from God, is not the head alone, but the head with the body, Christ with the Church. "This," saith he, "is the generation of them that seek him," that is, this one who shall ascend into heaven is a certain generation of men regenerated in Christ, whose chief care is to seek God, to pant after seeing the face of God, in a word to strive with all their strength toward the holy mountain. And truly it is one, and perhaps the chief sign of the elect of God, to sigh for their country. For the generation of the children of this world seeketh nothing less than God, dreadeth nothing more than death; and, if the choice were given, they would rather live always in this world, than be dissolved, and be with Christ. In the Greek text there is: Αὕτη ἡ γενεὰ ζητούντων Κύριον, *hæc est generatio quærentium Dominum* (this is the generation of them that seek the Lord); and so read in the Commentary Jerome, Augustine, Euthymius and others: yet in the Hebrew there is *quærentium eum* (of them that seek him), as our Latin edition hath. The sense is altogether the same; for that word, *eum* (him), is referred to the Lord, of whom it had been said, *he shall receive a blessing from the Lord*. And so the Seventy seem to have explained the pronoun *eum*, which is in the Hebrew. What followeth, *quærentium faciem Dei Jacob* (of them that seek the face of the God of Jacob), is in the Hebrew, *quærentium faciem tuam, o Jacob!* (of them that seek thy face, O Jacob!) In which place also the Seventy explained the obscure sentence of the Hebrew text, in which *God* is understood, so that the sense is: of them that seek thy face,

<!-- p. 135 (PDF 146) -->

O God of Jacob: unless perhaps by *Jacob* in the Hebrew text be understood the company of the blessed, or the heavenly Jerusalem, and then it will come to the same thing, to seek the face of God, and to seek the face of Jacob: because no man seeth the face of Jacob, that is, of the heavenly country, who seeth not the face of the God of Jacob, since the whole business, and the whole felicity of that most blessed Jacob is to contemplate the face of God. There is added in the Hebrew סלה *selah*, that is *always*, because this generation not only seeketh God, but always seeketh God, that is, is not wearied in seeking God, nor suffereth itself to be drawn away from seeking God, to seek anything beneath God.

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. LIFT UP YOUR GATES, O YE PRINCES, AND BE YE LIFTED UP, O ETERNAL GATES: AND THE KING OF GLORY SHALL ENTER IN.**

When the holy Prophet had foreseen that one was found, who was worthy to ascend into the mountain of God, that is, Christ; he foretelleth that he is even now about to ascend in very deed, and that the eternal gates of heaven are to be opened to him. But after the manner of poets he useth prosopopœia, and addresseth both the *princes* of heaven, that is, the angels, and the *gates* themselves, and biddeth the angels to open, and the gates to suffer themselves to be opened, nay to lie open of their own accord to the King of glory at his coming. Euthymius noteth that the gates are not opened, but taken up, because at the coming of a king the gates of a city are wont to be taken away, and laid down in sign of subjection. But David saith not: *Take away* the gates, but *lift up*; and he saith not to the gates: *Go out of the midst*, but *be ye lifted up*. He saith therefore *lift up* and *be ye lifted up*, because the gates of heaven are not as it were in a wall, so that they must be moved to the sides, but as it were in a roof, or ceiling, so that they are to be opened upwards. Whence also is refuted the opinion of those, who would have the Prophet speak of some house on the earthly mount Sion, into which the ark was to be brought. In the Hebrew word for word, as St. Jerome rendereth, the Prophet addresseth the gates alone, and saith: *Attollite, portæ, capita vestra; elevamini, januæ sempiternæ, et introibit Rex gloriæ* (Lift up, O gates, your heads; be ye lifted up, O everlasting doors, and the King of glory shall enter in). Now gates are said to lift up their heads, when they are raised upwards, because then their extreme parts are lifted up, which are in them as it were heads. And although the Hebrew word ראשים *rashim* may signify not only *heads*, as St. Jerome rendereth, but also *princes*, as the Seventy rendered, yet the pronoun added, which the Hebrews call an affix, according to the common Hebrew phrase is wont to be joined with the noun immediately preceding; and therefore that word, ראשיכם, ought to be rendered, *capita vestra* (your heads), as St. Jerome rendereth, or *principes vestros* (your princes), which in this place is not to the purpose. Yet because the Seventy Interpreters plainly enough understood by ראשים *rashim* the *princes*, since they rendered, οἱ ἄρχοντες, and joined the pronoun with the noun *portas* (gates), it must be said, either that in their text the pronoun was affixed to the noun שערים, which signifieth *gates*, and not to the noun ראשים, which signifieth *princes*; or that a pronoun affixed to one noun can be referred to another noun, although most rarely, and beside the rule.

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. WHO IS THIS KING OF GLORY? THE LORD WHO IS STRONG AND MIGHTY: THE LORD MIGHTY IN BATTLE.**

He bringeth in the princes of the heavenly Jerusalem asking: "Who is this King of glory," not that the angels on the day of the Lord's ascension were ignorant that Christ is the King of glory, but that he may express the wonder, which that new thing brought with it, that a true bodily man was seen to ascend above the heavens, not as a guest, but as Lord of the glorious and everlasting city. And the Prophet answereth that the King of glory is Christ, the Lord most strong and most mighty, who showed his might in battle against the Prince of darkness, whom he overcame, spoiled and bound.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. LIFT UP YOUR GATES, O YE PRINCES, AND BE YE LIFTED UP, O ETERNAL GATES: AND THE KING OF GLORY SHALL ENTER IN.**

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. WHO IS THIS KING OF GLORY? THE LORD OF HOSTS, HE IS THE KING OF GLORY.**

The Prophet feigneth the angels, who are set over heaven, as it were to delay in opening the gates, and therefore he thundereth again: "Lift up your gates," etc., which words signify nothing else but the exceeding newness of the thing, that an earthly body should mount above the heavenly body, and human flesh above the angelic spirits themselves, with the incredible wonder, and as it were amazement, of all nature. Again therefore he bringeth in the angels asking: "Who is this King of glory?" to whom he himself answereth: "The Lord of hosts, he is the King of glory." And having heard the name most well known to them, namely *the Lord of hosts*, they open forthwith, and joyfully receive the King of glory. *Dominus virtutum* (the Lord of powers)[^3] is in the Hebrew, *Dominus* צבאות *tsebaoth*, which St. Jerome rendereth, *Dominus exercituum* (the Lord of armies), which name in the Prophets is most frequently given to God, nor is it common to gods by participation, but proper to God alone properly so called, both because it containeth the name of four letters, proper to the true God alone; and because he alone is truly Lord of armies, since he is set over the armies of the angels, who are almost innumerable and most mighty; and also because all creatures fight for the true God alone. For, as we have it in *Ps.* cxlviii: "Fire, hail, snow, ice, stormy winds which fulfil his word," that is, fight at his command. Pharao indeed found by experience that God is the Lord of armies, when he fought against him not only by angels, but also by the smallest living creatures, namely by frogs, flies and gnats, and not only by living creatures, but also by things without life, hail, fire, darkness, diseases, and the like. Now the translation of the Seventy, who rendered, *Dominus virtutum*, cometh to the same thing. For by *virtutes* are not understood

[Margin: Jova tsebaoth is a name proper to the true God alone.]

the moral virtues, which are called ἀρεταί, but strengths and powers, which are called δυνάμεις. And so *Dominus virtutum* is the Lord of strengths and powers, such as chiefly are the ranks of a camp set in array. In the common speech also armed hosts are wont to be called *les forces*. From these things we have it that Christ the Lord is truly and properly God, not a creature, as the Arians and very many other heretics would have it. For in this Psalm he is called *the Lord of powers*, or *of armies*, which name in the whole of sacred Scripture is given to God alone; and also *the King of glory*, that is, the King of the heavenly Jerusalem, which is the region of light, and of the highest and everlasting brightness; which without doubt can agree to the true God alone. There is added in the Hebrew סלה *selah*, *always*, that it may be signified that Christ is the King of glory, not for a time, but unto all eternity.

[Margin: Christ true God.]
