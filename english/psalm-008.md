BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM VIII.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*Unto the end, for the presses: a psalm of David.*[^1]

The word *torcularibus* (the presses) is in the Hebrew הגתית *haggihttith*, which seemeth to signify nothing, and therefore the moderns, who so read, say that it is a kind of musical instrument unknown to us. But the Seventy and St. Jerome, who rendered *torcularibus*, read without doubt הגתות *hagattoth*, which signifieth *presses*, and that this is the true reading we cannot doubt. But what *pro torcularibus* (for the presses) is, it is hard to divine; for they who understand the Church, or martyrdoms, or the cross of Christ, expound mystically. I have observed that the title, *pro torcularibus*, is found in three Psalms which pertain chiefly to the love of God, namely, in this eighth, which beginneth from a great affection: "O Lord our Lord, how admirable is thy name in the whole earth!" and in the eightieth, which in like manner exhorteth to spiritual jubilation: "Rejoice to God our helper: sing aloud to the God of Jacob. Take a psalm, and bring hither the timbrel: the pleasant psaltery with the harp"; lastly in the eighty-third, which is full of the affection of the most ardent divine love: "How lovely are thy tabernacles, O Lord! my soul longeth and fainteth. My heart and my flesh have rejoiced in the living God." And so from the matter of these psalms I suspect that it is said, "for the presses," because they are most fit for pressing out the wine of divine love from the hearts of men, or because they are to be sung with gladness and jubilation of heart, as they are wont to be glad and to sing, in the time of the vintage, who press out of the presses a mighty plenty of wine. Yet I confess that I have not yet attained the understanding of this title, as also of many others; the argument of this Psalm therefore is the praise of the power, wisdom and goodness of God, and chiefly toward mankind.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

[Margin: The ineffable name.]

**1. O LORD OUR LORD, HOW ADMIRABLE IS THY NAME IN THE WHOLE EARTH!**

From the consideration of the greatness of God, the Prophet is rapt into wonder that so great a God maketh so much of man, who is dust and ashes, that he hath vouchsafed to visit him, and to adorn him with very many and very great benefits. That, *Domine* (O Lord), is in the Hebrew יהוה, the proper name of God, which is written with four letters, and is derived from *esse* (to be), according to that: "I am who am"; which name the moderns express by the word *Iehova*; but the Seventy Interpreters and St. Jerome, nay Christ himself and the Apostles, were not wont to pronounce it, but in place of it said *Domine* (Lord). But that, *Dominus noster* (our Lord), is in the Hebrew another word, אדני *Adonai*,

<!-- p. 44 (PDF 55) -->

[Margin: Nor was Christ himself wont to pronounce it.]

which properly signifieth *Lord*. And so the sense is: *O God, who art thyself the fountain of being, whence all things that are are derived, and therefore our Lord*, that is, thou art Lord of all things, *how admirable is thy name in the whole earth!* that is, thy glory, or the good fame of thy name, is spread through all the earth, with the mighty wonder of all who are busy to consider it. Which Isaias, chap. vi, saith in other words: "All the earth is full of his glory." Now he saith that the name of God is admirable, because, although few in very deed wonder, since there are few who consider the works of God; yet the name itself is most worthy of wonder, since all creatures unceasingly praise the Creator, as all beautiful works are said to praise the workman. And in this manner all the earth is full of the glory of God, because whatsoever is in the earth, even though it seem a very little thing, maketh manifest the infinite power and wisdom of the Creator.

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. FOR THY MAGNIFICENCE IS ELEVATED ABOVE THE HEAVENS.**

The reason is given why the name of the Lord is so admirable in the whole earth, namely, because the magnificence of God is higher than heaven, that is, is not contained by heaven; it is so great that the whole world cannot contain it. His glory covered the heavens, and the earth is full of his praise, saith Habacuc in his Canticle, chap. iii. Now magnificence is the virtue of great princes, who at great costs either build mighty palaces, or cities, or maintain a very great number of servants, or even whole armies, or bestow most ample gifts upon others. But God hath built the palace of this whole world, whose pavement is the round of the earth, and whose roof is heaven. And he maintaineth all living things, which are innumerable; lastly, he hath given to the holy Angels, who are in very great number, and is to give to just men, a most ample kingdom, not temporal, but eternal. Truly therefore his magnificence is very great.

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. OUT OF THE MOUTH OF INFANTS AND OF SUCKLINGS THOU HAST PERFECTED PRAISE, BECAUSE OF THY ENEMIES, THAT THOU MAYST DESTROY THE ENEMY AND THE AVENGER.**

[Margin: Who are the infants and sucklings?]

He answereth an objection which could be made: if the glory of God filleth the round of the earth, and his magnificence is elevated above the heavens, whence cometh it that not all know and praise it? He answereth that it cometh from this, that God vouchsafeth not to be known and praised by the proud, who presume upon their own strength; but by the humble and little ones, according to that of *Matt.* xi: "I confess to thee, O Father, because thou hast hid these things from the wise and prudent, and hast revealed them to little ones." And hence the glory and magnificence of God greatly increaseth, since he is not known but by those by whom he himself hath willed to be known. Now this verse can be understood in two ways. *First*, so that by infants and sucklings men are understood, who in truth, in comparison of the Angels, are as infants and sucklings, chiefly as touching the understanding of divine things: therefore, saith he, out of the mouth of mortal men thou hast perfected praise, revealing to them thy glory "because of thy enemies," that is, to the confusion of the proud Angels; "that thou mayst destroy the enemy and the avenger," that is, that thou mayst destroy the wisdom of thy chief enemy, the devil, and of his defender, or avenger, that is, of the host of reprobate Angels who followed him. *Secondly*, by infants and sucklings can be understood men who are humble, and little ones in their own eyes, and unskilled in secular sciences, such as were many Prophets, and Apostles, and a mighty number of monks, and of holy virgins, and also of those truly children, who in tender age so perfectly knew the glory of God, and his admirable name, that they doubted not to meet death for him. In this sense the Lord alleged this Psalm in the Gospel: "Have you never read: Out of the mouth of infants and of sucklings thou hast perfected praise?" *Matt.* xxi. And the enemies are called the wise of this world, and their defenders, who, although they knew God, yet glorified him not as God, and therefore became fools, as the Apostle teacheth, *Rom.* i. That which is said, *ut destruas* (that thou mayst destroy), is in the Hebrew *ut quiescere facias* (that thou mayst make to cease); but the sense is the same. For the Psalm speaketh not of the destruction of the person, but of the wisdom, according to that of *Isa.* chap. xxix, as it is cited by St. Paul: "I will destroy the wisdom of the wise"; and that of the Apostle, I *Cor.* i: "God hath made foolish the wisdom of this world."

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. FOR I WILL BEHOLD THY HEAVENS, THE WORKS OF THY FINGERS: THE MOON AND THE STARS WHICH THOU HAST FOUNDED.**

Holy David setteth himself in the number of the infants and sucklings praising God, as if he said: Assuredly out of the mouth of infants and of sucklings thou hast perfected praise. For I, one of them, as one who was a shepherd of sheep, will perfect thy praises. "For I will behold thy heavens," that is, I will attentively consider that admirable work of thy hands, and thence I will praise thee, the creator of so great a work. That, *tuos* (thy), is not in the Greek; yet it is in the Hebrew, שמיך *schameca*, and thence it was rightly brought over into our edition. Now why he said *thy*, he declareth, saying, *the works of thy fingers*; nor doth he say the works of thy arms, but of thy fingers, both to show that the heavens were made by God with the greatest ease; and because with the fingers are made works fine and precious, for the making of which skill, not labour, is required. He maketh no mention of the sun, but of the moon only and the stars, because David was wont to contemplate in the night time, since that time, on account of its quiet, is fitter for contemplation. "I rose at midnight," saith he, *Ps.* cxviii, "to give praise to thee." And elsewhere, *Ps.* lxii: "I will meditate on thee in the morning." As also Isaias, chap. lxii:[^2] "My soul hath desired thee in the night." Now by night heaven is seen

<!-- p. 45 (PDF 56) -->

adorned with the moon and stars. Lastly, the word *fundasti* (thou hast founded) signifieth that the heavens, the moon and the stars were created out of nothing, as if the Prophet said: Which thou hast made from the foundation, when before nothing of them was in being.

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. WHAT IS MAN THAT THOU ART MINDFUL OF HIM? OR THE SON OF MAN THAT THOU VISITEST HIM?**

From the greatness of God now declared, he cometh to extol the greatness of God toward men. "What is man," saith he, that thou, the creator of heaven and earth, vouchsafest to remember him? As if he said: This is a very great benefit, that the most high God vouchsafeth to be mindful of man, who is dust and ashes. But because this remembering in God is not a simple memory, but is to remember in order to do good, he addeth, explaining: "Or the son of man that thou visitest him?" Man, and the son of man, signify the same in this place, unless perchance this distinction is set down to signify that the benefits of God were not bestowed upon the first man alone, who was man, but not the son of man; but also upon all his posterity, who are so men that they are also sons of men. Now the visitation signifieth the special providence which God hath toward men, but chiefly that which God showed when he came into this world, and took human nature, and was seen upon earth, and conversed with men. This is properly the visitation of which Zachary speaketh in his Canticle, *Luke* i: "Blessed be the Lord God of Israel; because he hath visited and wrought the redemption of his people." And below: "The Orient from on high hath visited us." This visitation assuredly most of all deserveth that wonder: "What is man," etc.

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. THOU HAST MADE HIM A LITTLE LESS THAN THE ANGELS, THOU HAST CROWNED HIM WITH GLORY AND HONOUR: AND HAST SET HIM OVER THE WORKS OF THY HANDS.**

These words have a twofold sense, the literal and the allegorical. According to the literal sense three benefits of God toward human nature are recounted: first, that man was made by God of so noble a nature that he is not inferior to the Angels, save a little; next, that he was adorned with glory and honour above all the other lower creatures, because he was made to the image and likeness of God, endowed with reason and free will; lastly, that he received power and dominion over all the works of God, and chiefly over living creatures: therefore he subjoineth.

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. THOU HAST SUBJECTED ALL THINGS UNDER HIS FEET, ALL SHEEP AND OXEN: MOREOVER THE BEASTS ALSO OF THE FIELDS.**

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. THE BIRDS OF THE AIR, AND THE FISHES OF THE SEA, THAT PASS THROUGH THE PATHS OF THE SEA.**

By sheep and oxen are understood all tame living creatures; by the beasts of the fields, the wild living creatures, which wander freely through the fields; by the birds of the air, and the fishes of the sea, there is no doubt what is understood. This is to be noted, that in the Hebrew, after *pisces maris* (the fishes of the sea), there is added in the singular, *perambulans semitas maris* (that which passeth through the paths of the sea), and that in the Greek also at least the gender is changed: for after *pisces maris* there is in the neuter, *perambulantia semitas maris* (the things that pass through the paths of the sea). The sense therefore is, not only the fishes properly so called, but every thing that passeth, or all things that pass, through the paths of the sea, that is, whatsoever moveth in the sea, whether they be fishes, or monsters, or any thing else. But now according to the allegorical sense, yet one that is certain, and intended by God, as the Apostle witnesseth, *Heb.* ii, and I *Cor.* xv, it is signified that man in Christ, after that notable visitation of God, that is, the incarnation of the Word, was made somewhat lower than the Angels on account of the passion: for then Christ was seen in some manner less than the Angels, when there appeared to him an Angel from heaven, strengthening him, and whereas the Angels are impassible and immortal; but Christ at that time suffered and died. But absolutely Christ was always superior to the Angels, and superior in every respect. Which appeared when he was crowned with glory and honour, that is, through the resurrection clothed on every side with a glorious and immortal body, and set through the admirable ascension over all the works of God, exalted, namely, to the very right hand of his Father. All things also were subjected to him, nothing excepted, "except him," as the Apostle saith, I *Cor.* xv, "who put all things under him." Now the things chiefly subjected to him are, first, men, as well the faithful, signified by the sheep and oxen, subjects and Prelates, the lesser and the greater, as the unbelieving, signified by the wild living creatures; next, the Angels, superior to men, signified by the birds of the air, which are borne on high, and unceasingly sing the praises of God; lastly, the devils, inferior to men, signified by the fishes of the sea, which dwell in the lowest and deepest places, and are dumb to the praises of God, and delight in mire and moisture. That all these things can be expounded allegorically in various ways St. Augustine teacheth on this place; where he setteth down another exposition, which can be read in him. One thing is to be added in this place, which should have been mentioned above: when the Psalmist saith: "Thou hast made him a little less than the angels," that there is not in the Hebrew the name of the Angels, whom the Hebrews call מלאכים *malachim*, but אלהים *Elohim*, which is wont to be interpreted *God*, or *Gods*; but because the name of God is often given in the Scriptures to the Angels, and even to men themselves who are set over others, the Seventy Interpreters, looking to the sense of the Prophet, rightly put *ab Angelis* (than the angels), and that interpretation the Apostle approved, *Heb.* ii.

[Margin: Christ is superior to the Angels, and in every respect greater.]

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. O LORD OUR LORD, HOW ADMIRABLE IS THY NAME IN ALL THE EARTH!**

He repeateth the first sentence, to show that it is as it were the conclusion which in the whole Psalm he intended to declare and prove, as if he said: By right therefore, and deservedly, did I exclaim: "O Lord our Lord, how admirable is thy name in all the earth!"

The Latin of verses 1 and 9 is the same (*in universa terra*); the Douay-Rheims has "in the whole earth" in the first and "in all the earth" in the last.
