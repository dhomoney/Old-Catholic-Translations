BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM L.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*Unto the end, a psalm of David, when Nathan the prophet came to him after he had sinned with Bethsabee.*[^1]

The title teacheth on what occasion this Psalm was composed by David; and the history is related more at large in II *Kings,* chap. xii. The sum of the history is this. David from his palace saw a most beautiful woman, by name Bethsabee, washing herself. Taken with love, he committed adultery with her. And that this might be the more easily hidden, he gave her husband Urias to death: so he joined the widow to himself in marriage, and had a son by her. Afterwards by the admonition of God Nathan the prophet came to David, and corrected him for the enormity and the murder which he had committed. David, returning to himself, did penance in earnest, and on that occasion composed this Psalm, in which he bewaileth his sin. The sense therefore of the title is this: "A psalm unto the end," to be sung even to the end of the world, which David composed "when Nathan the prophet came to him," who came to rebuke him at that time "when" the same David "went in to Bethsabee," the wife of Urias, and committed adultery with her. Upon this title some few things seem fit to be noted concerning the persons who are named in it, to wit, David, Nathan and Bethsabee. *First,* that the sin of David may strike great fear into just men: for if so great a friend of God fell so easily, who shall not fear a fall? *Secondly,* that the penance of the same David bringeth the hope of salvation to all sinners; for his sin was on every side most grievous, and yet by true penance he easily found pardon. *Thirdly,* that Nathan was indeed a prophet, but inferior to David not only in the gift of prophecy, but also in power, wisdom and the other gifts of God; and yet it pleased God that he should correct David, and bring him to penance. By which example great men are admonished not to despise the ministers of the word of God, but to come to hear them, even though they themselves be perhaps wiser and greater than they. *Fourthly,* that hence it is known of how great evils the beauty of a woman is the cause, when joined with imprudence and easiness. For if Bethsabee, a most fair woman, had not bared her body in a place whence she could be seen, or had not consented to David with so great easiness, certainly so great a man would not have fallen into so grievous crimes. *Fifthly,* that Nathan did not go in to admonish David immediately after his going in to Bethsabee, as the title seemeth to show, but long after; for a son was already born to David of Bethsabee when Nathan came. Therefore that which is said in the title is understood rather of the occasion than of the time, and the sense is, that the Psalm was written when Nathan went in to David on the occasion on which David had gone in to another man's wife, by name Bethsabee.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. HAVE MERCY ON ME, O GOD, ACCORDING TO THY GREAT MERCY.**[^2]

[Margin: Why is the word great added after mercy?]

The Prophet beginneth the Psalm from a prayer, in which he asketh the remission of his sin, and at the same time explaineth the first reason by which he himself is moved to ask, and believeth that God can be moved to pardon, being afterwards to explain other reasons in the following verses. He saith therefore: "Have mercy on me, O God;" David acknowledgeth and confesseth himself miserable by reason of the iniquity committed, although otherwise he abounded in all the goods of nature and of fortune; as on the contrary he is wont to call them blessed "that fear the Lord," *Ps.* cxi and cxxiii,[^3] not them that abound in riches, or honours: from which we understand how perversely the children of this world judge of misery and felicity. He addeth, "according to thy great mercy," as if he should say: Therefore I dare to implore thy mercy, because I am miserable; for mercy regardeth misery, that it may take it away. Now although in the Hebrew copy there is not that word, *magnam* (great), yet the Seventy Interpreters put that word in their version, either because they found it in more correct Hebrew copies, or because they wished to explain this place by adding that word. And that is called *great mercy* whereby sins are remitted, both because sin is a great misery; and because in comparison of this mercy that may be called a small mercy by which God is wont to bestow temporal goods: for God giveth temporal goods even to his enemies, and to those whom in the end he will reject; but the grace of the remission of sins he giveth to those only whom he vouchsafeth to adopt as sons and heirs of the eternal kingdom. David therefore, not content with that small mercy, whereby he had received the glory of a kingdom, and plentiful wealth, and a multitude of sons, and victory over his enemies, and other things of that kind, asketh "great mercy," which he knew to be placed in the remission of sins and the restoring of grace.

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. AND ACCORDING TO THE MULTITUDE OF THY TENDER MERCIES BLOT OUT MY INIQUITY.**

He repeateth and declareth the same; for that which he said: "Have mercy on me," he declareth, saying: "Blot out my iniquity;" and that which he saith: "According to thy great mercy," he declareth when he saith: "According to the multitude of thy tender mercies." But let us not invert the order of the Prophet; for he himself elegantly in this second verse beginneth from that which was last in the first verse, that by this variety he might make the Psalm more beautiful. Those words therefore, *according to the multitude of thy tender mercies,* explain what the great mercy of God is, which is shown to his beloved sons. Now the multitude of tender mercies signifieth the abundance of the loving-kindness of God;

<!-- p. 338 (PDF 349) -->

[Margin: God giveth more than we dare to wish.]

for the Hebrew word רחמיך, as we noted on the first verse of *Ps.* xvii, properly signifieth *paternum* and *tenerum amorem* (a fatherly and tender love), which the Scripture is wont to explain by *viscera misericordiæ* (the bowels of mercy). The Church also hath most rightly expressed this in the collect of the XI Sunday after Pentecost, when she saith: "Who in the abundance of thy loving-kindness dost exceed both the merits and the desires of thy suppliants." For truly the divine loving-kindness is so great that it giveth much more not only than we deserve, but even than we dare to wish. Which the Lord also showeth in the parable of the prodigal son, *Luke* xv. For the father not only admitted the penitent son to favour, but also straightway embraced and kissed him, and commanded the first robe and a precious ring to be given him, and the fatted calf to be killed for his sake, and lastly showed so great signs of benevolence toward the son who had wasted all his goods, that he could not have shown more toward one who had returned a victor and in triumph over his enemies. That which followeth, "blot out my iniquity," is referred both to the guilt and to the blemish of sins: for David knew that from the act of sin there is left in the soul the guilt of eternal death, which is written in the conscience, and the blemish of the privation of grace, which maketh the soul deformed, dark, hateful to God; to both is referred the word *delendi* (blotting out): for writings are wont to be blotted out when a debt is remitted, and blemishes also are blotted out when that which was blemished is washed. David therefore asketh that God deal not with him according to the rigour of justice, but out of fatherly mercy remit the debt of sin, and wash the blemish by restoring the brightness of grace.

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. WASH ME YET MORE FROM MY INIQUITY, AND CLEANSE ME FROM MY SIN.**

[Margin: After the fault is remitted the remains of sin are left.]

After the fault is remitted and grace restored, there still remain in man the evil habits of vices and the concupiscence of the flesh itself, which make a man infirm and weak, such as they are who have newly recovered from a grievous sickness; and indeed evil habits are by little and little driven out by the exercise of the virtues, but concupiscence can indeed be lessened, but ordinarily is not rooted out save in death: now although our own study and diligence avail much to the driving out of vices and the lessening of concupiscence, yet the chief part belongeth to the grace of God, without which we can do nothing, and with which we can do all things. Therefore the Prophet David, who knew these things best of all, wrote in *Ps.* cii: "Bless the Lord, O my soul, and never forget all he hath done for thee. Who forgiveth all thy iniquities: who healeth all thy diseases." And in this place, after he hath asked that his sin be blotted out, and hath heard from Nathan the prophet, II *Kings* xii: "God hath taken away thy sin: thou shalt not die,"[^4] he asketh again to be washed and cleansed, that is, to be more and more justified through a greater infusion of grace, that, the evil habits being taken away and concupiscence mortified, the soul may become whiter and more beautiful, and thereby also stronger to resist temptations. "Wash me yet more," saith he, "from my iniquity, and cleanse me from my sin," that is, I hope that my sin hath been blotted out by thy grace, and my soul washed and cleansed from the guilt and the blemish contracted by the act of sin; but I desire and ask to be washed again and again through the increase of grace, that my soul may come forth whiter and stronger. It might also be expounded more simply, so that this second petition pertain to declaring the greatness of the sin, as if he should say: If the blemish of my sin were light, a simple washing would suffice; but because the blemish is huge and manifold, therefore I desire to be washed much, greatly, more and yet more, that no trace of so great a blemish may remain.

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. FOR I KNOW MY INIQUITY, AND MY SIN IS ALWAYS BEFORE ME.**

This is the second reason by which he endeavoureth to persuade God to pardon the sinner: to wit, because he confesseth his sin, and punisheth it in himself by continual thought and remembrance. Pardon, saith he, "for I acknowledge my sin,"[^5] I do not excuse it, I do not deny it, nor do I only acknowledge it, but I think of it perpetually with grief of mind; for it "is always before me," that is, being set in my sight, it ceaseth not to pierce and torment me like a dart. This example we ought to imitate who recite the Penitential Psalms, that we may be able truly to say: "My sin is always before me," which will come to pass if the sin once committed, and haply blotted out by the grace of God, live in our memory, and always admonish us of our ingratitude toward so great a benefactor.

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. TO THEE ONLY HAVE I SINNED, AND HAVE DONE EVIL BEFORE THEE: THAT THOU MAYST BE JUSTIFIED IN THY WORDS, AND MAYST OVERCOME WHEN THOU ART JUDGED.**

[Margin: Why doth he say: "To thee only have I sinned," when he offended against others?]

This is the third reason why he asketh pardon of God, because he feareth no other judge. "To thee only," saith he, "have I sinned;" he saith not: Against thee only have I sinned; for he had sinned also against Urias, whom he had slain, and against Bethsabee, whom he had defiled by adultery, and against the people, whom he had scandalized; but he saith: "To thee only have I sinned," to wit, as to the judge before whom alone he could be convicted; for neither had he another judge, and if he had had one, he could not have been convicted, since the deed was done without a witness; and although from suspicion the report of the sin had spread among the common people, yet the sin could not be lawfully proved; but before God as judge he was convicted, both because God had seen, and because his own conscience was a witness before God, who searcheth the reins and hearts; and therefore he subjoineth, declaring it, "and have done evil before thee;" for although David sinned secretly, in the darkness of a closed chamber, yet he could not shut out the eyes of God. "That thou mayst be justified in thy words;" that *ut* (that) doth not signify the cause, but the consequence, as Theodoret hath noted on this place,

<!-- p. 339 (PDF 350) -->

and the sense is: "To thee only have I sinned, and have done evil before thee," from which it hath followed "that thou mayst be justified in thy words," while thou pronouncest me to be a sinner and unjust; or at least some verb is understood, so that this is the sense: I say: "To thee only have I sinned," I say, "I have done evil before thee," I confess myself unjust, that thou mayst be found just and true in thy words, by which through Nathan the prophet thou hast pronounced me to be guilty of adultery and murder. "And mayst overcome when thou art judged:" it is a repetition of the same sentence, as if he should say: And if a judgment be set between thee and me, and I should wish to deny the crime of which thou hast said that I am guilty, thou wouldst altogether win the cause, and I should lose it, when thou art judged with me. This I think to be the literal sense of this obscure sentence, which sense is greatly confirmed from the words of the Apostle, *Rom.* iii: "But God is true; and every man a liar, as it is written, That thou mayest be justified in thy words, and mayest overcome when thou art judged." But I do not reject that which others say: that God "is justified in his words, and overcometh when he is judged," if he pardon the penitent, since he hath promised pardon to the penitent. St. Augustine referreth this verse to Christ, because he alone is without sin, and therefore to him alone, the just one, do we the unjust sin, and he alone overcame when he was judged unjustly by the unjust.

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. FOR BEHOLD I WAS CONCEIVED IN INIQUITIES; AND IN SINS DID MY MOTHER CONCEIVE ME.**

[Margin: Original sin is here established.]

[Margin: The sin of our origin is the fountain of all sins.]

The fourth reason is drawn from our first origin, and from the sin of the first man poured over into us by generation, by which we are made infirm and prone to sin, and by reason of this as it were natural misery we are worthy of mercy. Now that this place is not understood of the sins of parents, but of original sin itself, which we draw from our conception, St. Augustine proveth on this place, because David was not born of adultery, but of the chaste marriage of godly parents. But this very thing we can prove from the property of the Hebrew words; for that, *conceptus sum* (I was conceived), is in the Hebrew חוללתי *cholalthi*; which doth not signify *actum gignendi* (the act of begetting), which pertaineth to the parents, but *actum formationis membrorum* (the act of the forming of the members); which forming is done in the womb a good while after the coming together of the parents. And the Holy Ghost seemeth by divine Providence not to have willed that there be written in this place *genitus sum* (I was begotten), but *conceptus,* or *formatus sum* (I was conceived, or formed), that he might prevent the perverse interpretations of the heretics. That also, *concepit me mater mea* (did my mother conceive me), is in the Hebrew יחמתני *iechemathni,* which properly signifieth *calefecit me* (she warmed me), or *fovit me* (she cherished me); accordingly it is not referred to the act of coming together, in which parents sin when they commit fornication or adultery, but to the action of cherishing the unborn child in the womb, while the members are formed, that the soul may be infused. Now although St. Jerome hath rendered: *Peperit me mater mea* (My mother brought me forth); and St. Augustine readeth: *Aluit me mater mea* (My mother nourished me); and we with the Seventy read: *Concepit me mater mea* (My mother conceived me): yet the property of the word is that which we have said, with which those various readings are not at variance: for a mother is rightly said *concipere* (to conceive) when the unborn child is formed and cherished in her womb: as she is also said *alere* (to nourish), not only when she nourisheth with milk the child already brought forth, but also when she preserveth and increaseth it in the womb; lastly St. Jerome hath not ill rendered: *In peccato peperit me mater mea* (In sin did my mother bring me forth), because she brought forth a sinner, whom she had conceived in sin. Add also that in the original text it is said in the singular, *in peccato conceptus sum, et in peccato concepit me mater mea* (I was conceived in sin, and in sin did my mother conceive me). For truly original sin, which we contract in our conception, is one, not many. But the Seventy Interpreters rendered in the plural number, *in iniquitatibus conceptus sum, et in peccatis concepit me mater mea* (I was conceived in iniquities; and in sins did my mother conceive me), because that one sin is as it were the fountain of all other sins, and in it all are included.

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. FOR BEHOLD THOU HAST LOVED TRUTH: THE UNCERTAIN AND HIDDEN THINGS OF THY WISDOM THOU HAST MADE MANIFEST TO ME.**

[Margin: There is no mystery of Christ and of the Church which David did not foresee.]

This is the fifth reason, which is drawn from the truth and simplicity of heart which was always in David, and which God is wont most greatly to love, for the sake of which also God, who is truth, revealed to David many mysteries to come: for, as St. John Chrysostom copiously teacheth on this place, there is scarce any mystery of Christ and of the Church which David did not foresee, and foretell in the Psalms. David therefore maketh mention of this truth, for the sake of which he knew so many divine mysteries; and because he persevereth in the same truth, simply confessing his sin, he thence asketh of God the gift of remission: "Behold," saith he, "thou hast loved truth," that is, thou, O God, hast always loved truth and sincerity of heart, as also on the contrary thou hast always hated doubleness and perversity. "The uncertain and hidden things of thy wisdom thou hast made manifest to me," that is, because thou lovest truth, and hast found in me truth and rightness of heart, therefore thou hast laid open to me so many divine mysteries, most secret and most hidden, which are signs of thy infinite wisdom. That, *uncertain,* doth not signify that the divine mysteries are uncertain, that is, doubtful in themselves, as though they might not come to pass; but *uncertain,* and doubtful, to us, as we say: The day of judgment is uncertain to men, not that it is not certain that there will be a day of judgment, but because it is uncertain to us when it will be. In the original text the Hebrew word signifieth *absconditum* (hidden) and *coopertum* (covered). Therefore those things are called *the uncertain* and *hidden things of* the divine *wisdom* which the divine wisdom hath ordained from eternity, that they may come to pass in their own times; but they are hidden and secret before they come to pass, unless they be revealed by a special privilege.

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. THOU SHALT SPRINKLE ME WITH HYSSOP, AND I SHALL BE CLEANSED: THOU SHALT WASH ME, AND I SHALL BE MADE WHITER THAN SNOW.**

He here openeth one of the uncertain and hidden mysteries of the divine wisdom, to wit, that in the time of the new Testament men were to be sprinkled with clean

<!-- p. 340 (PDF 351) -->

water in baptism, and most perfectly justified; and he alludeth to the ceremony which is described *Num.* chap. xix, where three things are said to be necessary for expiation, the ashes of a red cow sacrificed for a holocaust, water mixed with the ashes, and hyssop, with which the water was sprinkled: by the ashes of the red cow is signified the death of Christ; by the water, baptism; by the hyssop, faith: for hyssop is a lowly herb, having its roots in the rock. For as in that typical expiation the water cleansed, but as having its virtue from the ashes of the slain cow, and as sprinkled by the hyssop: so also in baptism the water cleanseth, but as having its virtue from the death and merit of Christ, and as applied by faith. To these expiations therefore, both that which figured and that which was figured, David looketh when he saith: "Thou shalt sprinkle me with hyssop, and I shall be cleansed;" for he asketh that cleansing which he knew to be figured by the expiation of the water sprinkled with hyssop, and which he foresaw and foretold as to be fulfilled in baptism; and to show that the principal author of that expiation is God, he saith: "Thou shalt sprinkle me with hyssop," and he saith not: The priest shall sprinkle me with hyssop; and to show that the expiation of baptism will be most perfect, so that it truly taketh away sins, that they be not only not imputed, but be not at all, and not only taketh away sins, but also poureth in plentiful grace, he addeth: "Thou shalt wash me, and I shall be made whiter than snow." See St. Augustine, book of *Questions on Numbers,* chap. 33, and Theodoret on this place of the Psalm.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. TO MY HEARING THOU SHALT GIVE JOY AND GLADNESS: AND THE BONES THAT HAVE BEEN HUMBLED SHALL REJOICE.**

It is the effect and sign of perfect justification when the Spirit giveth testimony to our spirit that we are the sons of God. The Prophet, having once experienced this testimony in himself, asketh it again, saying: "To my hearing thou shalt give joy and gladness," that is, when thou shalt have perfectly washed me, thou wilt add this benefit also, that thou wonderfully affect me with a certain inward joy, as with a sign of sin remitted: which joy will be as a good messenger, whom I shall pleasantly receive with the ear of the heart. In the Hebrew text there is one single word for that which we have: *Auditui meo dabis* (To my hearing thou shalt give), and it properly signifieth *audire me facies* (thou shalt make me hear). Therefore the sense is: Thou shalt make me hear the inward voice of the Holy Ghost, who shall fill me with joy and gladness. And then "the bones that have been humbled shall rejoice," that is, then the mind, broken and dissolved by the spirit of fear, shall rejoice, its strength being recovered. In the Hebrew text there is *et tunc exultabunt ossa quæ tu confregisti* (and then the bones which thou hast broken shall rejoice). From which we understand that the spirit of fear, by which the heart is made contrite and humbled, is from God, and disposeth to the spirit of love, by which the soul is justified. By the bones are understood the powers of the mind, not the bones of the body; for a little after also it is said: "A contrite and humbled heart, O God, thou wilt not despise."

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. TURN AWAY THY FACE FROM MY SINS, AND BLOT OUT ALL MY INIQUITIES.**

[Margin: How is God said to forget sins?]

[Margin: Against the imputative justice of the heretics.]

That which he foretold as to come, he asketh for himself in the present; for he had said: "Thou shalt sprinkle me with hyssop, and I shall be cleansed: thou shalt wash me, and I shall be made whiter than snow. To my hearing thou shalt give joy," etc.: now he asketh these very things. And first he asketh that his sins be remitted, as before he had said: "Thou shalt sprinkle me with hyssop, and I shall be cleansed:" "Turn away," saith he, "thy face from my sins," that is, consider not my sins, to punish them: as another of the Saints said, *Tob.* iii: "Remember not, O Lord, my offences."[^6] Yet all these are metaphorical manners of speech; for neither can it be that God, from whom nothing is hidden, should turn away his face from sins, or forget them; but he is said *to turn away his face,* or to forget, when he doth that which they do who consider not, or remember not: for they who consider not, or remember not, do not punish. Now he addeth: "And blot out all my iniquities," that God may not only not punish for a time, but may never punish at all: for he who turneth away his face from some writing can turn his face to it again, and consider what is written; but he who hath blotted out the writing can read it no more. This maketh against the heretics, who teach that sin in justification is not imputed, but yet remaineth, as though God did indeed turn away his face from sins, but never altogether blotted them out.

[Margin: Verse 11.]

**11. CREATE A CLEAN HEART IN ME, O GOD: AND RENEW A RIGHT SPIRIT WITHIN MY BOWELS.**

[Margin: How doth God create cleanness in the heart?]

This answereth to those words, "thou shalt wash me, and I shall be made whiter than snow:" for he asketh that he not only blot out sins, but also pour in justice, which may renew the soul, and make it fair and bright: which also is against the heretics, who place justification[^7] in the remission of sins alone. And that which he saith: "Create a clean heart in me, O God," is understood formally, as the schools speak, that is, of the creation of cleanness, not of the creation of the heart: for by sin the substance of the heart perisheth not, but its cleanness. Therefore the sense is: Create cleanness in my heart; and he saith properly enough *create,* since God findeth nothing in the heart of the sinner out of which he may make cleanness in it, but out of his great mercy he justifieth men without any merits: for although sinners are disposed to justification by faith and penance, yet faith, and penance, and all other like things are gifts of God. "And renew a right spirit within my bowels." It is an explanation of the foregoing words: for that we might understand that for a clean heart to be created is for cleanness to be created in the heart, he addeth: "And within my bowels renew a right spirit," and not, *renew my bowels.* By the bowels are understood the inward parts of the mind, that is, the will itself, which a little before was called the heart. By a right spirit is understood a right affection, which is nothing else but charity: for by cupidity the affection of the heart is made crooked, while it is turned to lower things, and chiefly to itself; but by

<!-- p. 341 (PDF 352) -->

charity it is made straight, while it is turned to things above, and chiefly to God. A right spirit therefore is renewed within the bowels when, the heart being cleansed by grace, there is renewed in the soul the godly affection toward God, which had been lost by sin, and into whose place crooked cupidity had succeeded.

[Margin: Verse 12.]

**12. CAST ME NOT AWAY FROM THY FACE; AND TAKE NOT THY HOLY SPIRIT FROM ME.**

[Margin: Who is cast away from the face of God?]

[Margin: No man is forsaken by God, unless he first forsake him.]

Here he now asketh perseverance, mindful, to wit, of his own frailty, lest haply, being raised up by grace, it befall him to slip again. He is said in Scripture *to be cast away from the face of God* who is dismissed and forsaken, no more to be received into favour, I *Kings* xvi: "The Lord said to Samuel: How long wilt thou mourn for Saul, whom I have rejected?"[^8] and II *Kings* vii: "My mercy I will not take away from him, as I took it from Saul, whom I removed from before my face;" and IV *Kings* xxiv: "The Lord was angry against Jerusalem and against Juda, till he cast them out from his face." He saith therefore: "Cast me not away from thy face," that is, suffer me not to slip again into sin, lest haply thou remove me for ever from thy grace: for it would profit me little to be washed, and made whiter than snow, and to have a right spirit renewed in me, if at length I were to be cast away from thy face with the reprobate. And that thou mayst not cast me away from thy face, "take not thy holy spirit from me," that is, bestow on me the gift of perseverance, bringing it to pass by thy grace that thy holy Spirit, continually abiding in me, may preserve a right spirit within my bowels. In this place it is to be noted that the dogma of the Church is true, of the Council of Trent, sess. VI, chap. xiii,[^9] of St. Augustine, tract. 2 *on John,* and of St. Prosper, answer 7 *to the Vincentian objections,* that no man is forsaken by God, unless he himself first forsake God, that is, that the Holy Ghost is not taken away from the just, unless they themselves by sinning extinguish the Spirit in themselves; yet that a man sin not, and extinguish not the spirit, pertaineth to the gift of perseverance, of which the Apostle saith: "We pray that you may do no evil," II *Cor.* xiii. Of this gift we understand this place of the Psalm; for when David saith: "Take not thy holy spirit from me," he doth not signify, *take it not away, if I shall sin,* but *take it not away, that I may not sin.*

[Margin: Verse 13.]

**13. RESTORE UNTO ME THE JOY OF THY SALVATION, AND STRENGTHEN ME WITH A PERFECT SPIRIT.**[^10]

This verse answereth to those words: "To my hearing thou shalt give joy and gladness;" for as he foretold that after true and perfect justification there would follow in man an inward joy, from the testimony of the spirit speaking within: so now, after he hath asked the remission of sin, and the infusion of grace with the gift of perseverance, he asketh the sign and effect of justification, saying: "Restore unto me the joy of thy salvation," that is, by sin I lost grace and the joy that followeth it, therefore as I have asked that the grace lost be restored to me, so in consequence I ask that the joy of thy salvation be restored, that is, the joy which is born of the salvation given by thee to me; and lest haply joy should lift him up, and bring in a dangerous security, he adjoineth: "And strengthen me with a princely spirit," that is, I ask that thou establish and strengthen me in good by a princely spirit inspired into me by thee. In the Hebrew text there is the word נדיבה *nedibah,* which signifieth *principem* (princely), and *spontaneum* (willing), or *liberalem* (liberal): and it can be referred both to the Holy Ghost, who truly is the prince of all spirits, and most liberal and most bountiful, since he is the fountain of all gifts; and to the spirit, that is, the affection of David himself, which is asked of God such as becometh a very good prince, who ought to be willing toward God, and liberal toward the people. St. Augustine, and St. Jerome in his *Commentary,* and St. Bernard, sermon 3 *Of Pentecost,* write that by the princely spirit may be understood God the Father; by the right Spirit, God the Son; by the holy Spirit, God the Holy Ghost. But this seemeth a godly, yet not a literal exposition.

[Margin: Verse 14.]

**14. I WILL TEACH THE UNJUST THY WAYS: AND THE WICKED SHALL BE CONVERTED TO THEE.**

[Margin: David was to all posterity an example of the divine justice and mercy.]

He setteth out the fruit of his justification, which will redound to the glory of God, and the profit of many. I, saith he, being received by thee into favour after so grievous offences, "will teach," both by words and by example, "thy ways," to wit, mercy and justice: for "all the ways of the Lord are mercy and truth;"[^11] and thence it will follow that the wicked, taught by my example, be converted to thee. Now David was to all posterity a signal example of the divine mercy and justice: of mercy indeed, because after so grievous crimes, as soon as he said from his heart: "I have sinned against the Lord," his sin was remitted to him; and of justice, because the Lord took most grievous temporal punishments of him, so that not only did the son born of the adultery die immediately, but also a little after he himself was cast out from the throne of his kingdom, his wives were publicly violated by his own son, and two of his sons, Amon and Absalon, were slain. And this example profited not only the men of that age, but also all the rest even to the consummation of the world; for the Psalm composed by him concerning this matter will be and is everywhere in frequent use, as long as the state of the Church militant shall continue. Therefore David fulfilled what he promised when he put forth this Psalm: for he taught the unjust the ways of the Lord, and many sinners have been converted to God, and others hereafter without doubt will be converted. It is likely also that David after his restoration preached the divine pardon to many, and that by his exhortation not a few sinners were converted to God.

[Margin: Verse 15.]

**15. DELIVER ME FROM BLOOD, O GOD, THOU GOD OF MY SALVATION: AND MY TONGUE SHALL EXTOL THY JUSTICE.**

A little before David prayed for the washing away of his sin, and promised that he would teach sinners the ways of the Lord:

<!-- p. 342 (PDF 353) -->

[Margin: The merit of Christ wrought salvation among the ancients.]

now he prayeth for deliverance from the punishment or vengeance which the blood of Urias, unjustly shed, demanded, and promiseth that he will praise the justice of God. Now why he saith *de sanguinibus* (from bloods), and not rather *de sanguine* (from blood), as the Latin phrase required, St. Augustine giveth the reason, that the Interpreter wished to keep the number which he found in the Hebrew text: and in the Hebrew text the plural number is put, מדמים *middamim,* to signify a great shedding of blood, as *Gen.* iv: "The voice of thy brother's blood crieth to me from the earth." In the Hebrew there is קול דמי, *vox sanguinum* (the voice of bloods), that is, the voice of much blood shed. So therefore in this place it is said: "Deliver me from blood," that is, deliver me from the voice of the much blood of Urias, which, unjustly shed by me, crieth to thee, and demandeth vengeance against me; and he saith: "Deliver me," because he saw that blood standing against him as an armed soldier. Properly also he addeth: "O God, thou God of my salvation;" for it is the property of a saviour to deliver from imminent danger. And this also is the reason why he adjoineth: "And my tongue shall extol thy justice;" for true deliverance and salvation was wrought through the merit of Christ foreseen, as it is now wrought through the merit of Christ in very deed exhibited. Now the merit of Christ most exactly containeth justice, and such as deserveth to be praised with the whole heart and with the greatest straining of the voice. And this the Hebrew word תרנן *therannen* signifieth, for which we have *exultabit* (shall extol), that is, shall praise with exulting and crying aloud. St. Augustine and others by *bloods* understand sins; but, as Euthymius hath well noted, the literal sense is to be referred to the true blood of Urias: yet spiritually by blood sins can be understood, because sins arise from evil concupiscence, and in concupiscence blood chiefly beareth rule.

[Margin: Verse 16.]

**16. O LORD, THOU WILT OPEN MY LIPS: AND MY MOUTH SHALL DECLARE THY PRAISE.**

This now followeth from the perfect justification and deliverance of the sinner, that the lips which were wont to praise God, and which had been closed by sin, are opened again through pardon to thanksgiving and the praise of the Redeemer. He saith therefore: "O Lord, thou wilt open my lips," that is, thou indeed, by pardoning and forgiving sins, and by restoring confidence and gladness, "wilt open my lips," and then "my mouth shall declare thy praise," by proclaiming thy mercy and justice, not only to men present, but also to those to come.

[Margin: Verse 17.]

**17. FOR IF THOU HADST DESIRED SACRIFICE, I WOULD INDEED HAVE GIVEN IT: WITH BURNT OFFERINGS THOU WILT NOT BE DELIGHTED.**

[Margin: How did the sacrifices of the ancients please God, or not?]

He giveth the reason why he wisheth to offer the sacrifice of praise, to wit, because the sacrifices of cattle do not please God, as if he had said: "My mouth shall declare thy praise," because I know that thou art delighted with the sacrifice of praise, not with the sacrifices of brute animals; for if they pleased thee, I would not have delayed to offer them. Now the sense is not that the sacrifices of cattle in no way please God, since it is sufficiently certain from the book of Leviticus that they were instituted by God, and commanded to be offered. But they are said not to please of themselves, as though the slaughter of cattle were of itself a work pleasing to God, or profitable, or necessary; likewise they are said not to please if they be compared with the sacrifice of the eucharist, as is plain from the first chapter of Malachias, where the old sacrifices are said to be about to cease, when the clean oblation shall be offered among all nations; they are said also not to please when they are offered by sinners, as is said *Isa.* chap. i, since "obedience pleaseth God more than the offering of victims,"[^12] I *Kings* chap. xv. Lastly they are said not to please for the expiating of sins, since "it is impossible," as the Apostle saith, *Heb.* x, "that with the blood of oxen and goats sin should be taken away;" and in this sense David saith in this place: "If thou hadst desired sacrifice," for the remission of my sin, "I would indeed have given it;" but because "with burnt offerings thou wilt not be delighted," so as for their sake to forgive me my sins, therefore "my mouth shall declare thy praise:" for, as we said in the explanation of the Psalm above, the sacrifice of praise is most pleasing to God, because it is kindled upon the altar of the heart by the fire of charity. In the Hebrew it seemeth to read a little otherwise. For thus to the word it is read, *quoniam non voles sacrificium, et dabo: holocaustum non placebit* (for thou wilt not desire sacrifice, and I will give it: a burnt offering will not please). But it seemeth that the negation must be understood again when it is said, *et dabo* (and I will give it), so that the sense is, *quoniam non voles sacrificium, ego quoque non dabo* (for thou wilt not desire sacrifice, I also will not give it). Or perhaps the Seventy Interpreters in place of the negation read the conditional particle; for, that being set, the Hebrew text can be read thus: *Quoniam si velles sacrificium, et darem* (For if thou didst desire sacrifice, I would also give it); which is the same with that which we have from the version of the Seventy: "If thou hadst desired sacrifice, I would indeed have given it."

[Margin: Verse 18.]

**18. A SACRIFICE TO GOD IS AN AFFLICTED SPIRIT: A CONTRITE AND HUMBLED HEART, O GOD, THOU WILT NOT DESPISE.**

[Margin: What is an afflicted spirit?]

He explaineth more clearly what sacrifice of praise pleaseth God, to wit, that which proceedeth from a contrite and humbled heart, that is, when a man, acknowledging his own misery and the mercy of God, is humbled under his mighty hand, and ascribeth to him honour and glory, but to himself shame and confusion. *Dan.* ix: "To thee, O Lord, justice: but to us confusion of face;" and a little after: "O Lord, to us belongeth confusion of face, to our kings, to our princes, and to our fathers that have sinned. But to thee, the Lord our God, mercy and forgiveness." An afflicted spirit is the same as a contrite heart: for in the Hebrew copy, and in the Greek copy, the same word is repeated, *spiritus contritus* (a contrite spirit) and *cor contritum* (a contrite heart); but the Interpreter chose rather to vary the words; and the sense is the same: for a soul touched with grief, and thereby set in affliction for sin committed against God, is called *an afflicted spirit*: so also a soul which, from the grief conceived for

<!-- p. 343 (PDF 354) -->

sin committed, is as it were rent, and reduced to dust, no longer retaining a stony hardness and resistance, is called a contrite spirit. Now this contrition of spirit is a sacrifice most pleasing to God, because as God is offended by sins, so he is appeased by penance; and therefore it is rightly subjoined: "A contrite and humbled heart, O God, thou wilt not despise," "for God despiseth the proud, and resisteth them; but to the humble," who willingly subject themselves to him, "he always giveth grace,"[^13] *James* iv.

[Margin: Verse 19.]

**19. DEAL FAVOURABLY, O LORD, IN THY GOOD WILL WITH SION; THAT THE WALLS OF JERUSALEM MAY BE BUILT UP.**

This is the last reason which David useth to appease God, and to obtain perfect justification and restoration after so grievous a fall: for he saith that this his restoration will profit the whole people, as his ruin would hurt the whole people. Therefore he asketh that he grant this benefit to the city of Sion and Jerusalem itself. "Deal favourably, O Lord, in thy good will with Sion," that is, if I am not worthy to be heard, regard the city of which I am the head, and do good to it by healing its head, "in thy good will," that is, according to thy good pleasure, whereby it hath pleased thee to choose this city to thyself for thy own and peculiar seat. "That the walls of Jerusalem may be built up," that is, that so the walls of Jerusalem which have fallen may be built again. He calleth himself the walls of Jerusalem, who after the manner of walls kept and defended the whole people, by which metaphor it is said also of Christ the King: "A saviour, a wall and a bulwark shall be set therein," *Isa.* xxvi; and: "I will make thee to this people as a wall of brass," *Jer.* xv. In the Hebrew it is read *ædifica muros Jerusalem* (build up the walls of Jerusalem), which have fallen by my fall; restore to me a plentiful spirit and abundant grace, that I may be able to defend and keep thy people after the manner of walls.

[Margin: Verse 20.]

**20. THEN SHALT THOU ACCEPT THE SACRIFICE OF JUSTICE, OBLATIONS AND WHOLE BURNT OFFERINGS: THEN SHALL THEY LAY CALVES UPON THY ALTAR.**

[Margin: What is it to lay calves upon the altar?]

The effects of justification are the works of justice, which please God as true spiritual sacrifices, according to that of the Apostle, *Heb.* xiii, 16: "Do not forget to do good, and to impart; for by such sacrifices God's favour is obtained;" and I *Pet.* ii: "Offer up spiritual sacrifices, acceptable to God by Jesus Christ."[^14] "Then," saith he, that is, when I shall have been perfectly restored and justified, "shalt thou accept the sacrifice of justice," to wit, my good works, and those of my people; "oblations and whole burnt offerings," that is, which good works will be either spiritual oblations, or spiritual whole burnt offerings. They are spiritual oblations when a man out of charity giveth his goods, bestowing[^15] alms; but whole burnt offerings when he giveth himself wholly to the service of God, according to that of *Rom.* xii: "I beseech you, brethren, that you present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy," etc. "Then shall they lay calves upon thy altar," that is, when men shall see that these sacrifices of justice are most acceptable to thee, many will run together, and will strive to lay upon thy altar sacrifices, not of any kind whatsoever, but the best of all: for the sacrifice of a calf was the most excellent of all sacrifices; and thereby to lay calves upon the altar is nothing else but to offer to the Lord God works of perfect justice. St. Augustine after his manner expoundeth this place of the building of the heavenly Jerusalem, where sacrifices of justice, perfect on every side, shall be offered by all. Theodoret and Euthymius expound it also mystically of the building of the Christian Church, which sojourneth upon earth; the same also add that these two verses can be understood of the rebuilding of Jerusalem, which was done in the time of Nehemias, to wit, so that David in spirit, after he had bewailed his own sin, and obtained remission, foresaw the common sin of the whole nation, for which Jerusalem was to be overthrown, and at the same time prayed for the remission of that sin, and the restoration of the city.
