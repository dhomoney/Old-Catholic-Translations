BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XXVIII.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*A psalm for David, at the finishing of the tabernacle.*

This title is not in the Hebrew, but only, *A Psalm of David*; the rest was added either by the Seventy Interpreters, or by others, and the title of this Psalm is read and understood in two ways. For St. Basil, Theodoret and Euthymius read: *Psalmus David in exitu tabernaculi* (A Psalm of David at the going out of the tabernacle), and will have it signified, that the Priests and Levites going out of the tabernacle are commanded to prepare the things which are necessary for the worship of God. SS. Jerome and Augustine read, as we have in the Vulgate edition: *Psalmus David in consummatione tabernaculi* (A Psalm of David at the finishing of the tabernacle), and St. Jerome will have the sense to be, that this Psalm was composed when David finished the tabernacle, and placed in it the ark of the Lord. As to the argument, some will have it that according to the letter almost the whole Psalm treateth of the power of God, and that David would exhort the people to praise God, who is wont to manifest his power by wonderful works, which he doth by his word or command alone. Others, whose interpretation seemeth more sublime, and more worthy of the spirit of David, will have it that in this Psalm the preaching of the Gospel is celebrated. And the title agreeth, *at the finishing of the tabernacle*: for since David understood that the tabernacle of the covenant was a figure of the Church, which is called *the tabernacle of God with men* in *Apoc.* chap. xxi, and at the same time also knew, the Spirit revealing it, that the instrument by which this new tabernacle was to be finished would be the preaching of the Gospel, therefore at the finishing of that shadowy tabernacle, the Holy Ghost inspiring him, he sang the praises of the voice of the Lord, by which the true tabernacle is established. We shall briefly follow out both explanations.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. BRING TO THE LORD, O YE CHILDREN OF GOD: BRING TO THE LORD THE OFFSPRING OF RAMS.**[^1]

The Prophet, being about to sing the praises of the divine power, stirreth up the children of God, that is, the peculiar people of God, to whom God was known (for "in Judea God is known, his name is great in Israel," as it is in *Ps.* lxxv), to honour that power with the victims customary at that time, and also with the proclaiming of the voice, and the bowing of the body. But according to the latter understanding, being about to sing the praises of the divine voice, by which the tabernacle is finished, that is the Church, which is the mother of all the children of God, he inviteth the children of God themselves, those very ones called by that heavenly voice, to sacrifice to God the sacrifice of praise. "Bring," saith he, "O ye children of God, the offspring of rams," that is, ye who by the blood of the Lamb

<!-- p. 163 (PDF 174) -->

without spot have been made children of God, bring lambs, offer the sacrifice of thanksgiving and praise; for so the same Prophet himself explaineth in the following verse: "Bring to the Lord glory," etc. In the Hebrew there is not: "Bring to the Lord, O ye children of God," but only הבו ליהוה בני אלים. *habu l'* ADONAI *bene elim*, *bring to the Lord the offspring of rams*. Perhaps in the edition which the Seventy Interpreters had, there were both parts of the verse, and afterwards in course of time the former part fell out; perhaps also there was only the latter part: but it was interpreted in two ways, and the other interpretation, which was in the margin, crept into the text, and hence arose the reading which we have. For if the word אלים *elim* be read as it now standeth, it signifieth *rams*; if it be read with a single letter added, אלהים *Eloim*, it signifieth *God*; and so the same sentence could be rendered: *Bring to the Lord, O ye children of God*; and also: *Bring to the Lord the offspring of rams*. Nor are there wanting in the Vulgate edition other places, in which two interpretations of the same sentence are found together in the text. Certainly St. Basil in his Commentary on this Psalm writeth, that in many copies these words were added: "Bring to the Lord, O ye children of God." From which we understand that these words were not in all the Greek copies, but only in some. But whencesoever these words came into our edition, they agree well enough with what followeth, and are found in all the Latin copies, even the most ancient.

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. BRING TO THE LORD GLORY AND HONOUR: BRING TO THE LORD GLORY TO HIS NAME: ADORE YE THE LORD IN HIS HOLY COURT.**

This is the declaration of the sacrifice, which the Prophet admonisheth us to offer to the Lord: "Bring," saith he, "to the Lord glory and honour," that is, with voice and work glorify the Lord: and not only with voice and work praise God, but also venerate him with the bowing of the body, and by true adoration acknowledge and profess him the supreme Lord. In the Hebrew for the word *honorem* (honour), there is עז *hoz*, which properly signifieth *strength, power*, or *command*. From which we understand that the honour, which according to the Greek and Latin edition we owe to God, is the honour of confessing the power and command of the Lord; for then we truly honour God, when we acknowledge and confess his power and command. And the honour of power is very much to the purpose in this place, since in the whole Psalm the power of God is praised. For *nomini ejus* (to his name), in the Hebrew copies with the points of the Rabbins there is, *nominis ejus* (of his name): which reading hath an easier sense than ours. But because the Greek edition hath, *nomini ejus*, and St. Jerome rendereth in the same way from the Hebrew, the Seventy Interpreters and St. Jerome must have read otherwise than the Rabbins read. Namely those read, כבוד שמו *cavod shemo*, *gloriam nomini ejus* (glory to his name); but these, *chevod shemo*, *gloriam nominis ejus* (the glory of his name). The sense therefore is: "Bring to the Lord glory to his name," that is, bring glory to the Lord, that is, to his name, celebrating namely the name, the fame, the knowledge of the Lord. For the word *atrio* (court), in the Hebrew there is הדרת *hadrath*, *comeliness*, or *beauty*; in the Greek there is αὐλὴ; whence St. Augustine and others read *aula* (hall). But it is easy to see that the Seventy Interpreters did not read הדרת *hadrath*, but חדרת *chadrath*, a single very like letter being changed: for that word, if it be read with ה, signifieth *comeliness*, if with ח, signifieth *court*, and that this is the true reading we cannot doubt, unless we would charge the Seventy men, who in the judgment of all were most wise, with want of skill; to pass over that *in his holy court* agreeth better with the words, "Adore ye the Lord," than *in his holy comeliness*. Moreover the Greek word αὐλὴ doth not signify that which we in Latin call *aula* (a hall), but a porch, or a court open to the sky; and so they who read *in aula* keep the sound of the Greek word; they who read *in atrio* keep the true signification of both the Hebrew and the Greek word. By this holy court, in which we are bidden to adore God, is understood both the very porch of the Jewish tabernacle, to which there was access for all without distinction, whereas the priests alone were permitted to enter into the tabernacle; and chiefly the Catholic Church, which is as it were the court or porch of the heavenly tabernacle: for into the Church both good and bad are everywhere admitted, but into the heavenly tabernacle those alone shall enter, who shall be able to say to Christ, as we read in the Apocalypse: "Thou hast made us to our God a kingdom and priests," *Apoc.* v.

[Margin: The Catholic Church is the porch of the heavenly tabernacle.]

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. THE VOICE OF THE LORD IS UPON THE WATERS; THE GOD OF MAJESTY HATH THUNDERED, THE LORD IS UPON MANY WATERS.**

Here now the Prophet beginneth to explain why he hath invited us to praise and celebrate the power of God. For the cause is, that "the voice of the Lord" worketh wonders both in the bodily elements, and in the spiritual fabric of the Church. He describeth first the operation of God in the waters, then in the air, then in fire, lastly in the earth, which are the principal parts of this lower world best known to us. The first operation of God in the waters is described in *Gen.* chap. i, when it is said: "The spirit of the Lord moved over the waters:"[^2] and in the same place: "God said: Let there be a firmament made amidst the waters, and let it divide the waters from the waters;" and in the same place: "God said: Let the waters that are under the heaven be gathered together into one place, and let the dry land appear." Then therefore "the voice of the Lord" was "upon the waters," when God commanded that the waters should be divided, and being divided should be gathered together into one place, that is, into the caverns of the earth, and the earth should become habitable. This very voice is called thunder: "The God of majesty hath thundered," because as thunder straightway casteth us down, and compelleth us to obey: so at the command of the Lord the waters straightway drew back, and betook themselves into lower places. Now this voice, and this thunder of God was upon many waters, because then the waters on every side covered the whole earth, and so there was as it were an immense deep of waters upon the earth. This very thing the same David describeth more clearly in

<!-- p. 164 (PDF 175) -->

*Ps.* ciii: "The deep like a garment is its clothing, above the mountains shall the waters stand," that is, the immensity of the waters clothed the earth on every side, so that the waters stood above all the mountains. "At thy rebuke they shall flee: at the voice of thy thunder they shall fear: the mountains ascend, and the plains descend, into the place which thou hast founded for them," that is, at God's command the waters drew back, as though they fled from thunder, and then appeared the lofty mountains and the low plains. "Thou hast set a bound which they shall not pass over: neither shall they return to cover the earth," that is, not only did the waters draw back at the voice of the Lord, and render the earth dry and habitable, but from the same voice of the Lord they received a bound, which they will never dare to pass. But now according to the other exposition there is described the beginning of the preaching of the Gospel, which began, when God himself by himself, Christ being baptized in the waters of the Jordan, preached to the whole world that Jesus Christ is his Son, which is as it were an abridgment of the whole Gospel. Therefore "the voice of the Lord upon the waters" is that magnificent voice of God heard at the baptism of Christ: "This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased," *Matt.* iii. Then "the God of majesty thundered, and thundered upon many waters," because then baptism was instituted, and virtue given to all the waters of the whole world for regenerating the children of God. St. Augustine, by the voice of the Lord upon the waters, understandeth the voice of Christ upon the peoples, because it is said in *Apoc.* chap. xvii: "The many waters are many peoples;" and he addeth that therefore it was said: "The God of majesty hath thundered," because the divine majesty was in the human flesh of Christ as in a cloud, and so the voice of the Lord from human flesh was as it were thunder from a cloud. An elegant and true exposition; but the former, which is that of the Greek Fathers, seemeth more literal.

[Margin: That Jesus Christ is the Son of God is an abridgment of the whole Gospel.]

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. THE VOICE OF THE LORD IS IN POWER; THE VOICE OF THE LORD IN MAGNIFICENCE.**

To both interpretations this praise of the Lord's voice fitteth excellently. For the voice of the Lord commanding that in the beginning of the creation of all things the waters should be divided, and the lower waters betake themselves into the caverns of the earth, and not return to cover the earth, was not empty and without effect, as most thunders are wont to be, which have nothing but a mighty sound; but it was a voice full of power and glory, and the effect straightway followed upon it. So also the voice of the preaching of the Gospel, begun by God himself, and spread by Christ and the Apostles, was not an empty noise of words, such as is that of many orators and philosophers; but it was a most efficacious voice, and confirmed by signs and wonders. To the efficacy of the word pertaineth that phrase, *in power*; to the glory of the miracles that, *in magnificence*, or brightness and comeliness, as the Hebrew word הדר *hadar* soundeth. The Apostle Paul, I *Cor.* ii: "My preaching," saith he, "was not in the persuasive words of human wisdom, but in shewing of the Spirit and power;" and I *Thess.* chap. i: "My gospel hath not been unto you in word only, but in power and in the Holy Ghost."

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. THE VOICE OF THE LORD BREAKETH THE CEDARS: YEA, THE LORD SHALL BREAK THE CEDARS OF LIBANUS.**

According to the former interpretation the Prophet passeth from the operation of God in the waters to the operation in the air. Now he signifieth by these words that *the voice of the Lord*, that is, the command of the Lord, raiseth winds and tempests, which in Psalm cxlviii he calleth "stormy winds which fulfil his word." And truly wonderful is the power of God, which giveth to a thing so slight and thin so great a force, that it breaketh and layeth low even the greatest trees in a moment, which many men with great force and in a long time would scarce do. Now the Prophet nameth *cedars*, and *the cedars of Libanus*, which are trees most hard and most great, and nowhere are they found greater than on mount Libanus. But now according to the latter interpretation "the cedars of Libanus," by reason of their height, are men who are high, whether in greatness of power, or in splendour of wisdom, or in glory of eloquence; by reason of the good odour which cedars breathe, they are men given to pleasures and delights; lastly by reason of the closeness and hardness, in which cedars wonderfully excel, they are men obstinate and stubborn in their fathers' errors. All these cedars the preaching of the Gospel hath magnificently broken, and brought to Christian humility and meekness, and to bring forth fruits worthy of penance. The histories are full of examples.

[Margin: Who are the cedars of Libanus morally?]

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. AND SHALL REDUCE THEM TO PIECES, AS A CALF OF LIBANUS, AND AS THE BELOVED SON OF UNICORNS.**

From the Hebrew according to the former understanding there is an easy and most beautiful sense. For thus we read in the Hebrew: *Et saltare faciet eas, sicut vitulum Libanum, et Sarion sicut filium unicornium* (And he shall make them leap, as a calf, Libanus, and Sarion as the son of unicorns), and the sense is: Not only shall the voice of the Lord break the cedars of Libanus, but it shall also pluck up whole cedars by the roots, and make them leap forth with that ease with which calves leap. And not only the cedars of Libanus, but the very mountains also, Libanus and Sarion, it shall make to leap with that ease[^3] with which the son of unicorns leapeth. Like to which sentence is that in *Ps.* cxiii: "The mountains skipped like rams, and the hills like the lambs of the flock." According to the latter understanding the sense will be: The voice of the preaching of the Gospel shall not only break the cedars of Libanus, that is, bend men however high and obstinate to the humility of the Christian faith, but shall also root up the same cedars, and make them leap forth into another place, that is, shall draw them wholly away from earthly affections, and transfer them to a life almost angelic: which agreeth with apostolic men, who became not only faithful, but most religious and most perfect, so that they seemed not so much men, as earthly angels. And not only single cedars, but a whole mountain full of cedars, or whole

<!-- p. 165 (PDF 176) -->

forests of cedars the preaching of the Gospel shall make to leap forth, that is, it shall bring not only this or that man, but a vast multitude of men, and whole companies to faith and religion, and a perfect life. But we must see what the Greek and Latin edition hath, between which the only difference is, that in the Greek is read, *et comminuet eas tanquam vitulum Libanum* (and shall reduce them to pieces as a calf, Libanus); but in the Latin, *et comminuet eas tanquam vitulum Libani* (and shall reduce them to pieces as a calf of Libanus). Now although St. Basil and Euthymius read *Libanum*, yet it is probable that in the more correct Greek editions there was *Libani*, as SS. Jerome and Augustine read, and the most ancient copy which the church of St. Peter at Rome useth in singing the Psalms. And indeed those former words: "And shall reduce them to pieces, as a calf of Libanus," can without difficulty be reconciled with the Hebrew words: for when cedars are broken and reduced to pieces with great violence, certainly the fragments leap forth, and are scattered into divers parts. And so the sense will be: And he shall reduce them to pieces, namely the cedars, and thereby make them leap forth in fragments and pieces, as the calves of mount Libanus are wont to leap forth and skip. But the following words: "And as the beloved son of unicorns," can with more difficulty be reconciled with the Hebrew words which are now in the Bibles: for *Sirion*, for which we have *dilectus* (beloved), signifieth mount Hermon, *Deut.* iii; and a coat of mail, I *Kings* xvii, and III *Kings* xxii. It must needs be therefore that the Seventy Interpreters either read something else, or at least understood that that word, which signifieth mount Hermon, and a coat of mail, things namely so unlike, signifieth also beloved; and they are to be believed, since they were most learned, and most skilled in the Hebrew tongue. Now this reading being laid down, according to the former understanding, this will be the sense: "And shall reduce them to pieces," namely the cedars, and by reducing them to pieces shall make the fragments of the cedars leap forth, "as a calf of Libanus," that is, as a calf leapeth over mount Libanus, and "as the beloved son of unicorns," that is, as the beloved son of unicorns leapeth. Now he is called the beloved son of unicorns, because it is a fair and lovable animal, especially when it is a young unicorn. Others expound thus: And he shall reduce to pieces the hardest cedars, as the tenderest calves, and the tenderest sons of unicorns can be reduced to pieces; but our exposition cometh nearer to the Hebrew reading. According to the latter understanding the sense will be: The preaching of the Gospel shall not only break the cedars of Libanus, that is, humble powerful and wise men, but shall also reduce them to pieces as a calf of Libanus, that is, shall bring the highest cedars to the littleness of a small calf, which feedeth on mount Libanus. Now by the calf is rightly understood Christ, who not only was humble and meek after the manner of a sucking calf, but was also immolated in sacrifice to God. "And the beloved as the son of unicorns," that is, and then when the cedars of Libanus shall have been broken, the beloved shall appear, who is Christ most beloved of the Father, and desired of all nations: he shall

[Margin: Christ is understood by the calf.]

appear, I say, no longer as an unarmed calf, but as the son of the most strong unicorn. For the divine majesty and omnipotence of Christ then first began to appear, when through the Gospel preaching of fishermen, orators and philosophers, and even the very princes of the world began to believe in Christ. Of the strength of the unicorn or rhinoceros, see *Job* chap. xxxvi.[^4]

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. THE VOICE OF THE LORD DIVIDETH THE FLAME OF FIRE: THE VOICE OF THE LORD SHAKETH THE DESERT: AND THE LORD SHALL SHAKE THE DESERT OF CADES.**

In the first part of this verse, which among the Hebrews is a whole verse, the Prophet passeth from the operation of God in the air to the operation in fire, and saith that *the voice of the Lord*, that is, the command and power of the Lord, *cutteth asunder*, that is divideth, *the flames of fire*: which is done, when at the Lord's command divers thunderbolts are sent from the clouds, as from heavenly furnaces, upon the earth, than which nothing more terrible and dreadful is wont to befall mortals. That cutting asunder, or dividing of the flames, can also be referred not only to the multitude and variety of the thunderbolts, but also to the fineness and sharpness of the thunderbolts themselves; for things that are sharpened are divided and cut, that they may be brought to fineness; and there is nothing at all in bodily things sharper, finer, more piercing than the thunderbolt of heaven; whence is that in the canticle of Moses, *Deut.* xxxii: "If I shall whet my sword as the lightning." According to the other understanding, "the voice of the Lord," that is, the preaching of the Gospel, "divideth the flames of fire," while through it the Holy Ghost darteth into the hearts of men the fire of divine love, in many and various ways; in sign of which there appeared on the day of Pentecost upon the believers "parted tongues as it were of fire," *Acts* ii. In the other part of the verse the Prophet celebrateth the power of God working in the earth, and saith that "the voice of the Lord shaketh and moveth the desert." The Hebrew word יחיל *iachil* signifieth *shall make to travail*, from which we understand that the shaking and moving, which is expressed in the Greek and Latin edition, is not a simple earthquake, or a shaking from the crash of thunder resounding peculiarly in the desert, as some will have it; but is a shaking and moving unto bringing forth. And so there is signified in this place the wonderful power of God, which as it can, when it will, strip mount Libanus of all its cedars, and make it a desert, so on the contrary can make the very desert of Cades, where nothing is wont to grow, to bring forth by a single shaking, and straightway fill it with trees and living creatures. Like is that which is said in *Ps.* cvi: "He hath turned rivers into a wilderness, a fruitful land into barrenness;" and a little after: "He hath turned a wilderness into pools of waters, and hath placed there the hungry, and they made a city for their habitation, and they sowed fields, and planted vineyards." But according to the other understanding it is signified, that at the preaching of the Gospel even the barbarous nations were to be converted,

<!-- p. 166 (PDF 177) -->

which before had lacked spiritual tillage and the watering of the grace of God, so that in comparison of other nations they might be called desert. What if we should say that here also is signified, that true deserts were to bring forth infinite troops of monks, and thereby, as St. Jerome speaketh in the Epistle *to Heliodorus*, that the deserts would be blooming with the flowers of Christ? For that in this very desert of Cades there were very many monasteries in the time of St. Jerome, he himself witnesseth in the *Life of St. Hilarion*.

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. THE VOICE OF THE LORD PREPARETH THE STAGS: AND HE WILL DISCOVER THE THICK WOODS: AND IN HIS TEMPLE ALL SHALL SPEAK HIS GLORY.**

According to the former understanding the Prophet, who hath praised the power of God in all the elements, water, air,[^5] fire and earth, now praiseth it in living creatures and plants, being about a little after to praise it in men. He saith therefore: "The voice of the Lord prepareth the stags;" where he setteth, by way of example, the operation of God in the stags. For, as we have in the book of Job, chap. xxxix, hinds bring forth with the greatest difficulty, and the cause seemeth to be, that they bring forth young so perfect, that these straightway go to feed, and return not to the mother, as is said in the same place. Wonderful therefore is the power of God seen in this, that he maketh the hinds to bring forth. In the Hebrew there is, קול יהוה יהולל אילות *kol* ADONAI *iccholel aialoth*, *the voice of the Lord making the hinds to bring forth*; or, as St. Jerome rendereth, *vox Domini obstetricans cervis* (the voice of the Lord doing the midwife's office to the hinds). The Greek and Latin words signify the same: for *to prepare the stags* is nothing else in this place, but to prepare for bringing forth. The noun *cervos* (stags) would more fitly be read *cervas* (hinds): for in the Hebrew it is a feminine noun, and in the Greek it is a noun of common gender, ἐλάφους. But *præparantis cervos* (preparing the stags) can also be rightly understood, for the masculine noun is put to express deer in general, not distinguishing the sexes, as if it were said: *The voice of the Lord prepareth the stags*, that is, the deer kind for bringing forth, which kind without a peculiar providence of God could not bring forth. In the following words: "And he will discover the thick woods," or, as is more openly read in the Hebrew, *he will open the forests*, is signified the operation of God in the trees, and the sense is, that to God nothing is shut, but all things open; for he pierceth all things, even the thickest forests, and worketh everywhere; and therefore: "In his temple all speak his glory," that is, all creatures praise and glorify God in this universe of things, which is as it were his temple. In the Hebrew there is כלו *coullo*, *all that itself*, or this whole, *shall speak glory*: therefore we expounded *all*, that is, all creatures: for he speaketh not only of men, but of all that which had been said, that is water, fire, etc. According to the other more sublime understanding, *the voice of the Lord prepareth the stags*, when the preaching of the Gospel prepareth spiritual men, seeking high things, and slaying venomous serpents by their spirit, for the bringing forth of wonderful works: for what is greater and more wonderful, than that a weak man should work works meritorious of everlasting life? And when the voice of the Lord shall have made all the stags to bring forth, then at last *he will discover the thick woods*, that is, as St. Basil expoundeth in the second exposition of this Psalm, "he will bring to light the hidden things of darkness, and will make manifest the counsels of the hearts;" at the last judgment, according to the Apostle, I *Cor.* iv; and then the justice of God will be so evident, that in that great theatre and temple all without exception, both good and bad, shall glorify God. For then "every knee shall bow to Christ," and all, either unwilling or gladly, shall say: "Thou art just, O Lord, and thy judgment is right;" thus in his temple all shall speak his glory. St. Augustine by that phrase, *he will discover the thick woods*, understandeth the revelation of the mysteries and secrets of holy Scripture, which are opened to holy and perfect men, as to stags seeking high things.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. THE LORD MAKETH THE FLOOD TO DWELL: AND THE LORD SHALL SIT KING FOR EVER.**

In the Hebrew there is יהוה למבול ישב ADONAI *lemabboul iashab*, *Dominus diluvium inhabitat* (the Lord inhabiteth the flood), and so also from the Greek Basil and Augustine read: yet Jerome in the epistle *to Sunnia and Fretela* approveth rather our reading, namely, *inhabitare facit* (maketh to dwell), and saith that he so translated. Euthymius readeth in the same way. And certainly the Greek word κατοικίει signifieth both, *to inhabit* and *to settle*, that is, *to make to dwell*; the Hebrew word by a small change can be brought to the same signification. Our reading therefore being kept, according to the former understanding, the cause is given why all things speak glory to God. For the cause is, that "God maketh the flood to dwell," that is, poureth out abundant rain upon the earth, from which food is brought forth for all things, and life is sustained, and the same God himself shall sit King and Lord for ever, namely ruling all things, governing all things, disposing all things. But according to the latter understanding, when the Lord at the last judgment shall have discovered the thick woods, and his justice shall have been praised by all, then he shall make the flood to dwell, pouring out all kinds of evils upon the evil, and so, none at all resisting, every other principality being taken out of the way, and the kingdom of the devils, and of wicked men, "the Lord shall sit King for ever." Some refer this flood to the flood of Noe, others to baptism, and because they are grave authors, and of great name, I dare not disapprove.

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. THE LORD WILL GIVE STRENGTH TO HIS PEOPLE: THE LORD WILL BLESS HIS PEOPLE WITH PEACE.**

This is the conclusion of the Psalm, in which, according to the former understanding, the Prophet, who hath praised the power of God in all the other things of this lower world, praiseth it lastly in men, that is, in his people, to whom he saith there is to be given by God *strength*, that is, might and force against all adversaries, and, all these being overcome, most sweet rest and peace. But according to the latter understanding, he concludeth the Psalm, promising from the Lord *strength*,

<!-- p. 167 (PDF 178) -->

that is, power and might in this pilgrimage for resisting temptations, and *blessing*, and everlasting peace in eternal life. It seemeth that in this place the pious observation of some is not to be passed over, who so fit the voice of the Lord, seven times repeated in this Psalm, to the seven sacraments of the Church, that they will have the voice of the Lord upon the waters to agree with Baptism; the voice of the Lord in power, with Confirmation; the voice of the Lord in magnificence, with the Eucharist; the voice of the Lord breaking the cedars, with Penance; the voice of the Lord shaking the desert, with Order; the voice of the Lord dividing the flame of fire, with Matrimony; the voice of the Lord preparing the stags, with Extreme Unction.

[Margin: The seven sacraments are here marked out by the seven voices.]
