BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XVI.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT

*The prayer of David.*[^1]

This Psalm, as the title itself showeth, is the prayer of a just man unjustly suffering persecution, by which he asketh deliverance of God; and he draweth his arguments, the first from his own innocence, the second from the justice of God, the third from the mercy of the same, the fourth from the malice of the wicked who persecute the just.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. HEAR, O LORD, MY JUSTICE: ATTEND TO MY SUPPLICATION.**

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. GIVE EAR UNTO MY PRAYER, WHICH PROCEEDETH NOT FROM DECEITFUL LIPS.**

These two verses are joined into one among the Hebrews; but among the Greeks and the Latins they are distinct. The matter is of small moment, and doth not change the meaning. First therefore he prayeth that his justice may be heard: for with a just judge the cause crieth louder than the person. Then he addeth, and asketh that regard be had also to his supplication, because God loveth not only justice, but also the just, and, as St. James saith, chap. v, 16: "The prayer of a just man availeth much." Lastly he joineth both, justice and supplication, when he saith: "Give ear unto my prayer, which proceedeth not from deceitful lips," that is, my prayer, which cometh not forth from deceitful lips, but resteth on justice. And so the sense is: Let justice move thee, O Lord, let supplication move thee; let the supplication of a just man move thee.

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. LET MY JUDGMENT COME FORTH FROM THY COUNTENANCE: LET THY EYES BEHOLD THE THINGS THAT ARE EQUITABLE.**

He seeketh a second argument from the justice of God, as if he said: To thee I appeal, O Lord, by thee I would be judged, who art a most just Judge. "From thy countenance," that is, from thy mouth, let my judgment come forth, that is, let sentence be pronounced. "Let thy eyes behold the things that are equitable," that is, do not shut thy eyes, and pass over the calumnies of the wicked, but open thy eyes, and consider what justice requireth. For, as I have noted elsewhere, equity is not put in this place for *epicheia*, but for true justice itself, as the Hebrew and Greek words sound. In the Greek there is: *Oculi mei videant æquitatem* (Let my eyes behold equity), and so St. Augustine readeth, and expoundeth it of the judgment of the just man, not of the judgment of God, so that the sense is: Let my judgment come forth from thy countenance, that is, enlighten my mind, that I may judge justly, "let my eyes behold the things that are equitable," that is, let not my mind go astray in judging. But if the meaning of the Greek copy ought to agree with the Hebrew and Latin reading, this ought to be the sense: Let my eyes behold the things that are equitable, namely those which thou dost exercise, that is, let them behold thy just judgments. And indeed the reading which hath *oculi tui* (thy eyes) cohereth better with what goeth before, and St. Jerome approveth the same in the epistle *to Sunnia and Fretela*.

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. THOU HAST PROVED MY HEART, AND VISITED IT BY NIGHT, THOU HAST TRIED ME BY FIRE: AND INIQUITY HATH NOT BEEN FOUND IN ME.**

He giveth the cause why he would be judged by God, namely, because he alone searcheth hearts, and perfectly knoweth the innocence of his servants. "Thou hast proved," saith he, "my heart," that is, thou hast tried what manner of man I am within, where no one seeth but thou, thou hast made trial of my sincerity. And how he did this he declareth, saying: "Thou hast visited it by night, and thou hast tried me by fire." For there are two times in which it appeareth what is in the heart, the occasion of acting secretly, and the time of tribulation. For many are evil within, good without; if they have an occasion of sinning in secret, where there is no danger of their being discovered, then malice betrayeth itself. So also in the time of prosperity the evil are not discerned from the good; but when the fire of persecution shall have come, then the gold will shine, and the chaff will smoke. Of the first time he saith: "Thou hast visited it by night," that is, when I had occasions of sinning in secret. But of the second: "Thou hast tried me by fire," that is, by burning tribulation. "And iniquity hath not been found in me"; word for word it is read thus: *Non invenisti, cogitavi, non transibit os meum* (Thou hast not found, I have thought, my mouth shall not pass). Which words seem to make no sense. It must be known therefore that the Seventy Interpreters did not read *zammothi*, which signifieth *I have thought*; but *zimmathi*, the points only being changed, that is, *my iniquity*, and they joined this noun

[Margin: Two times in which it appeareth what is in the heart.]

<!-- p. 83 (PDF 94) -->

with the verb above, and therefore they rendered: *Non est inventa in me iniquitas* (Iniquity hath not been found in me), which is the same with this: *Non invenisti iniquitatem meam* (Thou hast not found my iniquity), as it is word for word in the Hebrew. The words following, namely *non transibit os meum* (my mouth shall not pass), the Seventy Interpreters joined with the words that follow, *in opera hominum* (to the works of men), and rendered, *ut non loquatur os meum opera hominum* (that my mouth may not speak the works of men). For it is the same thing: *Non transibit os meum in opera hominum* (My mouth shall not pass over to the works of men), and: *Non loquetur os meum opera hominum* (My mouth shall not speak the works of men).

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. THAT MY MOUTH MAY NOT SPEAK THE WORKS OF MEN: FOR THE SAKE OF THE WORDS OF THY LIPS, I HAVE KEPT HARD WAYS.**

He showeth whence it came that God found no iniquity in him; and he saith that it came from this, that he kept the hard ways of justice for the sake of the precepts of God, not for any human hope. For they who for human hope keep the way of justice do so outwardly, where men see, and thereby there is found in them a hidden iniquity; but they who for the commandment of God do not forsake the way of justice do so outwardly and inwardly, and therefore no iniquity is found in them. He saith therefore: "I have kept hard ways," that is, I have remained in the way of justice, although rough and hard, nor hath any tribulation made me turn aside from that way. And I have remained in this way "for the sake of the words of thy lips," that is, for the sake of thy commandments, or for the sake of thy promises and threats. "That my mouth may not speak the works of men," that is, that I may not be forced to implore human help, nor depend on human hope, nor praise the works of men. In the Hebrew, for that which we read, *vias duras* (hard ways), there is *vias* פריץ, that is, the ways *of the robber*, or *of the waster*: but, the points being changed, that word *parits* can be read *perets*, that is, *a breach*, or *a breaking*; and the sense will be: I have kept ways broken and steep, and thereby dangerous and hard.

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. PERFECT THOU MY GOINGS IN THY PATHS: THAT MY FOOTSTEPS BE NOT MOVED.**

Acknowledging that not by his own strength, but by the help of God, he hath remained in the narrow path of justice, he asketh God to confirm this benefit: "Perfect thou my goings in thy paths," that is, confirm and establish my goings in this thy way, lest perchance I turn aside from it, if I be left destitute of thy help. In the Hebrew it is word for word: *Sustenta gressus meos, ne labantur vestigia mea* (Uphold my goings, that my footsteps slip not); where the very same thing is declared by a similitude drawn from a slippery way. And so *perfect my goings* signifieth, uphold and establish them; and *that my footsteps be not moved* signifieth, that they slip not by reason of the slipperiness of the way. For truly the weakness of the flesh, and the allurement of the world, make the way of human life very slippery, and many slip in it.

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. I HAVE CRIED TO THEE, FOR THOU, O GOD, HAST HEARD ME: O INCLINE THY EAR UNTO ME, AND HEAR MY WORDS.**

Having set forth the argument from his own innocence, and from the justice of God, he again repeateth the prayer proposed in the beginning of the Psalm, which is as it were the conclusion that is gathered from those two arguments. The meaning therefore is this: "I have cried," to thee, O Lord, "for thou hast heard me," that is, I have cried with confidence, because thou hast always heard me at other times. And therefore according to thy wonted kindness now also "incline thy ear unto me, and hear my words."

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. SHEW FORTH THY WONDERFUL MERCIES; THOU WHO SAVEST THEM THAT TRUST IN THEE.**

This is the third argument, which is taken from the mercy of God; as if he said: I have set forth my innocence, I have appealed to thy justice; now I call upon thy mercy, because, although I think myself innocent of the crime for which I suffer tribulation, yet there are perhaps many other sins of mine, for which I could justly be punished. "Shew forth therefore thy wonderful mercies," that is, so manifestly exercise thy mercies in delivering me, that all may wonder. For it is thine to save all who have their hope laid up in thy mercy.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. FROM THEM THAT RESIST THY RIGHT HAND KEEP ME, AS THE APPLE OF THY EYE.**

In the Hebrew those words, *from them that resist thy right hand*, are joined with the verse above, so that the sense is: Thou who savest from them that resist thy right hand those who trust in thee. But they cohere also very well with the words following, in this manner: *Keep me, as the apple of thy eye*, that is, with the greatest diligence; *from them that resist thy right hand*, that is, from wicked men, who resist thy right hand, either by hurting those whom thou protectest, or by not walking whither thou sayest. Nor is this contrary to that which we read in the book of *Esther*, chap. xiii, 9: "There is none that can resist thy will." For that is understood of the will of good pleasure, which is always fulfilled: but this of the will of sign, which is not always fulfilled, because God permitteth the wicked to do many things against the laws laid down by him; but afterwards he punisheth them, as they deserve. Therefore St. Peter exhorteth, saying: "Be you humbled under the mighty hand of God, that he may exalt you in the time of visitation," I *Pet.* v, 6. That similitude, "as the apple of thy eye," is most apt, because the apple of the eye is a thing at once most precious and most weak, and therefore it is wont to be kept most diligently, and is fortified by nature herself, both with divers coats, and also with eyelids, and with a rampart of hairs.

[Margin: The will of good pleasure is always fulfilled, the will of sign not so.]

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. PROTECT ME UNDER THE SHADOW OF THY WINGS. FROM THE FACE OF THE WICKED WHO HAVE AFFLICTED ME.**

He repeateth the very same thing by another similitude. For as chickens are covered by the wings of the hen, and in that manner are overshadowed, and lie hid secure, so that they cannot be hurt by the kites: so the just man desireth to be protected by the divine help from his persecutors. That phrase, *from the face of the wicked*, can signify, from the sight of the wicked:

<!-- p. 84 (PDF 95) -->

for the wings, while they cover the chickens, cause that they be not seen by the kites. Likewise, from the bite of the wicked, or from the anger of the wicked: for the teeth also are in the face, and anger is shown in the face. That phrase, *who have afflicted me*, signifieth that the just man hath already felt the bites of the wicked, and therefore seeketh protection, lest, if he be bitten oftener, he be at last consumed. This similitude is familiar to divine Scripture, *Ps.* lxii, 8: "Under the covert," saith he, "of thy wings I will hope";[^2] and again, *Ps.* xc, 4: "He will overshadow thee with his shoulders: and under his wings thou shalt trust"; and the Lord himself in the Gospel: "How often would I have gathered together thy children, as the hen doth gather her chickens under her wings, and thou wouldest not?" *Matt.* xxiii, 37.

[Margin: Verse 11.]

**11. MY ENEMIES HAVE SURROUNDED MY SOUL: THEY HAVE SHUT UP THEIR FAT: THEIR MOUTH HATH SPOKEN PROUDLY.**

This is the last argument, which is taken from the malice of the enemies: "My enemies," saith he, "have surrounded my soul," that is, they press upon my life, or upon myself, on every side; "they have shut up their fat," that is, they are moved by no mercy, although they see me brought to extremity; "their mouth hath spoken proudly," that is, not only have they no mercy, but they also insult arrogantly. Now to shut up the fat signifieth to shut the bowels, and thereby not to have mercy, according to that word of St. John: "He that hath the substance of this world, and shall see his brother in need, and shall shut up his bowels from him: how doth the charity of God abide in him?" I *John* iii, 17. But because the bowels are wont to be shut when the fat groweth, therefore the Prophet chose rather to say: "They have shut up their fat," than, they have shut their bowels, that he might at the same time point out the cause whence the bowels are shut, which is the multiplying of fat, that is, of temporal wealth. For as natural fat shutteth the bowels of the flesh: so the multiplying of riches maketh men to be proudly minded, and to despise their neighbours, and shutteth the spiritual bowels.

[Margin: To shut the bowels is the same as not to have mercy.]

[Margin: Verse 12.]

**12. THEY HAVE CAST ME FORTH AND NOW THEY HAVE SURROUNDED ME: THEY HAVE SET THEIR EYES BOWING DOWN TO THE EARTH.**

He goeth on to relate the wickedness of the enemies, and saith that they enter now upon one way, now upon the contrary, yet so that they always do hurt. "They have cast me forth and now they have surrounded me," that is, they who before cast me forth from their company, now have surrounded me. In the Greek the sense is clearer, because that word, *projicientes* (casting forth), is of the past time, ἐκβαλόντες, and signifieth *cum projecissent me* (when they had cast me forth). And so the sense is: whereas before they had cast me forth from their company, that I might not enjoy the common goods, now again they seek me, and surround me, to afflict me with evils. Now the cause of this their malice is, that "they have set their eyes bowing down to the earth," that is, they have firmly resolved not to look to God, who is in heaven, nor to admit any fear of God; but to gaze upon the earth alone, and to follow after those goods which are on the earth. In the Hebrew, for that word, *projicientes me* (casting me forth), there is אשורנו *ashurenu*, which some render *gressu meo* (by my step); others, *incendentes in me* (kindling upon me);[^3] others otherwise. But the Seventy Interpreters seem to have read חורישוני *horisuni*, which signifieth *they have driven me out*; for the letters are almost the same, but transposed.

[Margin: Verse 13.]

**13. THEY HAVE TAKEN ME, AS A LION PREPARED FOR THE PREY; AND AS A YOUNG LION DWELLING IN SECRET PLACES.**

Not only, saith he, have they surrounded me, but they have raged against me most cruelly: for "they have taken me," with that greediness and fierceness with which "a lion prepared for the prey" taketh the little sheep that cometh in his way. Now a lion is said to be prepared for the prey, which is hungry, and intent upon the prey. He repeateth the same by the words following: "And as a young lion dwelling in secret places," that is, as a lion's whelp lurking in ambush taketh the prey that cometh in his way. In the Hebrew, for *susceperunt me* (they have taken me), there is דמיונו *dimiono*, *his likeness*, namely of the wicked man, as of a lion desiring the prey. But the Seventy read a little otherwise, as that word can be read, if the points be removed; namely they seem to have read דמוני *demauni*, from the same verb *damah*, which signifieth not only *to be like*, but also *to cut down*. And so they understood that there is here signified ὑπέλαβον, *succiderunt me* (they have cut me down), as a lion is wont to cut down the prey offered to him; but for *succiderunt* (they have cut down) they chose rather to render *susceperunt* (they have taken), namely in order to cut down, because they did not cut him down in very deed, but only in desire: for God did not permit that they should cut him down in very deed, as is plain from the verse following.

[Margin: Verse 14.]

**14. ARISE, O LORD, DISAPPOINT HIM AND SUPPLANT HIM; DELIVER MY SOUL FROM THE WICKED ONE: THY SWORD FROM THE ENEMIES OF THY HAND.**

Having explained the malice of the persecutors, he asketh God to be present, and himself to deliver him, who alone can: "Arise, O Lord," put off thy help no longer; "disappoint him" (*præveni eum*, come before him),[^4] that is, the wicked one, who like a lion hath seized me to devour me; come before his teeth, lest he fasten them in me, and kill me. And truly God alone can come before the action of any cause whatsoever, even of one acting most swiftly, as he came before the teeth of the lions, that they should not hurt Daniel lying among them when they were hungry; and as he came before the violence of the fire, that it should not burn the three children cast into the furnace: whence great consolation ariseth to the just, who know that such is the power of their God, that he is able to keep them secure among the teeth of lions and among the flames of burning fire. "Supplant him," that is, deceive him, bringing it about by thy wonderful providence, that while he thinketh that he fasteneth his teeth in the flesh of the just man, he may fasten them in his own flesh. "Deliver my soul from the wicked one," that is, suffer me not to be killed by the wicked one raging after the manner of a lion, but deliver and save me. "Thy sword from the enemies of thy hand." In the Hebrew the verse endeth after *frameam tuam* (thy sword), and then

[Margin: God alone can come before the action of any cause whatsoever.]

<!-- p. 85 (PDF 96) -->

another verse beginneth; and because the Hebrews do not decline nouns by cases, that sentence can be rendered thus: *Eripe animam meam ab impio, framea tua* (Deliver my soul from the wicked one, thy sword), that is, as St. Jerome rendered it, *ab impio qui est framea tua* (from the wicked one who is thy sword); or, as others will have it, *eripe animam meam ab impio framea tua* (deliver my soul from the wicked one with thy sword), that is, by thy sword; but, following the version of the Seventy and the Vulgate edition, the sense will be: Deliver my soul from the wicked one, and, that thou mayest do this, take away thy sword, that is, the power of afflicting, from the enemies of thy hand, that is, from those who do not use it well, who are the enemies of thy hand, and thereby do not follow the direction of thy hand, but against it abuse the power granted to them by thee: "For all power is from God"; or, as the Apostle speaketh: "There is no power but from God," *Rom.* xiii. In the Hebrew, for *inimicis manus tuæ* (the enemies of thy hand), there is *a viris manus tuæ* (from the men of thy hand), or *a mortuis manus tuæ* (from the dead of thy hand); for the Hebrew word מתים *methim* signifieth both *men* and *the dead*. The Seventy Interpreters understood that the dead are signified, and rendered ἀπ' ἐχθρῶν τῆς χειρός σου, *ab inimicis manus tuæ* (from the enemies of thy hand); because the enemies of the hand of the Lord, that is, they who will not follow the direction of the hand of the Lord, are dead to the hand of the Lord, as all sinners are said to be dead to God, and enemies of God; but they rendered *enemies* rather than *dead*, because that is much clearer.

[Margin: Verse 15.]

**15. O LORD, DIVIDE THEM FROM THE FEW OF THE EARTH IN THEIR LIFE: THEIR BELLY IS FILLED FROM THY HIDDEN STORES.**

It is a prophetic imprecation, that is, a prediction by way of imprecation; for he foretelleth that it shall come to pass, that the wicked shall be divided from the just, and that to the wicked indeed temporal things only shall be given, but to the just eternal things also. "O Lord, divide them from the few of the earth," that is, separate the multitude of the wicked from thy little flock. In the Hebrew there is the same word מתים *methim*, which is at the end of the verse above. For David, as he had called the enemies of God the dead of God, so now calleth the friends of God the dead to the world, or to the earth; for the word חלד *cheled* signifieth *the earth, the world, the age*, and the like. And so it could have been rendered: Divide them from those dead to the world; but the Seventy chose rather to say, from the few of the earth, because they who are truly dead to the world are very few, in respect of the lovers of the world. That phrase, *divide eos* (divide them), is in the Hebrew *pars eorum* (their portion); but, the points being changed, the same words signify *divide them*. That which followeth, *in their life*, could rightly be joined with what followeth, were it not for the particle *et* (and), *et de absconditis tuis* (and from thy hidden stores), etc., which particle is in the Hebrew and in the Greek, and in many Latin copies: nor do I know why it is omitted in the Vulgate edition. The sense therefore is, that not only in the life to come, but also in the present, which is properly their life, namely that which alone they acknowledge and love, they are to be divided from just men, who are dead to the world. And the division consisteth in this, that "their belly is filled from thy hidden stores," that is, with the goods of the earth created and preserved by God, and which flow forth every year as from a hidden treasure, they fill their belly, and say that this is their portion.

[Margin: Verse 16.]

**16. THEY ARE FULL OF CHILDREN: AND THEY HAVE LEFT TO THEIR LITTLE ONES THE REST OF THEIR SUBSTANCE.**

That is, they abound in children, and after death they leave to them their goods which remain over to themselves; for this is accounted by the children of this world perfect felicity, if both they themselves overflow with earthly goods, and leave their children heirs of their goods. From the Greek St. Augustine rendered: *Saturati sunt porcina* (They are filled with swine's flesh); but he saith that it can also be rendered: *Saturati sunt filiis* (They are full of children). The reason of this variety is at hand:[^5] for the Greek word, if it be written with a simple ypsilon, ὑῶν, signifieth *swine*; if it be written with ypsilon and iota, υἱῶν, signifieth *children*; but from the Hebrew it is plain that it is to be read *filiis* (of children), not *porcina* (with swine's flesh).

[Margin: Verse 17.]

**17. BUT AS FOR ME, I WILL APPEAR BEFORE THY SIGHT IN JUSTICE: I SHALL BE SATISFIED WHEN THY GLORY SHALL APPEAR.**

In this, I say, the division consisteth, that they desire to be filled in this life with earthly goods, "but as for me," as also the rest of the just, in this life I will hunger after justice; and in the other I shall be satisfied with glory and beatitude. "I will appear before thy sight in justice," that is, I will study to live justly before thee; "I shall be satisfied when thy glory shall appear," that is, as now my justice shall appear to thee, so afterwards thy glory shall appear to me, and then at last I shall be truly satisfied, desiring nothing more. In the Hebrew, for *apparebit gloria tua* (thy glory shall appear), there is *cum excitabitur similitudo tua* (when thy likeness shall be awakened), and the sense is the same; for then the glory of the Lord shall appear to us, when there shall be awakened in us the likeness of which it is written, I *John* iii, 2: "We shall be like to him: because we shall see him as he is." For when we shall see him as he is, then we shall be like to him in glory; for he also is blessed, because he seeth himself. For this is life, and everlasting glory, to see God as he is.
