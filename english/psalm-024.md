BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XXIV.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*Unto the end, a Psalm for David.*

In the Hebrew there is not *in finem* (unto the end), but only *Psalmus David* (a Psalm for David); but it mattereth not much whether the Seventy added that *in finem*, or it fell out of the Hebrew text. This Psalm is the first of the alphabetical ones; for it hath as many verses as there are letters of the alphabet, namely twenty-two, and the first verse beginneth from the first letter of the Alphabet, the second from the second, and so forth. There is one letter only, from which no verse beginneth, namely *caph*,[^1] but that this loss may be repaired, at the end of the Psalm after the last letter there is set another verse beginning from the letter *phe*; and so two verses begin from the letter *phe*. What was the cause of this thing, it would be hard to divine. Like alphabetical Psalms are xxxiii, xxxv,[^2] cx, cxi, cxviii and cxliv. Why Psalms are written alphabetically, no better reason occurreth, than that the memory may be helped. The argument of the Psalm is a most devout prayer, in which is asked the remission of sins, and the grace of living well. And this prayer befitteth a man beginning to despise temporal things, who is so held by the desire of pleasing God, that yet he laboureth in overcoming concupiscences.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. TO THEE, O LORD, HAVE I LIFTED UP MY SOUL. IN THEE, O MY GOD, I PUT MY TRUST; LET ME NOT BE ASHAMED.**[^3]

In this verse, which is one with the Seventy, because it containeth an entire sentence, there are two according to the Hebrews; for the second beginneth from those words: "In thee I put my trust," because there the second letter of the alphabet is set. "To thee, O Lord, have I lifted up my soul," that is, finding no rest in any creatures, but everywhere thorns and thistles, weary of my former life, I have lifted up to thee my soul, withdrawn from the affection by which it clave to the earth; I have begun to cleave to thee by continual thought, and by love inspired by thee; from thee I look for help against temptations, and because "in thee I put my trust, let me not be ashamed," that is, let me not go back from thee confounded, help not being obtained, so that I be forced to blush before my enemies. In the Greek text there is added εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα, *in æternum* (for ever); but that is not in the Hebrew, nor is it read by St. Jerome, St. Augustine, and Theodoret in their Commentaries, but by Euthymius alone of the ancients. And so it is rightly not received in the Latin edition.

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. NEITHER LET MY ENEMIES LAUGH AT ME: FOR NONE OF THEM THAT WAIT ON THEE SHALL BE CONFOUNDED.**

He explaineth what he had said, *let me not be ashamed*, because then he would be ashamed, if he were laughed at by his enemies as one who had hoped in God in vain. In the Hebrew there is, *neque exultent de me inimici mei* (neither let my enemies rejoice over me). But the sense is the same. For the rejoicing of enemies is a kind of laughing to scorn. Now by enemies may be understood both evil men, and also the devils, whose rejoicing and scorn would breed an intolerable confusion, if we thought seriously upon it. Afterwards he giveth the cause, why he altogether trusteth that he shall not be confounded, "for," saith he, "none of them that wait on thee shall be confounded," that is, because by long experience, by the examples of our forefathers, and from thy very words we have learned that none is confounded, nor falleth from his hope, of those who wait on thee, that is, who trusting in thee patiently look for thy help. To wait on the Lord (*sustinere Dominum*) is most frequent in the Scriptures, and signifieth to look for the Lord with a sure hope of help.

[Margin: What is it to wait on the Lord?]

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. LET ALL THEM BE CONFOUNDED THAT ACT UNJUST THINGS WITHOUT CAUSE.**

This verse may have a twofold sense: for first the sense may be, that it is signified that they are to be confounded, who sin without cause, that is, who sin not out of ignorance, or infirmity, but out of malice. For these think not of penance, nor of casting away sin; therefore, if perchance they hope anything from God, their hope is presumption. So Theodoret and Euthymius. There may also be another sense, perhaps more literal, that it is signified that the enemies of the just, both visible and invisible, are to be confounded, because they act unjustly in vain while they vex just men; for they attain not the end set before them, namely to destroy the just, and bring them to hell fire; since on the contrary that vexation is to them an exercise of virtue, and the merit of everlasting glory. The Prophet therefore turneth back the confusion upon the enemies, as if he said: Suffer not, O Lord, that I be confounded; which would be, if my enemies laughed at me, and rejoiced over my perdition; but on the contrary let them be confounded, while they see that they have acted unjustly without cause (*supervacue*) and in vain, to provoke me to impatience.

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. SHEW, O LORD, THY WAYS TO ME, AND TEACH ME THY PATHS.**

By the ways of the Lord we understand the law of the Lord, which is truly the way, by which we go to God. For "if thou wilt enter into life," saith the Lord, *Matt.* xix, "keep

<!-- p. 140 (PDF 151) -->

the commandments." When therefore the Prophet had asked the help of the Lord against temptations, he explaineth what help he chiefly desireth to have, and saith: "Shew, O Lord, thy ways to me," that is, make me to walk by the ways of thy commandments; "and teach me thy paths," that is, show me the narrow way of thy most just law: for so shall I escape all the scorn of the enemies, and not I shall be confounded, but they shall be confounded, who in vain harass me with their temptations. Now he desireth to be taught the law of the Lord, not as to speculative understanding, but as to practice, that is, he desireth the grace, which may persuade the will, that it may please it to fulfil the commandments.

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. DIRECT ME IN THY TRUTH, AND TEACH ME; FOR THOU ART GOD MY SAVIOUR; AND ON THEE HAVE I WAITED ALL THE DAY LONG.**

He repeateth the same, and addeth a reason. "Direct me," saith he, "in thy truth," that is, of myself I easily decline to the right hand, or to the left, forsaking the way of thy commandments, by reason of temporal prosperities, or adversities, therefore do thou direct me, leading me by the help of thy grace by the right way in thy truth, that is in thy law, which is the truest way. "For all thy statutes are truth," *Ps.* cxviii.[^4] "For thou art God my Saviour," that is, therefore do I ask this help of thee, because thou alone, who art God, canst save my soul. For there is no other physician, who knoweth how to cure the diseases of souls, and much less who can restore perfect health, but God. And moreover this I ask of thee, and hope to obtain, because on thee have I waited all the day long, that is, perseveringly and patiently have I looked for thy medicine, nor would I receive consolation from elsewhere. It is of great merit with God, in the time of temptation not to fall away from the hope of heavenly help, nor to seek after human consolations.

[Margin: God alone is the physician of souls.]

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. REMEMBER, O LORD, THY BOWELS OF COMPASSION; AND THY MERCIES THAT ARE FROM THE BEGINNING OF THE WORLD.**

When God suffereth a soul to be tossed with temptations, or to stick fast in sins, he seemeth to have forgotten his mercy; therefore the just man, having long wrestled with temptation, or seeing that he cannot be delivered from falling into sins, which he most earnestly desireth, crieth to God that he would remember his compassions and his mercies of old. Between *miseratio* (compassion) and *misericordia* (mercy) there is this difference, that *miseratio* signifieth the act of having mercy, *misericordia* the habit of the virtue, and almost the same difference is found in the Hebrew words, although they are more unlike each other than the Latin. Where we have here *miserationes* (compassions), in the Hebrew there is רחמיך, which properly signifieth *love* and *a father's bowels*; whence is that of *Ps.* cii: "As a father hath compassion on his children."[^5] And where we have here *misericordia* (mercy), in the Hebrew there is חסדיך, which signifieth *piety* and *goodness*, and the virtue of *mercy*. And so the sense is: Remember, O Lord, that thou hast been merciful from eternity, and not[^6] only merciful, but also wont to have mercy, and to show a father's bowels toward thy children, and therefore that this is proper and natural to thee. That phrase, *quæ a sæculo sunt* (that are from the beginning of the world), in the Hebrew and Greek is, *quia a sæculo sunt* (for they are from the beginning of the world); and so read St. Augustine and Theodoret. Yet St. Jerome in the Commentary, and Euthymius read, as we have it, *quæ a sæculo sunt*; from which it appeareth that the Greek and Latin texts were of old various. The sense is the same, whether we say: Remember, that thou art merciful from eternity; or remember thy mercy, which is from eternity.

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. THE SINS OF MY YOUTH AND MY IGNORANCES DO NOT REMEMBER.**

He beautifully setteth forgetting against remembering, as if he said: Remember thy mercy, and forget my sins. For the one is the cause of the other: for then doth God remember his mercy, when he will no more remember our sins, but remitteth and blotteth them out, as if they were committed to everlasting forgetfulness. Now he mentioneth the sins of youth and ignorances, that is, offences committed out of infirmity and ignorance, both because mercy befitteth these more than others, according to that of the Apostle, I *Tim.* i: "I obtained mercy, because I did it ignorantly"; and because perhaps David hath no other sins: and this is the prayer of a just man, whose strife is with light sins; and also because, as Euthymius noteth, sins of malice are not remitted by prayer alone, but need fruits worthy of penance. In the Hebrew there is not *ignorantias meas* (my ignorances), but פשעי, that is *scelera mea* (my crimes). But because the Seventy rendered ἄγνοιας, that is *ignorances*, and of their learning and faithfulness we cannot doubt, and in truth ignorance agreeth better with the sins of youth than crime; we are compelled to say that the Hebrew word is general, and signifieth as well crimes as lighter sins.

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. ACCORDING TO THY MERCY REMEMBER THOU ME: FOR THY GOODNESS' SAKE, O LORD.**

He declareth what he had said: Remember thy mercy, and forget my offences. For this is wholly done, when God remembereth a sinful man according to his mercy.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. THE LORD IS SWEET AND RIGHTEOUS: THEREFORE HE WILL GIVE A LAW TO SINNERS IN THE WAY.**

He strengtheneth himself in the hope of obtaining, affirming that the Lord is good and just, and therefore is wont willingly to correct sinners. For so he exerciseth mercy toward the man, and justice toward the sin. For *dulcis* (sweet), in the Hebrew there is טוב *tob*, which properly signifieth *good*; and in the Greek also χρηστὸς signifieth *good* rather than *sweet*. Therefore that word, *dulcis*, which is in our Latin edition, is to be taken for *good*; for *legem dabit* (he will give a law), in the Hebrew there is, יורה *iora*, that is *he will teach*, and in the Greek νομοθετήσει, that is *he will show himself a lawgiver*, or

<!-- p. 141 (PDF 152) -->

*a teacher*. And so the sense is not, that God giveth a law in any manner whatever, but that he giveth a law by teaching it, and thereby causing it to be kept. For these words answer to the words above: "Shew thy ways to me, and teach me thy paths, direct me in thy truth, and teach me." For what he then asked, he now affirmeth that God is wont to grant. This therefore is the sense: "The Lord is sweet and righteous," and therefore he loveth men, and hateth sins; and for that cause he showeth his law, and persuadeth it to sinners in the way of this life, that they also of unjust and evil may be made just and good.

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. HE WILL GUIDE THE MILD IN JUDGMENT: HE WILL TEACH THE MEEK HIS WAYS.**

He declareth what he had said, "he will give a law to sinners," and saith that this is to be understood not of all sinners, but only of the mild and the meek, who resist not the Lord teaching, nay desire to be taught. "He will guide," saith he, "the mild in judgment," that is, he will lead by the right way[^7] of his law (which is also called *judgment* in the Scriptures, as we said in the explanation of Psalm xviii) men humble and meek. Then he declareth this very thing in other words, saying: "He will teach the meek his ways," that is, to the meek he will give the grace of knowing and loving, and thereby of fulfilling the law. In this place it is to be noted, that the proud are not altogether shut out from the grace of God, but that an order is assigned. For the proud are not capable of the perfection, of which this Psalm properly treateth, unless first, having conceived fear, they do penance, and, fear being laid aside, be made meek and humble. And so first the grace of God softeneth and breaketh the proud and obstinate, then to the contrite and humbled he showeth his ways, and guideth them in judgment.

[Margin: The proud are not altogether to be shut out from the grace of God.]

[Margin: Verse 11.]

**11. ALL THE WAYS OF THE LORD ARE MERCY AND TRUTH, TO THEM THAT SEEK AFTER HIS COVENANT AND HIS TESTIMONIES.**

He addeth to that which he had said, that not only are the mild guided by God, but all the works of God toward such men are mercy and truth, that is, mercy and justice, so far as justice is taken for faithfulness and truth, which maketh one to fulfil what is promised. *The ways of the Lord* in this place are called the works of God, by which God in a certain manner cometh to us; unless perhaps it please better to understand by the ways of the Lord the custom of God or the rule, and as it were the law, which God useth. And so the way of the Lord always signifieth a law, but sometimes the law which he giveth to us, that by it, as by a right way, we may ascend to him; sometimes the law which he himself useth, when by his works he descendeth to us. And because David had already said many things of the former way of the Lord, that is, of the law given to us, he now addeth something of the latter way of the Lord, that is, of the law which he hath set to himself, and which he keepeth toward us. Therefore, saith he, "all the ways of the Lord are mercy and truth," that is, the law, or rule, or manner of proceeding of God consisteth in mercy and truth, so that he mercifully promiseth, and faithfully fulfilleth what he hath promised. But with whom doth God use this law? with "them that seek after his covenant and his testimonies." He calleth the *testament*[^8] of God that covenant, which he made with men,[^9] when he gave the law, that they should be his people, and he their God. Which covenant indeed is also called in the Scriptures a testament, because it containeth the promise of an inheritance, and because it was to be confirmed by the death of the testator, as in very deed it was confirmed in the death of Christ, in sign of which thing Moses sprinkled the blood upon the people, saying: "This is the blood of the testament, which God hath enjoined unto you," *Exod.* xxiv, and *Heb.* ix.[^10] And he calleth the testimonies of God the law itself, which God gave, because, as we have said elsewhere, by the law God testifieth to us his will. With those therefore God useth the law of mercy and truth, who seek after the covenant entered into with God, that they may observe it, and likewise seek after the law of God, that they may fulfil it, that is, with men of good will that fear and love God. In the Hebrew there is for the word *testamentum*, ברית *berith*, which properly signifieth *a covenant*. But the Seventy are wont to render διαθήκην, that is *a testament*, for the reason already given. So also for the word *requirentibus* (to them that seek after), in the Hebrew there is נצרי *notsere*, *custodientibus* (to them that keep); but the sense is the same. For they who seek after the covenant, which perhaps they had already forgotten, seek after it to this end, that they may keep it.

[Margin: What the way of the Lord signifieth.]

[Margin: Verse 12.]

**12. FOR THY NAME'S SAKE, O LORD, THOU WILT PARDON MY SIN: FOR IT IS GREAT.**

From the universal law, which God useth toward them that fear him, the Prophet gathereth that he may have good hope of the remission of his sins. "For thy name's sake, O Lord," that is, that thy mercy and truth may be made manifest, "thou wilt pardon my sin, which is great." Now it may be called great (*multum*, much), either by reason of multitude, as a people is called *multus* (many), according to that of the apostle James, chap. iii: "In many things we all offend"; or by reason of greatness and grievousness, because holy men esteem even small sins great; and truly they are great, if the greatness of him who is offended be considered.

[Margin: Verse 13.]

**13. WHO IS THE MAN THAT FEARETH THE LORD? HE HATH APPOINTED HIM A LAW IN THE WAY HE HATH CHOSEN.**

The Prophet doth after the manner of lovers, who now sigh for the thing loved, now praise it, now sigh again. The just man loved the grace of God, he desired the remission of sins, the gift of living rightly, and of pleasing God: therefore now he asketh this very thing, now he praiseth that grace, and recounteth the happiness of them that fear God, that is, of those who have obtained that grace, now he returneth to desiring and asking this very thing. And so in this verse and the two following he telleth the good things, which they that fear God attain. "Who is," saith he,

<!-- p. 142 (PDF 153) -->

"the man that feareth God?" as if he said: Let the man truly fearing God come forth, and he shall hear from me his happiness. What followeth, "he hath appointed him a law in the way he hath chosen," many will have to be the beginning of the happiness, which the Prophet hath undertaken to recount, as if he said: This man that feareth God shall have this first of all, that God will instruct him concerning his law in the way he hath chosen, that is, in the state of life which he hath chosen. So Theodoret and Euthymius. I reject not the exposition; but I think another more literal, which Jerome and Augustine intimate. For it is the custom of the Prophet to repeat the very same thing twice in the same verse, or to declare in one part of the verse what was said in the other. This therefore I judge to be the sense: "Who is the man that feareth God?"[^11] Who, I say, is he whom God hath instructed concerning his law[^12] in the way, which the man himself hath chosen, that is, in the right road of living well and of going to God, which he himself hath now willingly chosen; for so one part of the verse declareth the other. For he is the man fearing God, who by the gift of God hath chosen the way, by which we go to God, which is the keeping of the commandments. Those words, *legem statuit ei* (he hath appointed him a law), in the Hebrew and Greek are one word only, which signifieth *to teach, to instruct, to command, to show oneself a teacher*, and the like. And so it might have been rendered: *Quis est ille, quem Deus docuit* (Who is he, whom God hath taught); therefore *legem statuit ei* signifieth: He hath given a law in his heart, that is, he hath instructed and taught him. And the pronoun *ei* (him) is to be explained (as St. Jerome did) by the relative *quem* (whom), or *cui* (to whom), that is, whom he hath taught concerning the law, or to whom he hath given a law in the heart. For this is the right interpretation of the Hebrew word.

[Margin: Verse 14.]

**14. HIS SOUL SHALL DWELL IN GOOD THINGS: AND HIS SEED SHALL INHERIT THE LAND.**

This is the happiness of him that feareth God, that "his soul," that is, he himself that feareth God, "shall dwell in good things," that is, shall enjoy[^13] good things not for a short time, or in passing, but abidingly and stably. Which sentence is most true, for "to them that love God, all things work together unto good," as the Apostle saith, *Rom.*[^14] Therefore he that feareth God shall always enjoy good things, because if things go well, he shall enjoy the good of prosperity; if otherwise, he shall enjoy the good of patience, and the hope of a great reward in the kingdom of heaven. So he shall always be glad. Nor shall he alone dwell in good things, but his children also for his sake shall inherit the land, that is, shall possess the land. For in the Scriptures, as we expounded on *Ps.* xv, *to inherit* and *to possess* are taken for the same thing. Now the children of them that fear God are said to be about to possess the land, because they shall dwell peaceably in the land, and no man shall hurt them, in that sense which we have touched; because to the good all things work together unto good, and tribulations themselves are turned into joy.

[Margin: Verse 15.]

**15. THE LORD IS A FIRMAMENT TO THEM THAT FEAR HIM: AND HIS COVENANT SHALL BE MADE MANIFEST TO THEM.**

This is the reason, why they that fear God always dwell in good things, because they lean not upon things frail and perishing, but God himself is their firmament, that is, they have their hopes founded in the friendship and help of God. In the Hebrew there is סוד *sod*, which signifieth *a secret*, and therefore St. Jerome rendereth, *secretum Domini timentibus eum* (the secret of the Lord to them that fear him), supply, *is revealed*. But the Seventy seem to have read יסוד *iesod*, *a foundation*, one little letter being added, which could with little trouble fall out of the Hebrew text in the course of time. And without doubt a foundation, or firmament, agreeth better in this place than a secret. Now why they that fear God rightly lean upon God, as upon a most solid foundation, the reason is, because the *testament*, that is the covenant of the Lord, maketh this manifest to them: for because they that fear God know very well, and often consider that God hath made a league with men, that he will be their God, and as it were a most loving father, if they shall keep his laws, therefore from this covenant they understand that they can rest in God, as in a most firm foundation. This is the meaning of this place, but because the words of the Latin edition are obscure, they will have to be cleared from the Greek words. That word *testamentum* is of the nominative[^15] case in the Greek, namely διαθήκη, and so also it ought to be understood in the Hebrew and the Latin. That phrase, *ut manifestetur eis* (that it may be made manifest to them), is in the Greek δηλῶσαι αὐτοῖς, *manifestabit eis* (shall make manifest to them), that is, the covenant of the Lord shall make manifest to them that fear God, that the Lord is their firmament, that is, from that covenant they shall know that the Lord is the firmament of them that fear him. To the same sense may be expounded the Hebrew words ובריתו להודיעם, *et pactum ejus ad docendum eos* (and his covenant to teach them); for so it is word for word, and the sense is: And the covenant of the Lord shall be to teach them, that is, shall teach them, that the Lord is the firmament of them that fear him. To the same sense therefore we must also fit the Latin words: *Et testamentum ipsius ut manifestetur illis* (And his testament, that it may be made manifest to them), that is, his testament, or covenant, supply *shall cause*, that there be made manifest to them that which was said a little before, namely that God is the firmament of them that fear him.

[Margin: Verse 16.]

**16. MY EYES ARE EVER TOWARDS THE LORD: FOR HE SHALL PLUCK MY FEET OUT OF THE SNARE.**

Having praised for a little while the happiness of them that fear the Lord, he returneth to wishing and asking for it: "My eyes are ever towards the Lord," that is, I continually look upon God with the eyes of the mind, as wholly hanging upon his help. It is a most effectual kind of praying, to look in pitiable guise upon him, from whom thou hopest for help, and to implore the bounty of the rich man rather by showing thy want, and by modest silence, than by hateful clamour. "As the eyes of the handmaid," saith the same Prophet in *Ps.* cxxxii,[^16] "are on the hands of her mistress: so are our eyes unto the Lord our God, until he have mercy on us." "For he shall pluck my feet out of the snare," that is, therefore do I look so intently upon God, because he at length, as I hope, will deliver me from every danger of temptations, which as snares everywhere lie in wait for us in this life. That phrase also, *my eyes are ever*

<!-- p. 143 (PDF 154) -->

*towards the Lord*, might be referred to the intention of always pleasing God, and of in no wise admitting anything against his will. It might also be referred to the contemplation of the divine beauty, which they have always before their eyes, who earnestly love God. But the first explanation seemeth the most literal.

[Margin: Verse 17.]

**17. LOOK THOU UPON ME, AND HAVE MERCY ON ME; FOR I AM ALONE AND POOR.**

Because he himself always looketh upon God, he rightly asketh to be looked upon by God. For this is what he silently asked, when he had his eyes fixed upon God, namely that his solitude and poverty might be mercifully looked upon. He calleth himself *unicus* (alone), that is solitary and desolate, either because he had in truth been forsaken by friends and kinsmen; or, which I rather approve, because he had in mind separated himself from all others, and had placed all his hope in God alone. And he calleth himself *poor*, because he was humble, and esteemed himself empty of virtues and merits.

[Margin: Verse 18.]

**18. THE TROUBLES OF MY HEART ARE MULTIPLIED: DELIVER ME FROM MY NECESSITIES.**

I understand this place of the temptations to sins rather than of temporal adversities. For he also was one of those, who groan with the Apostle and say, *Rom.* vii: "I feel[^17] another law in my members, fighting against the law of my mind," etc.; and: "Wretched man that I am, who shall deliver me from the body of this death?" Now the necessities, from which he desireth to be delivered, seem to be those most troublesome concupiscences, which, will we nill we, vex us, and sometimes draw us into sin. "Are multiplied": in the Hebrew we have הרחיבו *hirchibu*, *dilatatæ sunt* (are enlarged). But because the Hebrew word צרות properly signifieth *straits*, for which we have *tribulationes* (troubles), and *straits*, when they are enlarged, cease to be straits, therefore to take away the ambiguity, the Seventy prudently rendered, not as the words sound, *angustiæ dilatatæ sunt* (the straits are enlarged), but as the sense was, *tribulationes multiplicatæ sunt* (the troubles are multiplied). For in this place the straits are said to be enlarged, not because they have perished, but because they have grown. For what is enlarged, surely groweth. It might also be that the Seventy did not read הרחיבו *hirchibu*, *dilatatæ sunt*, but הרביבו, *multiplicatæ sunt*, a single letter being changed. St. Jerome also rendereth in the same way, *tribulationes multiplicatæ sunt*; therefore if the Hebrew reading be corrupted, it was corrupted after the times of St. Jerome.

[Margin: Verse 19.]

**19. SEE MY ABJECTION AND MY LABOUR; AND FORGIVE ME ALL MY SINS.**

He goeth on in his prayer, and asketh that the sins be remitted him, into which he sometimes slippeth when temptation overcometh. For although he that feareth God is afflicted, and laboureth much in resisting concupiscences, yet the just man falleth seven times in the day; but hence also he is proved to be just, that the stains, which he contracteth by walking less warily, he carefully[^18] washeth away by weeping and praying. By *humility* (*humilitatem*)[^19] in this place he understandeth not the virtue of humility, but abjection and vileness: for the just man groweth exceeding vile to himself, when he desireth to be without spot, and yet escapeth not stains. For the word *dimitte* (forgive), in the Hebrew there is נשא, which St. Jerome rightly rendereth, *porta* (bear): *Porta*, saith he, *omnia peccata mea* (Bear all my sins), and although the sense is the same, yet that word, *porta*, hath I know not what force; for it signifieth a certain fear, or solicitude of a man loving God, lest perchance God, offended by the multitude of sins, be provoked to wrath: therefore he crieth, *bear all my sins*, as if he said: Although they be many, yet be not thou wearied with pardoning, bear with my weakness.

[Margin: Verse 20.]

**20. CONSIDER MY ENEMIES FOR THEY ARE MULTIPLIED, AND HAVE HATED ME WITH AN UNJUST HATRED.**

He draweth an argument from another place, namely from the multitude and cruelty of the enemies, as if he said: Thou hast seen, O Lord, my abjection and my labour, see also the multitude of my spiritual enemies, and their cruelty, and iniquity. The enemies who try to draw us into sins, and cease not to kindle concupiscence with fiery darts, are the devils, whom the Apostle, *Ephes.* iv,[^20] calleth *the spirits of wickedness*: that these are innumerable is known, nor is it less known that they burn against us with a hatred of iniquity, that is, with a most unjust hatred. Hatred is called unjust or most unjust, when one hateth another without cause, from whom, namely, he hath received no injury. Hatred may also be called unjust, or most unjust, when one seeketh to hurt another out of the mere desire of hurting, not that he himself may gain anything thereby. Such altogether is the hatred of the devil toward mankind, and chiefly toward the elect; for neither have men done the devil any injury, but he, blinded with envy from the beginning, destroyed man. "By the envy of the devil," saith the Wise Man, chap. ii, "death came into the world."[^21] And now he harasseth the faithful with temptations, not that he himself may carry off any profit, but because he delighteth in the perdition of the just.

[Margin: Verse 21.]

**21. KEEP THOU MY SOUL, AND DELIVER ME: I SHALL NOT BE ASHAMED, FOR I HAVE HOPED IN THEE.**

Since I am surrounded by so many enemies, and especially invisible ones, I am not sufficient by my own strength to resist them; therefore I run back to thee, that thou mayest keep my soul, and by keeping mayest free it, and deliver it out of their hands. For not only are they said to be freed and delivered out of the hands of enemies, who have already been taken, but also they who are fortified, lest they be taken; and who without doubt would be taken, unless they were helped. "Thou hast delivered me," saith the same Prophet, *Ps.* xciii,[^22] "out of the lower hell," that is, thou hast caused that I should not fall into the lower hell. The sense may also be: Keep my soul in this prison of the body, in which I am held captive: for the law of the members leadeth me captive in the law of sin, *Rom.* vii; and afterwards, in its own time, deliver me.

[Margin: Verse 22.]

**22. THE INNOCENT AND THE UPRIGHT HAVE ADHERED TO ME: BECAUSE I HAVE WAITED ON THEE.**

Because he had said in the preceding verse: "I shall not be ashamed, for I have hoped in thee," he addeth the reason why he feareth to be ashamed, if he be forsaken by God. And the reason is this, that many "innocent and upright," moved by his example, adhered to him, chiefly for this, that they saw him hope in God alone. Who surely would force him to be ashamed and confounded, if he were disappointed of his hope. Wherefore that phrase, *I shall not be ashamed*, is taken one way in the beginning of the Psalm, another way in this place. For in the beginning of the Psalm the sense was: Let me not be ashamed before enemies that laugh at me: in this place the sense is: I shall not be ashamed before friends that love and grieve. In the Hebrew there is word for word, *innocentia et rectitudo conservabunt me* (innocence and uprightness shall preserve me); but it is not unusual, that the abstract be taken for the concrete, and the future for the past, that is, *innocence and uprightness*, for *the innocent and the upright*; and *shall preserve*, for *have preserved*. But for the Hebrew word יצרוני *itseruni*, which signifieth *shall preserve me*, or *have preserved me*, the Seventy seem to have read *itsroruni*, a single point being changed, which word is derived from צרר *zarar*, and signifieth *shall bind*, or *have bound themselves to me*, which is the same with that which the Seventy rendered, *adhæserunt mihi* (have adhered to me). Rightly therefore standeth the Greek and Latin interpretation, but the Rabbins did not rightly read the Hebrew word, and therefore did not rightly set all the points beneath.

[Margin: Verse 23.]

**23. DELIVER ISRAEL, O GOD, FROM ALL HIS TRIBULATIONS.**

Because David was not only one of the people of God, but was also the prince and head of the rest, therefore after he hath prayed long enough for himself, he addeth a prayer for his whole people, and a general prayer, because he could not easily descend to the necessities and straits of single persons. "Deliver," saith he, "Israel, O God, from all his tribulations." Where that word, *Israel*, is in the accusative case, as is known from the articles added in the Hebrew and Greek copies. The sense therefore is: It is not enough for me, if I alone be delivered from my straits, but I ask also that thou deliver Israel, namely the Israelitish people, from every kind of tribulations.
