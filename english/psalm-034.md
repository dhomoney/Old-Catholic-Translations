BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XXXIV.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*For David himself.*

The title hath nothing new. The argument of the Psalm seemeth to be a prayer of Christ to the Father against his persecutors, and those of his body, which is the Church. Perhaps also, as Theodoret, Euthymius and others will have it, David himself prayeth for his own deliverance, and against king Saul and his other persecutors. But because he was a Prophet, and for the most part spoke of Christ in the Psalms, as is gathered from the words of the Lord, *Luke,* last chap.; therefore with St. Augustine and St. Jerome we judge that the principal sense pertaineth to Christ.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. JUDGE THOU, O LORD, THEM THAT WRONG ME: OVERTHROW THEM THAT FIGHT AGAINST ME.**[^1]

It is a general proposition, in which help is asked against persecutors. But the meaning must be explained from the Hebrew text. For the Hebrew hath, to the word, *judica, Domine, judicantes me* (judge, O Lord, them that judge me); or *litiga cum litigantibus mecum* (go to law with them that go to law with me). For the verb and the participle, or verbal noun, are formed from the same word; and although the Hebrew word ריבה *ribah* is ambiguous, and may be referred both to judgment and to a suit, yet the Greek and Latin edition have declared that it is to be referred to judgment; and so the sense will be: *Judge them that judge me,* that is, by a just judgment condemn them who by an unjust judgment have condemned me, such as were the princes of the Hebrews, Annas and Caiphas, and the heathen princes, Pilate and Herod, who judged Christ unjustly, and the many princes who condemned the holy martyrs by a most iniquitous judgment. And that which we read, "judge them that wrong me," hath the same sense: for he who judgeth unjustly without doubt doth wrong. And because the persecutors of Christ and of the Church wished to seem to adjudge Christ and his servants to death as it were according to the laws and out of zeal for justice, whereas in very deed they were not judges, but enemies, and with a false piety waged war for their superstitions; therefore the Psalm subjoineth: "And overthrow them that fight against me;" or, as is had more clearly in the Hebrew and Greek text, where there is a like allusion of verb and participle, *pugna contra pugnantes mecum* (fight against them that fight with me), that is, take up my cause and my war, that, thou overthrowing my enemies, I may escape their hands and carry off the victory.

[Margin: The princes who condemned Christ.]

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. TAKE HOLD OF ARMS AND SHIELD: AND RISE UP TO HELP ME.**

He declareth that word, *overthrow them that fight against me;* and because he who fighteth ought to be furnished with arms, both those with which he may ward off the blows of the adversary, and those with which he may strike the adversary; therefore in this verse he maketh mention of arms of the former kind, and in the following of the latter. For instead of that which we read: *Apprehende arma et scutum* (Take hold of arms and shield), the Hebrew hath to the word, *apprehende clypeum et scutum* (take hold of buckler and shield); but the Greek and Latin interpreters translated the first word *arma* (arms), lest they should set down the same thing twice, but by *arms* they understood defending and protecting arms: for that, by the consent of all, is signified by מגן *maghen,* from the verb גנן *ghanan,* which is *protegere* (to protect). Now the buckler and shield of God is his good will, according to that of *Ps.* v: "O Lord, thou hast crowned us, as with a shield of thy good will." Likewise equity or justice, *Wis.* v: "He will take equity for an invincible shield." And indeed the benevolence of God is the buckler with which God protecteth us. For he is plainly secure whom God loveth, and of him it may be said: "Thou hast crowned him as with a shield of thy good will." The justice of God, which in the Scriptures is wont to be called equity, is the shield with which God himself is protected from the judgments and calumnies of wicked men: for howsoever severely and terribly God punisheth the wicked, he always punisheth most justly; and therefore God feareth not the darts of tongues, or of judgments, according to that: "That thou mayst be justified in thy words, and mayst overcome when thou art judged," *Ps.* l. And of this it is said: "He will take equity for an invincible shield," namely when God shall come to the last judgment, and shall be armed for the revenge of his enemies. Wherefore not without cause is the word buckler, or shield, set twice in this place, since God is armed with both together, when he both mercifully protecteth us, and justly defendeth himself.

[Margin: The buckler and shield of God is his good will.]

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. BRING OUT THE SWORD, AND SHUT UP THE WAY AGAINST THEM THAT PERSECUTE ME: SAY TO MY SOUL: I AM THY SALVATION.**

Here now he speaketh of arms fit for hurting, and by *framea* he understandeth *gladium* (a sword), as St. Jerome translateth, and the sense is: Unsheathe the sword, and grip it fast against my persecutors. For that word, *effunde frameam* (pour out the sword), signifieth an easy and ready unsheathing of the sword, such as there is when

<!-- p. 213 (PDF 224) -->

the sword is polished and smooth: for then it is taken out with no labour, and is as it were poured from the scabbard; but on the contrary a sword eaten up with rust can scarcely be bared with great labour. And that word, *et conclude* (and close), is understood of the closing upon the sword, which ought to be gripped hard by the hand, that it may be held firmly, and strike the more strongly. For the Hebrew verb סגור *segor* signifieth *claudere* (to close) and *stringere* (to grip). But because *framea,* and the Hebrew word חנית *chanith,* and the Greek ῥομφαία, doth not properly signify *gladium* (a sword), but *spiculum* (a dart), or *lanceam* (a spear), therefore this place may also be thus expounded: "Bring out the sword," that is, cast the dart, or spear, at the enemies, "and shut up the way against them that persecute me," that is, close the way, set an obstacle, that the enemies may not approach. Now the sword and spear of God is his anger, that is, the vindictive justice with which he punisheth the wicked. For so we read *Deut.* xxxii: "If I shall whet my sword as the lightning, and my hand take hold on judgment, I will render vengeance to my enemies;" and *Wis.* v: "He will sharpen his severe wrath for a spear." For both sword and spear are arms of offence. A wonderful spectacle indeed is set before the faithful soul, when she thinketh that God standeth for her armed with shield and sword, or spear, and heareth him saying in her heart: "I am thy salvation." For although that which the Apostle saith, *Rom.* viii, 31, is enough for all security: "If God be for us, who is against us?" yet the Holy Ghost, that he might yet more powerfully uphold our weakness, describeth God not in any manner whatsoever, but armed with every kind of arms, fighting for the Church, and for each of his faithful, against enemies visible and invisible. "Say to my soul: I am thy salvation." It is not enough for tranquillity of mind that God fight for us, but it is necessary that this become known to us; therefore Christ asketh for his body that this may be revealed, and that, the Holy Ghost speaking to the heart, confidence may arise. And although God may more properly be called a physician than *salus* (salvation, which is also health), yet most rightly is he called health, because physicians and medicines do not always heal, nor inwardly penetrate the substance of the thing which they desire to heal: but God healeth always, and inwardly entereth into the soul; and as it cannot be that, health being present, a man be not whole, even though all other things be absent: so it cannot be that, God willing to heal, and being present by his grace, the soul be not whole, even though all other things be wanting.

[Margin: The sword and spear of the Lord is his anger.]

[Margin: God may better be called a physician than health.]

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. LET THEM BE CONFOUNDED AND ASHAMED THAT SEEK AFTER MY SOUL.**

He describeth the end of the wicked, against whom God taketh arms, and first he saith: "Let them be confounded and ashamed," that is, let them blush for the victory lost who seek to kill me; for those two words, *confundantur* (let them be confounded) and *revereantur* (let them be ashamed), signify the same. For here it is not a matter of reverence toward another, but of the shame with which he is wont to be covered who loseth his cause. That, *who seek after my soul,* is ambiguous. For sometimes it is taken in a good part, as in that place of *Ps.* cxviii:[^2] "Flight hath failed me: and there is no one that hath regard to (*requirat*) my soul," that is, I have no refuge, neither is there any who would know me, and seek me out, to defend me. Sometimes it is taken in an evil part, as in this place, and oftener elsewhere, where to seek the soul is to endeavour to take away the soul, that is, the life.

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. LET THEM BE TURNED BACK AND BE CONFOUNDED THAT DEVISE EVIL AGAINST ME.**[^3]

Not only therefore let them be confounded, and covered with shame, but also "let them be turned back," that is, let them withdraw vanquished and confounded, "who devise evil against me," that is, who meditate my death.

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. LET THEM BECOME AS DUST BEFORE THE WIND: AND LET THE ANGEL OF THE LORD STRAITEN THEM.**

He addeth thirdly, that they may not only blush, nor only withdraw, but be forced to flee swiftly, and be scattered into divers places. For dust is scattered by the wind into divers parts with great violence, and swiftly. And in this place the swiftness of the flight, and the scattering, is heightened both on the part of the dust and on the part of the wind. For instead of dust the Hebrew hath מוץ *mots,* which signifieth *glumam* (husk), as we said in the explanation of the first Psalm, that is, the lightest dust, made up of the smallest and driest chaff. And instead of a bodily wind, here is set *the Angel of the Lord,* who is a living Spirit, and far more powerful and effectual than any wind. Let them therefore become as the lightest dust, which may easily be caught away by any wind, and yet let not a light wind, but *the Angel of the Lord* be *straitening* and driving *them.*

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. LET THEIR WAY BECOME DARK AND SLIPPERY; AND LET THE ANGEL OF THE LORD PURSUE THEM.**

He addeth last, that they may not only be scattered, and forced to flee, but also be cast headlong into ruin without any remedy: for they that flee are wont to be helped by knowledge of the ways, by a safe and firm path, or at least, if the way be slippery, by slowness of walking. But here all these remedies fail; for the Prophet imprecateth upon the wicked that they be forced to flee in darkness, and by a slippery way, so that neither eyes nor feet can do their office, and with the Angel of the Lord pressing after them, and vehemently urging, so that they must needs rush into the precipice and into ruin. All these things are fulfilled at this time in the Jews, and the other persecutors of Christ and of the Church, who by the just judgment of God dwell in the darkness of ignorance, and in the slipperiness of concupiscence; and, the anger of God pursuing them, they daily slide into greater sins, and hasten with the utmost speed to everlasting death. But they shall be more manifestly fulfilled in the day of judgment: for then the wicked shall be confounded and put to shame with unspeakable confusion, and shall be turned back into the precipice of hell fire, forced by the divine power, and that without any remedy, even as he rusheth and falleth into ruin without remedy, who is compelled to hasten in darkness through a slippery place, another pursuing him.

[Margin: The fall and destruction of the enemies of Christ and of the Church.]

<!-- p. 214 (PDF 225) -->

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. FOR WITHOUT CAUSE THEY HAVE HIDDEN THEIR NET FOR ME UNTO DESTRUCTION: WITHOUT CAUSE THEY HAVE UPBRAIDED MY SOUL.**[^4]

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. LET THE SNARE WHICH HE KNOWETH NOT COME UPON HIM: AND LET THE NET WHICH HE HATH HIDDEN CATCH HIM: AND INTO THAT VERY SNARE LET THEM FALL.**

In the first seven verses the Prophet hath spoken in the person of Christ and of all the just concerning persecutions in general: in the following verses he explaineth three kinds of persecutions which the just are wont to suffer from the wicked. For first they vex the just with frauds and ambushes, then they assail the same with false testimonies, lastly by open force, not with words only, but also with deeds, they oppress the just. He saith therefore of the first kind: "For without cause they have hidden their net for me unto destruction," that is, since indeed, provoked by no injury from me, they cease not to lay ambushes for me; therefore I pray God that by his providence he would bring it to pass that those ambushes be turned to their own undoing. Which imprecation, as we have elsewhere noted, is not so much an imprecation as a prophecy. For truly the providence of God bringeth this about, that ambushes either hurt only them that lie in wait, or hurt them first, and more than others: as a torch kindled to burn a house beginneth to burn and consume itself before the house, and often doth the house no hurt: so the malice of them that lie in wait beginneth at once to lay waste the mind of him that lieth in wait; but others it hurteth either not at all, or far less, because the evil of punishment is less than the evil of fault. As to the words, that: *Absconderunt mihi interitum laquei sui* (they have hidden for me the destruction of their snare), St. Augustine readeth: *Absconderunt mihi laqueum corruptionis suæ* (they have hidden for me the snare of their corruption); but the Hebrew and Greek copies agree with our edition. And the sense is the same. For *interitus laquei* (the destruction of the snare) is said of the death which is prepared by the instrument of a snare, and the snare of destruction, or of corruption, is said of the snare which is prepared, or laid, to catch and to kill. The Hebrew hath רשתם, *rete* (a net), not *laqueus* (a snare); but it mattereth little: for each is an instrument fit for catching. That word, *supervacue* (without cause), is in the Hebrew חנם *chinnam,* that is *gratis* (without cause), as a little before; but the Interpreter chose to vary. And so the sense is: *supervacue,* that is *sine causa* (for no cause), since they have received no injury from me, they have upbraided my soul, that is, they have put me to reproach, laying snares and nets for me as for a wild beast of the woods. The Hebrew now hath חפרו *chapheru,* that is *foderunt* (they have digged); but the Seventy seem to have read חרפו, one letter being transposed; for that signifieth *exprobraverunt* (they have upbraided). Although if we read, *foderunt animæ meæ* (they have digged for my soul), the sense will be the same, namely, without cause they have digged a hole of deceit for my life, and thereby have put me to shame. And that, *veniat illi laqueus quem ignorat, et captio quam abscondit, apprehendat eum, et in laqueum cadat in ipsum* (let the snare which he knoweth not come upon him, and let the catching which he hath hidden take him, and into the snare let him fall, into it), is much clearer in the Hebrew, namely thus: *Veniat illi* (let there come upon him), to wit upon him that lieth in wait, *calamitas quam ignorat* (a calamity which he knoweth not), that is, a calamity unhoped and unlooked for. And he declareth what that calamity is, adding, *rete suum* (his own net), or *laqueus suus quem abscondit mihi, capiat ipsum* (let his own snare, which he hath hidden for me, take him), and so *incidat* (let him fall) into that very calamity which he had prepared for me. Yet the meaning is the same, save that the Seventy in both places chose to set for the calamity the instrument of the calamity, namely the snare, and for the net or snare they set *captionem* (the catching), which is the effect of the snare. That repetition, *in ipsum* (into it), is a Hebraism, for that which we Latins say, *in ipsum eumdem* laqueum cadat (let him fall into that very same snare).

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. BUT MY SOUL SHALL REJOICE IN THE LORD; AND SHALL BE DELIGHTED IN HIS SALVATION.**

[Margin: Verse 11.]

**11. ALL MY BONES SHALL SAY: LORD, WHO IS LIKE TO THEE?**

[Margin: Verse 12.]

**12. WHO DELIVEREST THE POOR FROM THE HAND OF THEM THAT ARE STRONGER THAN HE; THE NEEDY AND THE POOR FROM THEM THAT STRIP HIM.**

In these three verses the Prophet explaineth the unspeakable gladness with which the just man shall be filled, when he shall see himself perfectly free from all that lie in wait. And they are poetical, metaphorical, admirable phrases. And this is the meaning: when that shall have come to pass which I have prayed for, "my soul," by which I live and understand, for gladness "shall rejoice in the Lord" God, namely praising and magnifying him; and withal "shall be delighted in its own salvation," which it shall see itself to have obtained, or rather in the Lord himself who is its salvation; not the soul only, but also the body, yea all the parts of the body, even the inmost and most abject, such as are the bones, which have sense:[^5] nor only *the bones,* but *all the bones,* even the least, shall leap for delight, and if they had sense and voice, would surely cry out, and say: "Lord, who is like to thee?" for there is nothing on earth nor in heaven stronger, kinder, wiser, more lovable than thou: who so mightily and mercifully deliverest the poor man from the hand of an enemy much stronger than himself, who desired violently to seize his goods, and his very life. St. Augustine, after his manner following the allegorical sense, by *the bones* understandeth the perfect men who are in the Church, and by the flesh weak men, so that the sense is: Although in the time of temptation, or of persecution, the flesh of the mystical body of the Church give way, yet the bones will not give way, but will resist, and say: "Lord, who is like to thee?" As to the words, that, *salutari suo* (in his salvation), is in the Hebrew בשועתו, *salutem suam* (its salvation). But because the second part of a verse for the most part repeateth and explaineth the first, and in the first it is said, *shall rejoice in the Lord,* St. Augustine rightly understandeth by *salutare* (salvation) the Lord himself, who said above: "I am thy salvation." And that, *all my bones shall say,* is like that which is had in *Ps.* cii: "Bless the Lord, O my soul: and let all that is within me bless his holy name." Now in this phrase is signified a certain perfect gladness, which filleth the whole man. For sometimes the mind hath joy but the body hath pain, and then there is not full

<!-- p. 215 (PDF 226) -->

and perfect gladness: but when the Lord shall heal all our diseases, and shall satisfy all our desire with good things, then at length the whole man, filled with unspeakable delight, shall rejoice in the Lord, and all his parts, even to the insensible bones, shall say to the Lord: "Who is like to thee?" For as insensible things are said to thirst when they lack necessary helps, according to that of *Ps.* lxii: "For thee my soul hath thirsted; for thee my flesh, O how many ways!" so also the same insensible things, when their lack is taken away, may be said to rejoice and to leap for joy. That, *Lord, who is like to thee?* is doubled in the Greek, *Lord, Lord;* but the doubling is neither had in the Hebrew, nor read by Augustine, or Theodoret, or Euthymius. That, *eripiens inopem* (delivering the poor), is in the Hebrew more easily joined with what goeth before: for so it is read to the word: "Lord, who is as thou, who deliverest the poor?" The meaning therefore is thus to be joined: *Domine, quis similis tibi, qui es eripiens inopem de manu fortiorum ejus?* (Lord, who is like to thee, who art he that delivereth the poor from the hand of them that are stronger than he?) That also, *de manu fortiorum ejus* (from the hand of them that are stronger than he), is clearer in the Hebrew; for so we read, מציל עני מחזק ממנו, *eripiens inopem a robusto præ ipso* (delivering the poor from one mighty beyond himself), that is, from an enemy mightier than he himself is. But the Latin Interpreter rendered to the word what he found in the Greek. Lastly, for those words, *a diripientibus eum* (from them that strip him), the Hebrew hath only מגזלו *miggozelo, a violento* (from the violent man); but the Interpreters chose to explain more at large what was contained in a single word in the original text.

[Margin: The soul sometimes hath joy, and the body pain.]

[Margin: Verse 13.]

**13. UNJUST WITNESSES RISING UP HAVE ASKED ME THINGS I KNEW NOT.**

[Margin: Verse 14.]

**14. THEY REPAID ME EVIL FOR GOOD: TO THE DEPRIVING ME OF MY SOUL.**[^6]

The Prophet now goeth on to the second kind of persecutions, in which the wicked persecute the just with false testimonies, not from ambush, but openly, and he wonderfully heighteneth the wickedness of these witnesses. *First* he saith *rising up,* to signify that they did not wait for a summons, but came of themselves. *Secondly* he addeth, *things I knew not,* to show that the just man is accused by the unjust witnesses of that which he had neither done nor thought. For we are said not to know those things which we do not approve, nor have ever done, as though we knew not how to do them. So of Christ the Apostle saith, II *Cor.* v: "Him, who knew no sin, he hath made sin for us." *Thirdly* he addeth, *they asked me,* to show the boldness and imprudence of the witnesses, who, not content to have spoken false testimony before the judge, dare also to rebuke and to question the just man himself to his face. *Fourthly* he addeth, *they repaid me evil for good,* to show that the false witnesses, provoked by no injury, but on the contrary loaded with benefits, were led to calumniate out of mere malice. He addeth last, *sterilitatem animæ meæ* (barrenness to my soul), to show that by false testimonies they seek not some light evil, but the greatest of all. For the barrenness set in this place may be taken in two ways: first, in general, for desolation and universal wasting, such as befell holy Job, who in one day lost wealth, children, health. And hither pertaineth the exposition of Theodoret and Euthymius, who take *barrenness* for *oblivion,* that is, such a desolation that the very name also is given over to oblivion. Secondly, barrenness may be taken for one kind of evil, but of the greatest account, both with the devil, who endeavoureth to inflict it by his ministers, and with the just man, who is forced to suffer it. That evil is, that the just man, branded with infamy by false testimonies, remain barren and bereft, so that he can bear no fruit, and that which he hath already borne may perish. So St. Augustine seemeth to expound.

[Margin: Barrenness is here taken in two ways.]

[Margin: Verse 15.]

**15. BUT AS FOR ME, WHEN THEY WERE TROUBLESOME TO ME, I WAS CLOTHED WITH HAIRCLOTH.**

[Margin: Verse 16.]

**16. I HUMBLED MY SOUL WITH FASTING; AND MY PRAYER SHALL BE TURNED INTO MY BOSOM.**

Before the Prophet beginneth to speak of the third kind of persecutors, he setteth forth what he did against the persecutors of the second kind, and saith that he did not render evil for evil, nor think of vengeance, but turned himself to pray to God with great humility: "When, saith he, the adversaries were troublesome to me," I did not proudly insult over them, but clothed in sackcloth I began to fast, that my prayer might the more easily appease God. For sackcloth and fasting are the wings of prayer. So that king of the Ninivites, praying with sackcloth and fasting, is heard, *Jonas* iii. Which same we read of king Achab, III *Kings* xxv;[^7] whence also the wise man saith: "The prayer of him that humbleth himself shall pierce the clouds." And he addeth: "And my prayer shall be turned into my bosom," to show that he expecteth no doubtful fruit of prayer. For *in sinu meo* (in my bosom) is put for *in sinum meum* (into my bosom), as is had in the Hebrew and Greek text, as though he said: Prayer proceeding from so great humility shall not return to me empty, but shall fill my bosom with heavenly consolation. We may also, if we will, take that, *in sinu meo,* as it lieth, in the ablative case, and expound this place of the frequency of prayer, so that the sense is: My prayer, being in my bosom, shall often return and run back, that what it found not, seeking once or twice, it may find by persevering, that is, by often returning. And although to the letter these things seem not to agree to Christ, of whom we do not read in the Gospel that he fasted and prayed clothed in haircloth while he suffered persecutions, yet they agree to the same Christ as he is in his members; for this is proper to the members of Christ, while they suffer adversity, not to contend with adversaries, but to return to their own heart, and humbly to make supplication to God. As to the words, that, *cum mihi molesti essent* (when they were troublesome to me), is in the Hebrew בחלותם *bachalotham, cum ipsi infirmarentur* (when they were sick). But it is likely that the Seventy Interpreters read with other points, or with the addition of some other letter, in the conjugation *piel,* or *hiphil.* For then that verb ought to be taken actively, as not only the Seventy, but also St. Jerome translated from the Hebrew. For so

[Margin: Sackcloth and fasting are the wings of prayer.]

<!-- p. 216 (PDF 227) -->

we read in Jerome, *cum infirmarer ab eis* (when I was made sick by them), that is, when they made me to be sick, which is the same with that which the Seventy read, *cum mihi molesti essent.* It may also be said that the Seventy and St. Jerome read, *cum infirmarentur ipsi* (when they themselves were sick), as we now read in the Hebrew; but that they considered more deeply that the Prophet speaketh of that sickness of mind which is always joined with unjust persecution; for whosoever persecuteth another unjustly hurteth and layeth waste himself; and while he maketh another sick with the disease of punishment, he himself is sick with the disease of fault. Rightly therefore have the Seventy and St. Jerome rendered the meaning; for it is the same, as to the sense, *cum mihi molesti essent,* and *cum morbo iracundiæ,* or *odii,* or *invidentiæ, adversus me ægrotarent* (when they were sick against me with the disease of anger, or of hatred, or of envy). And that, *induebar cilicio* (I was clothed with haircloth), is in the Hebrew text, and in the Greek, *induebar sacco* (I was clothed with sackcloth). Whence we understand that by haircloth is not to be understood a garment of the hair of beasts, which is wont to be worn within, as we read of king Joram, IV *Kings* vi; but a garment of sackcloth, which some use outwardly also to signify humility, as Jonas testifieth of the king of the Ninivites, chap. iii. That, *I humbled my soul with fasting,* is a Hebrew phrase; for in many places the Hebrews put the soul for the whole man.

[Margin: Verse 17.]

**17. AS A NEIGHBOUR AND AS AN OWN BROTHER, SO DID I PLEASE: AS ONE MOURNING AND SORROWFUL SO WAS I HUMBLED.**

This verse is much clearer in the Hebrew text than in the Greek or Latin. For it may thus be rendered to the word with St. Jerome: *Quasi ad amicum, quasi ad fratrem meum sic ambulabam; quasi lugens mater tristis, sic incurvabam* (As to a friend, as to my brother, so did I walk; as a mourning mother, sorrowful, so did I bow down); and the sense is: I, being placed in tribulation, not only did not render evil for evil, but rendered good for evil: for I was so affected toward each of my adversaries as a friend toward a friend, yea as toward a brother, or rather as a mother toward a little and languishing son. For as a mother, seeing her son sick, boweth herself down sorrowful and groaning to take him up, so did I also bear myself with my adversaries. A tender affection toward enemies could not be more grandly and more clearly expressed. And indeed the Prophet David performed in deed what in this place he expressed in the person of Christ, and in his own, and in that of all perfect men. For David bewailed Saul when dead as a son, whom, while he lived, he loved as a brother. The same did Christ far more excellently, who seeing the city wept over it, *Luke* xix; and likened himself to a hen striving to gather her chickens under her wings, *Matt.* xxiii. The reading of the Latin edition hath the same sense: but it is obscure, because it hath rendered the Greek words in as many Latin words. But it is to be made clear and supplied from the Hebrew reading. This therefore will be the sense: *Quasi proximum, quasi fratrem nostrum sic complacebam* (As a neighbour, as our brother, so did I please), that is, I pleased myself, or was affected toward each of my adversaries as toward a neighbour, or companion and friend, yea even as toward our brother. "As one mourning and sorrowful, so was I humbled," that is, not only was I affected to my persecutor as to a neighbour and brother; but "I was humbled" and bowed myself down to him, grieving out of compassion, and sorrowful for his evils, as a tender mother boweth down sorrowful to her son, languid and lying sick.

[Margin: The tender affection of David toward his enemies.]

[Margin: Verse 18.]

**18. BUT THEY REJOICED AGAINST ME, AND CAME TOGETHER: SCOURGES WERE GATHERED TOGETHER UPON ME, AND I KNEW NOT.**

The Psalm teacheth in consequence that the persecutors of the just render evil for good, and at the same time passeth to the third kind of persecutions, in which the wicked, not content to have vexed the just with frauds and calumnies, set upon them with scourges also. "But they rejoiced against me;" I grieved for their evils, they rejoiced at my evils: nor content with this wickedness, "they came together against me," armed with scourges, that, if they could, they might kill me; "and I knew not," that is, I knew not their counsels, and therefore could not beware, or I bore myself so patiently as though I knew not what they did. As to the words, for *et adversum me* (and against me), St. Jerome translateth, *et in infirmitate mea* (and in my infirmity): for the Hebrew word צלעי may signify *latus meum* (my side), and *infirmitas mea* (my infirmity). The Seventy followed the former signification, and therefore translated, *adversum me,* which is almost the same as *ad latus meum* (at my side). St. Jerome followed the latter. But in the meaning there is no difference. And that, *congregata sunt super me flagella* (scourges were gathered together upon me), is in the Hebrew, *congregati sunt super me flagellantes* (scourgers were gathered together upon me); but the sense is the same. Lastly, for *ignoravi* (I knew not), St. Augustine readeth, *ignoraverunt* (they knew not), and referreth it to the persecutors, who truly knew not how great men they struck, when they scourged Christ, or the saints. But although the Greek word is ambiguous, yet the Hebrew ידעתי is not ambiguous, nor can it be rendered save in the first person: accordingly the Latin Vulgate edition rightly hath, *et ignoravi,* as the Greeks Theodoret and Euthymius also read.

[Margin: Verse 19.]

**19. THEY WERE SEPARATED, AND REPENTED NOT: THEY TEMPTED ME, THEY SCOFFED AT ME WITH SCORN: THEY GNASHED UPON ME WITH THEIR TEETH.**

He goeth on to explain the malice of the adversaries, and saith that they could not fulfil what they desired, the divine providence protecting its own, that they should not be hurt by the unjust persecutors. But that not even so did they rest: for those whom they had not been able to move with scourges of stripes,[^8] they tried to terrify with scourges of words, with mockings and threatenings. "They were separated," saith he, that is, they who had met together to hurt and to scourge were divided one from another, God scattering their conventicle: but they did not therefore "repent" (*compuncti sunt*), that is, they did not come to a better mind; or, as the Hebrew word דמו properly soundeth, they were not *silent* and *at rest,* as was meet; but rather they tempted me, namely to

<!-- p. 217 (PDF 228) -->

provoke me to anger, because *they scoffed at me with* a mocking *scorn,* and *gnashed against me with their teeth,* threatening cruel menaces. As to the words, that which we read in the Greek and Latin, *tentaverunt me, subsannaverunt me subsannatione* (they tempted me, they scoffed at me with scorn), St. Jerome translateth from the Hebrew, *in simulatione verborum fictorum* (in the dissembling of feigned words). But the Hebrew words themselves seem to admit neither this sense nor that: for so we read בחנפי לעגי מעוג, that is, *in hypocrisi,* or *simulationibus subsannationum verbi otiosi* (in the hypocrisy, or dissemblings, of the scoffings of an idle word). But truly it is altogether credible that the Seventy Interpreters read otherwise, and had the best books, especially since the Hebrew words, as they are now read with the points of the Rabbins, seem to signify nothing. I think therefore that the points are to be changed, and that of the noun, *in simulationibus* (in dissemblings), is to be made a verb of the infinitive mood with the affix of the first person, which will have to be rendered, *in tentando me*[^9] (in tempting me). For although חנף signifieth *simulare* (to dissemble), and *hypocritam esse* (to be a hypocrite), yet to tempt is proper to hypocrites, according to that of the Lord: "Why do you tempt me, ye hypocrites?" *Matt.* xxii; and *in tentando me* is the same as *in hypocrisi agendo mecum* (in dealing with me in hypocrisy); and in like manner of the noun *subsannationum* (of scoffings) will have to be made a verb of the past tense, with the affix of the first person, and then the sense will be, *subsannaverunt me* (they scoffed at me); and that, מעוג, signifieth indeed *verbum otiosum* (an idle word), but it signifieth also *subsannationem* (scorn). Accordingly the whole sense will be, *in tentando me subsannaverunt me subsannatione* (in tempting me they scoffed at me with scorn), which is the same with that which we have, *tentaverunt me, subsannaverunt me subsannatione.*

[Margin: Verse 20.]

**20. LORD, WHEN WILT THOU LOOK UPON ME? RESCUE THOU MY SOUL FROM THEIR MALICE: MY ONLY ONE FROM THE LIONS.**

The persecutors having been set forth, the Prophet returneth, in the person of all the just who suffer persecution, to pray to God, that he may join the end with the beginning. And because God seemeth not to look upon what is done on earth, when he doth not punish the unjust, therefore he saith: "Lord, when wilt thou look upon me?" that is, when wilt thou show, by punishing, that thou seest the evils of the wicked? "Rescue thou (*restitue*) my soul from their malice," that is, deliver my life from the danger in which it is, while it is in the power of the malignant, and restore it to its former security, or, restore to me the life which I have almost lost, while it is in the hands of the malignant. Which same thing he repeateth, and more clearly explaineth, when he adjoineth: "My only one from the lions," that is, restore to me my soul, which is my only one, and thereby most beloved, which thou wilt do by plucking it out of the power of the enemies, who for cruelty and fierceness may be called lions, and who *gnash,* as was said above, *with their teeth.* As to the words, that, *a malignitate* (from malice), St. Augustine readeth, *ab astutiis* (from crafts); and St. Jerome translateth, *a calamitatibus* (from calamities). The cause of the diversity was that the Seventy derived the Hebrew word משאיהם from the verb נשא, which is *decipere* (to deceive), and therefore St. Augustine readeth, *astutiis;* but our Interpreter, *a malignitate;* and St. Jerome derived it from another root, namely from שאה *saah,* which is *desolari* (to be made desolate), and therefore he translateth, *a calamitatibus;* but the sense is the same, as is plain. By *the only one* St. Augustine understandeth the Church, which Christ willeth to be plucked out from the persecutors. Which indeed is true, but to the letter it seemeth altogether to be referred to the soul, or life. As also that of *Ps.* xxi: "Deliver, O God, my soul from the sword: my only one from the hand of the dog." For the soul is rightly called the only one, as being singularly beloved. For temporal life is the foundation of all temporal goods, and eternal life of all goods simply. And so the Lord also saith in the Gospel, *Matt.* xvi: "What doth it profit a man, if he gain the whole world, and suffer the loss of his own soul? Or what exchange shall a man give for his soul?" and yet many are so foolish that for nothing they willingly lose the soul, which they ought to have loved above all things.

[Margin: The soul is rightly called the only one, and why?]

[Margin: Verse 21.]

**21. I WILL GIVE THANKS TO THEE IN A GREAT CHURCH; I WILL PRAISE THEE IN A STRONG PEOPLE.**

He promiseth that he will not be ungrateful, if he obtain deliverance from the adversaries. "I will give thanks to thee, saith he, in a great church," that is, I will not hide thy benefit, but will publish it openly before all; and he repeateth the same in other words, "I will praise thee in a strong people." For *confiteri* (to confess, here to give thanks) and *laudare* (to praise) are the same, and the same also are a great church and a strong people. For the church is called great by reason of multitude, which the Hebrew word רב *rab* properly signifieth, that is a church that is *many;* and likewise the people is called *gravis* (weighty), or, as is had more expressively in the Hebrew, עצום, *robustus* (mighty), by reason of multitude. For then is a people mighty, when it aboundeth with so many men that it feareth not enemies. The Prophet seemeth also to look to the Christian Church, in which God is continually praised for the deliverance of the godly. For the Christian church is truly many and great, since it possesseth the whole world, and truly weighty and mighty, since "the gates of hell cannot prevail against it," *Matt.* xvi. The Church triumphant also will be a great Church, since it is written of it, *Apoc.* vii: "I saw a great multitude, which no man could number;" and a mighty people, since it is added in the same place: "And palms in their hands."

[Margin: The Christian Church is truly many.]

[Margin: Verse 22.]

**22. LET NOT THEM THAT ARE MY ENEMIES WRONGFULLY REJOICE OVER ME: WHO HAVE HATED ME WITHOUT CAUSE, AND WINK WITH THE EYES.**

He returneth to the prayer which he had begun, and asketh to be delivered from persecutors, especially hypocrites, who feign themselves friends, when they are nothing less. "Let them not rejoice over me" (*non supergaudeant mihi*), that is, *non gaudeant super me* (let them not have joy over me), or let them not be glad of my destruction, "that are my enemies wrongfully" (*inique*), that is, who persecute me feigning friendship: for the Hebrew hath

<!-- p. 218 (PDF 229) -->

שקר, which properly signifieth *mendacium* (a lie); whence St. Jerome translateth, *inimici mei mendaces* (my lying enemies); and this very thing is the greatest iniquity, to persecute a man, and to deceive him under the show of friendship. This same is declared and repeated in the following words: "Who have hated me without cause, and wink with the eyes," that is, who hate me without cause, when I have brought no evil upon them, and yet feign friendship by winking with the eyes, saluting, looking kindly, and approving what I say. What is it, saith Augustine, to wink with the eyes? Declaring with the countenance what they bear not in the heart.

[Margin: Verse 23.]

**23. FOR THEY SPOKE INDEED PEACEABLY TO ME; AND SPEAKING IN THE ANGER OF THE EARTH THEY DEVISED GUILE.**

The Prophet declareth why he said of his enemies, *and wink with the eyes,* and saith: For they indeed with outward words spoke peaceably with me, as though they were friends; but inwardly they were rent asunder with anger, and *devised guile,* whereby to destroy me: so much for the sense. In the words there is great difficulty; for the Hebrew copy hath thus to the word: *Quoniam non pacem loquebantur, et super scissuras terræ verba dolosa meditabantur* (For they spoke not peace, and upon the rents of the earth they meditated deceitful words). And so also St. Jerome translateth, save that for *scissuras terræ* (the rents of the earth) he putteth, *in rapina terræ* (in the rapine of the earth); but the Greek copy hath thus: *Quoniam mihi quidem pacifica loquebantur, et super iracundiam dolos cogitabant* (For to me indeed they spoke peaceable things, and upon anger they devised guile). And so St. Augustine, Theodoret and Euthymius read and interpret. But it is to be observed that the Hebrew word לא *lo,* which signifieth *non* (not), was read by the Seventy Interpreters לי *li,* which signifieth *mihi* (to me), and it is altogether credible that the Seventy Interpreters had more correct Hebrew books: accordingly it seemeth not to be doubted that the Latin reading of the Vulgate edition, which answereth to the word which the Seventy had in their books, is better than that which we now have in the Hebrew. And that which the Hebrew hath, *super scissuras terræ,* the Seventy translated, *super iracundiam,* because they understood the speech of the Prophet to be metaphorical, and that by *the rents of the earth* was signified anger, which is a certain rent of the earthly and animal heart. For they who are angry are rent asunder inwardly, and lose quiet and peace: and indeed the Latin Interpreter chose to add that word *terræ* (of the earth), which he found in the Hebrew; but the Greek Interpreter thought it enough that it be understood in the word anger, which is a heat about the heart. That word therefore, *terræ,* is to be explained so as to be taken for the earthly and animal heart, as we have said. But why St. Jerome translateth, *rapinam terræ,* I do not make out, unless by rents he metaphorically understood rapine, or perchance the Hebrew word can signify this also. But absolutely the interpretation of the Seventy Interpreters, which the Church followeth in the Latin edition, is to be preferred. And that which we have in the Latin edition: *Loquentes dolos cogitabant* (speaking they devised guile), differeth not in sense from the Hebrew words, *verba dolosa cogitabant* (they devised deceitful words). For it is the same to devise deceitful words, and, speaking within oneself, to devise guile. Nor doth it much matter that the Seventy have not *verba* (words) or *loquentes* (speaking), but simply, *dolos cogitabant* (they devised guile), because in the devising itself the inward speech is sufficiently understood.

[Margin: The Latin Vulgate edition is better than that which we now have in the Hebrew.]

[Margin: Verse 24.]

**24. AND THEY OPENED THEIR MOUTH WIDE AGAINST ME; THEY SAID: WELL DONE, WELL DONE, OUR EYES HAVE SEEN IT.**

How true that is which he said above of his enemies, that with a feigned heart they spoke peaceably, the Prophet showeth in the person of the just in this verse, where he saith that the same enemies who at one time flattered and winked with the eyes, afterwards, when he had fallen into those calamities which they had fraudulently contrived, began with mouth opened wide openly to insult, and to congratulate one another and say: We have seen at last what we so greatly desired. All this we read in the Gospel to have been fulfilled in Christ, who is the head of the just: for at one time the enemies spoke peaceably, saying: "We know that thou art a true speaker, and teachest the way of God in truth," *Matt.* xxii; but meanwhile they meditated guile, that they might insnare him in his speech. But when they saw him fastened to the cross, "they opened their mouth wide, insulting and saying: Vah, thou that destroyest the temple of God," etc. As to the words, all the copies agree, this excepted, that the Hebrew interjection האח the Seventy rendered *euge* (well done); St. Jerome, *vah;* and *euge* is of one that is glad, *vah* of one that insulteth: but it mattereth little; for each is of one that rejoiceth, and the rejoicing of enemies is a kind of insulting.

[Margin: Verse 25.]

**25. THOU HAST SEEN, O LORD, BE NOT THOU SILENT: O LORD, DEPART NOT FROM ME.**

[Margin: Verse 26.]

**26. ARISE, AND BE ATTENTIVE TO MY JUDGMENT: TO MY CAUSE, MY GOD, AND MY LORD.**

[Margin: Verse 27.]

**27. JUDGE ME, O LORD, ACCORDING TO THY JUSTICE; O LORD MY GOD, LET THEM NOT REJOICE OVER ME.**[^10]

The Prophet returneth to prayer, repeating the selfsame thing in other and other words to express his affection. "Thou hast seen, saith he, O Lord," namely how great is the oppression of thy poor servant; "be not thou silent," as though thou sawest not, or couldst not, or wouldst not defend them that hope in thee. "Depart not," or, as the Hebrew word hath it, *ne elongeris a me* (be not far from me), that is, forsake me not placed in tribulation, as they do who go far away. And not only be not silent, and depart not, but neither sit, as though idly looking on, or slumbering; but arise, and as a just and powerful judge apply thy mind diligently to my judgment, that is, to the suit which I have with my persecutors; be attentive, I say, "my God, to my cause, and judge me according to thy justice, O Lord," that is, if thy justice, which is most right, judgeth that I am unjustly oppressed by my adversaries, deliver me out of their hands, that they may no longer rejoice over my miseries.

<!-- p. 219 (PDF 230) -->

[Margin: Verse 28.]

**28. LET THEM NOT SAY IN THEIR HEARTS: IT IS WELL, IT IS WELL, TO OUR MIND: NEITHER LET THEM SAY: WE HAVE SWALLOWED HIM UP.**

[Margin: Verse 29.]

**29. LET THEM BLUSH: AND BE ASHAMED TOGETHER, WHO REJOICE AT MY EVILS.**

[Margin: Verse 30.]

**30. LET THEM BE CLOTHED WITH CONFUSION AND SHAME, WHO SPEAK GREAT THINGS AGAINST ME.**

He explaineth that which he had said: Let them not rejoice over me; "let them not say, saith he, in their hearts: It is well, it is well, to our mind" (*Euge, euge, animæ nostræ*), that is, let them not rejoice in their hearts over my life, as though now extinguished. For as that, *non supergaudeant mihi,* signifieth, *non gaudeant super me* (let them not have joy over me): so also this, *non dicant euge, animæ nostræ* (let them not say, well done, to our soul), ought to be expounded: *Non dicant euge super animam nostram* (let them not say, well done, over our soul), and he explaineth in the following words: "Neither let them say, saith he, we have swallowed him up," that is, let them not be able to boast that I have been devoured by them, as by lions. But on the contrary rather, the hope of victory being lost, let them blush and be ashamed, that is, let all my enemies together be abashed, who now in hope of victory congratulate themselves on my evils, that is, those hanging over me; nor let them only lightly blush and be abashed, but "let them be clothed with confusion and blushing," that is, let them be wholly covered with confusion, "who speak great things against me," that is, who magnificently boasted their power over me. Some read *maligna* (malignant things), for *magna* (great things); but our reading agreeth with the Greek and Hebrew truth.

[Margin: Verse 31.]

**31. LET THEM REJOICE AND BE GLAD, WHO ARE WELL PLEASED WITH MY JUSTICE, AND LET THEM SAY ALWAYS: THE LORD BE MAGNIFIED, WHO DELIGHT IN THE PEACE OF HIS SERVANT.**[^11]

[Margin: Verse 32.]

**32. AND MY TONGUE SHALL MEDITATE THY JUSTICE, THY PRAISE ALL THE DAY LONG.**

As the Prophet prayed that the men of iniquity may be confounded, so also now he prayeth that the just may rejoice and be glad, who wish him well, and love his innocence, or desire that his justice may openly appear. He stirreth up also to the praise of God all who desire his peace, which was to follow from his deliverance from all evils. The Hebrew hath, *qui vult pacem servi sui* (who willeth the peace of his servant). The Seventy Interpreters seem to have read the verb in the plural number, which in our books is in the singular number; but each sense is very good. David closeth the Psalm promising thanksgiving for the benefit received: "My tongue, saith he, shall meditate thy justice," that is, shall be exercised in publishing thy justice; and this same he repeateth, and by repeating declareth, saying: "Thy praise all the day long," that is, my tongue shall all the day long meditate thy praise, that is, shall be exercised in publishing the praise of thy justice. And that he saith, *all the day long,* signifieth *frequently* and *continually,* so that forgetfulness of so great a benefit may never creep in. St. Augustine in this place piously admonisheth that he who always doeth well always praiseth God.
