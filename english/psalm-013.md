BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XIII.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*Unto the end, a psalm for David.*[^1]

The title hath nothing new. But the argument is a lamentation over corrupted human nature, such as is chiefly seen in unbelievers, in whom it hath not yet begun to be healed by grace.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. THE FOOL HATH SAID IN HIS HEART: THERE IS NO GOD.**

To so great folly did human nature, corrupted in the first man, come down, that there hath been found one who, although he dared not with his voice deny that there is a God, yet said in his heart: "There is no God." David doth not signify that one certain foolish man said this, but he would say that men corrupted in mind come to so great blindness, that they become fools even concerning the last end, and think that there is no God who careth for human affairs, and to whom they are bound to render an account. "The fool," saith he, "hath said," that is, the man brought to the utmost folly, "hath said in his heart: There is no God," that is, hath begun to think that there is no God.

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. THEY ARE CORRUPT, AND ARE BECOME ABOMINABLE IN THEIR WAYS: THERE IS NONE THAT DOTH GOOD, NO NOT ONE.**

That phrase, *usque ad unum* (no not one), is not found in the Hebrew and Greek edition. In the Latin it is found, and it is cited and expounded by Saints Jerome and Augustine. The Prophet hath said that the mind was corrupted, and made foolish: now he addeth that the will also was corrupted, so that men for the most part sin, but never do well; for it is one thing not to sin, another to do well, if we speak of a work absolutely and perfectly good. For men destitute of faith[^6] and grace, using only the strength of corrupted nature, for the most part sin; but sometimes they also do certain morally good works, which cannot be called sins; but they are not perfectly and absolutely good, as St. Augustine argueth in Book IV *Against Julian*, since they do not lead to the supreme good. David therefore saith: "They are corrupt, and are become abominable in their ways" (*in studiis*, in their pursuits),[^2] that is, in their desires, or affections: they have corrupt and abominable desires, whence they themselves also are corrupt and abominable. "There is none that doth good, no not one"; men indeed are corrupt in desires and in iniquity, but not so generally, that all their desires, and all their works, are corrupt and iniquitous. For certainly when an unbeliever from human inclination hath pity on a poor man, or taketh care of his children, he doth nothing evil. But good perfectly, and absolutely, no man at all doth by the sole strength of corrupted nature. Hence thou hast it, that the true understanding of this place maketh nothing at all for the heretics of this time, who abuse the testimony of this Psalm to prove that all the works of a sinful man, or of one not born again, are sins.

[Margin: Men without grace do certain good works.]

[Margin: Not all the works of a wicked man are sins.]

**3. THE LORD HATH LOOKED DOWN FROM HEAVEN UPON THE CHILDREN OF MEN, TO SEE IF THERE BE ANY THAT UNDERSTAND AND SEEK GOD.**

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. THEY ARE ALL GONE ASIDE, THEY ARE BECOME UNPROFITABLE TOGETHER: THERE IS NONE THAT DOTH GOOD, NO NOT ONE.**

He had said that human nature is corrupted both as to the mind, and as to the will: now he showeth whence he came to know this, namely from the revelation of God. For God, who knoweth all things, seeth that this is so, and hath revealed it to his Prophets. He describeth God, after the manner of men, as having looked from heaven, that is, from his lofty watch-tower, to see "whether there were any that understood," that is, not corrupted as to the mind; "or sought God," that is, not corrupted as to the will, who therefore would understand, and love, and so seek God, who is the supreme good. And what God came to know, that is, made us to know, he explaineth in those words: "They are all gone aside," or are turned away, that is, he saw that all without exception had gone aside from the light of wisdom and from the paths of justice, nay, that they were turned away, and had turned their backs on wisdom and justice. "They are become unprofitable together," that is, he saw that all alike were become unprofitable to God, seeing that they do not worship him, nor serve him, nor pay back the tribute of thanksgiving. Lastly he saw that there is none that doth a work perfectly and absolutely good. St. Jerome, from the Hebrew, for those words: "They are become unprofitable together," rendered *pariter adhæserunt* (they have cleaved together), or *agglutinati sunt* (they are glued together). But I know not what he followed. For the Hebrew word נאלחו *neelachu* signifieth *to rot* and *to stink*, and it could have been rendered *putrefacti sunt* (they are become rotten), and therefore are become unprofitable. For things rotten are of no use.

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. THEIR THROAT IS AN OPEN SEPULCHRE: WITH THEIR TONGUES THEY ACTED DECEITFULLY; THE POISON OF ASPS IS UNDER THEIR LIPS.**

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. THEIR MOUTH IS FULL OF CURSING AND BITTERNESS; THEIR FEET ARE SWIFT TO SHED BLOOD.**

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. DESTRUCTION AND UNHAPPINESS IN THEIR WAYS: AND THE WAY OF PEACE THEY HAVE NOT KNOWN: THERE IS NO FEAR OF GOD BEFORE THEIR EYES.**

These three verses are not in the Hebrew, nor in the translation of the Seventy Interpreters, as St. Jerome testifieth, both in the preface of Book XVI *On Isaias*, and in the commentary on this place. Nay, neither are they found in the Latin edition of Psalm lii, which is altogether the same with this thirteenth. But because Blessed Paul, *Rom.* iii, from divers places of Scripture, namely *Ps.* v, *Ps.* cxxxix, *Ps.* ix, *Isa.* xxix,[^3] and *Ps.* xxxv, cited all these verses together, after he had alleged the two verses last explained, some of the ancients judged that all these verses belong to this Psalm, and had by some chance fallen out; therefore they brought them over hither from the Apostle. Now by these verses is signified the malice of wicked men, who by word and work hurt their neighbours. "Their throat is an open sepulchre," that is, as from an open sepulchre there breatheth out the stench of dead bodies, so from the mouth of these men come forth foul words that testify the corruption of the heart. "With their tongues they acted deceitfully," that is, they speak not only foul words, but also deceitful ones. "The poison of asps is under their lips," that is, they pour out words not only foul and deceitful, but also poisoned and infecting, and provoking to evil. "Their mouth is full of cursing and bitterness; their feet are swift to shed blood," that is, these abandoned men not only curse, and quarrel in bitter contentions, but also do evil and hurt in very deed. For they whose mouth is full of cursing and bitter threatenings, their feet run most swiftly to hurt and to kill. "Destruction and unhappiness in their ways: and the way of peace they have not known," that is, all their pursuits look to crushing, laying waste, and afflicting with calamity. For the way of peace, that is, peaceful pursuits, they are wholly ignorant of. "There is no fear of God before their eyes." This is the fountain of all the evils aforesaid, because they have utterly cast away the fear of God, saying in their heart: "There is no God." Let us now return to the verses proper to this Psalm.

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. SHALL NOT ALL THEY KNOW THAT WORK INIQUITY, WHO DEVOUR MY PEOPLE AS THEY EAT BREAD?**

[Margin: By the grace of God men are made truly pious and just.]

From this verse it is manifestly gathered that what was said above of the universal corruption of mankind is to be understood of mankind such as it is of itself, that is, according to the strength of corrupted nature alone. For by the grace of God men are made truly just and pious, and these he calleth in this place his people, whom those wicked men persecute, and hold for nothing. "Shall not all they know," etc., is the voice of one rebuking, as if he said: Will they always be fools; will they not at some time open their eyes; will they not at length begin to know? "Who devour my people as they eat bread," signifieth that the wicked could have been admonished of their iniquity by this so great evil. For as bread is eaten daily, and always hath a relish: so the wicked always and with delight vex the pious, and are never satisfied.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. THEY HAVE NOT CALLED UPON THE LORD: THERE HAVE THEY TREMBLED FOR FEAR, WHERE THERE WAS NO FEAR.**

David giveth the reason why the wicked do so great evils, namely, because they "have not called upon" God, that is, they have not placed their hope in the true God, but in created things, and therefore "there have they trembled, where there was no fear," that is, not knowing in whom

we must hope, and of whom we must be afraid, they have trembled lest they should incur temporal evils, or lest they should lose temporal goods, which are not matter of just fear, since they are small and perishable; and also because, if they hoped in the Lord, "and sought the kingdom of God, and his justice, all these things would be added unto them," *Matt.* v.[^4]

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. FOR THE LORD IS IN THE JUST GENERATION: YOU HAVE CONFOUNDED THE COUNSEL OF THE POOR MAN, BUT THE LORD IS HIS HOPE.**

This verse giveth the reason why the wicked lovers of this world fear where there is no fear. The wicked feared where there was no fear, "for the Lord is in the just generation," or *justorum* (of the just), as the Greek text hath it, that is, as the wicked have not called upon God, nor hoped in him: so God hath forsaken them, and abideth in the generation of the just; and being forsaken by God, by the true light, by truth itself, they walk in darkness: and therefore they fear even where there is no cause of fearing. Then he maketh an apostrophe to the wicked themselves, saying: "You have confounded the counsel of the poor man, because the Lord is his hope,"[^5] as if he said: So blinded are you, that not only do you not call upon God, but you mock those who do so. "For the counsel of the poor man," who wholly dependeth on God, "you have confounded," that is, you have mocked, that you might make the poor man blush, as though it were foolish to place hope in God. And you have mocked the counsel of the poor man as foolish, "because the Lord is his hope," that is, because that poor man hath placed his hope in the Lord. For it seemeth a foolish counsel to the men of this world to hope in God, who is not seen, and not to hope in riches and other things, which are seen.

[Margin: Verse 11.]

**11. WHO SHALL GIVE OUT OF SION THE SALVATION OF ISRAEL? WHEN THE LORD SHALL HAVE TURNED AWAY THE CAPTIVITY OF HIS PEOPLE, JACOB SHALL REJOICE AND ISRAEL SHALL BE GLAD.**

This last verse prayeth God for the speedy coming of the Saviour, to deliver mankind from the captivity of the devil, in which were all those blinded and perverse men, whose darkness and malice he had described through the whole Psalm. "Who shall give out of Sion the salvation of Israel?" that is, O that salvation may quickly come out of Sion to Israel himself! "For salvation is of the Jews," as the Lord saith to the Samaritan woman, *John* iv; and not only of the Jews, that is, of the tribe of Juda, but of the family of David, whose city was called Sion. And so salvation, or the promised Saviour, was expected and desired out of Sion the city of David, that is, of the stock of David, who should save Israel, that is, his people. Now Christ is called the Saviour of Israel his people, because, although he came for the whole world, yet in very deed he hath saved none but certain ones, who are called the people of God, and the spiritual Israel. "When he shall have turned away, or turned back, the captivity of his people, Jacob shall rejoice and Israel shall be glad," that is, I pray and ask for salvation out of Sion, and therefore for a Saviour, to deliver from captivity, because, when that shall have been done, then truly and perfectly shall the people of God rejoice and be glad, which is spiritually called Jacob and Israel: for both are the name of the same man. And that these promises, and others like them, do not belong to the carnal Israelites alone, but to the people of God gathered partly from the Jews, partly from the Gentiles, the Apostle teacheth in the Epistle *to the Romans*, and more copiously Augustine in Epistle 200 *to Asellicus*.

[^3]: Translator's note: the words are at Isa. lix, 7-8. The reference to chapter xxix is given as printed.

[^4]: Translator's note: the words are at Matt. vi, 33. The reference to chapter v is given as printed.
