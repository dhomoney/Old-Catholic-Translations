BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XLVII.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*A psalm of a canticle, for the sons of Core, on the second day of the week.*

[Margin: What day is the second of the Sabbath?]

The title is known from those things which have been said elsewhere, except those words, *secunda Sabbati* (on the second day of the week; to the word, on the second of the Sabbath), which are not had in the Hebrew, but were added by the Greeks. Now by those words it is signified that the Psalm was delivered to the sons of Core, that they might sing it *on the second of the Sabbath,* that is, on the second day of the week. But which is the second day, and why it was to be sung on the second day, is not agreed among authors. St. Ambrose thinketh that *the second of the Sabbath* is the Lord's day, because it is the second from the Sabbath, which was the principal feast of the Jews; and since we read in the Gospels that Christ rose again on the first of the Sabbath, St. Ambrose answereth that the Lord's day was among the Jews the second of the Sabbath, and is therefore so named in the title of this Psalm, but that by reason of the Lord's resurrection the Lord's day began to be the first of the Sabbath, and is therefore called in the Gospels the first of the Sabbath. St. Jerome and St. Augustine understand in this place by the second of the Sabbath the day following after the Lord's day, which is the first of the Sabbath, not only in the Gospels, but also in the title of the twenty-third Psalm: and this is the truer opinion; for the Sabbath among the Jews was indeed a festival and solemn day; yet it was not called the first, but the last day of the week. And because on the first day light was made, and on the second the firmament was made, St. Jerome and St. Augustine will have the foregoing Psalm to have pertained to Christ, who is the true light; but this one to pertain to the Church, which is the spiritual firmament, wherein are innumerable stars, that is, very many men shining with the glory of wisdom and holiness. The argument of the Psalm is the praise of God for the building of the holy city Jerusalem; and indeed the Greeks, Chrysostom, Theodoret and Euthymius, think that it treateth to the letter of the rebuilding of Jerusalem after the return from the Babylonian captivity: the Latins, Ambrose, Jerome and Augustine, by Jerusalem understand simply the Church; but it may be that the Holy Ghost intended both senses, but the latter more: for it is certain that the holy city Jerusalem bore the figure of the Church of Christ.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. GREAT IS THE LORD, AND EXCEEDINGLY TO BE PRAISED IN THE CITY OF OUR GOD, IN HIS HOLY MOUNTAIN.**[^1]

[Margin: Why is the Lord called great?]

The Prophet, being about to praise the building, beginneth from the praise of the architect, and saith that in the building of the holy city there wonderfully shineth the art and wisdom of God, who built it. "Great," saith he, "is the Lord, and exceedingly to be praised." The Lord is great, whether thou regard

<!-- p. 313 (PDF 324) -->

[Margin: The excellence of the Church.]

his essence, or his power, or his wisdom, or his justice, or his mercy: for all are infinite, eternal, incomprehensible; and hence the same "Lord is exceedingly to be praised," so that all the angels, all men, and all his works suffice not to praise him enough. But of those things which have been revealed to us, we have scarce anything greater from which we may better know the greatness of the Lord, and from which we may more praise him, than the building up of the Catholic Church; and therefore the Prophet saith: "Great is the Lord, and exceedingly to be praised in the city of our God, in his holy mountain," that is, the greatness of God, for which he is greatly to be praised, is beheld in the foundation and construction of the Church, which is the city of our God that is set in the holy mountain, that is, upon the height of perfection. For of her it is said, *Isa.* ii: "The mountain of the house of the Lord shall be prepared above the top of mountains."[^2] And *Matt.* v, the Lord himself calleth his Church "a city seated on a mountain." And that we may most briefly touch upon the miracles of this building: *first,* consider that an incredible variety of nations, set far apart in tongues, manners, rites and laws, have so come together into the unity of one faith and of the sacraments that they make one people, yea one family. *Secondly,* consider that the Church was founded upon Peter, as is openly said *Matt.* xvi, that is, upon a man who was a fisherman, mean, unlearned and poor, and yet "so solidly founded that the gates of hell cannot prevail against it:"[^3] for whether the world will or no, in which the Church of Christ is a pilgrim, and let all the unclean spirits rage as much as they will and can, and let all the kings of the earth stir up persecutions, yet she shall come safe to the land of promise, and, being one day set above all the heavens, shall reign most happily for ever. These things assuredly cannot be done save by a great Lord, that is, by a most mighty and most wise architect, who therefore is exceedingly to be praised, that is, is greater than all praise.

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. WITH THE JOY OF THE WHOLE EARTH IS MOUNT SION FOUNDED, ON THE SIDES OF THE NORTH, THE CITY OF THE GREAT KING.**

[Margin: The Church occupieth the whole world.]

The Prophet explaineth why God is greatly to be praised in the Church, which is signified by mount Sion and the city Jerusalem: and he saith the cause is, that by God the Church "is founded with the joy of the whole earth;" and he saith not, was founded, but "is founded," because the Church is always being founded in divers places where before it was not. For the Church is not one small house, which can be founded at once, but it is a great city occupying the whole world, and at divers times it is founded in divers places by spiritual architects, who, succeeding the Apostles, lay by their preaching Christ the foundation stone, and then build living stones upon him. Moreover this founding is done with "the joy of the whole earth," because in the whole earth the Church is founded by the preaching of the Gospel, which preaching bringeth an incredible spiritual gladness to them that receive it: for therefore the knowledge of the kingdom of heaven was compared by the Lord to a treasure hidden in a field, which he that findeth, with joy selleth all that he hath, and buyeth the field in which he knoweth the treasure to be hidden, *Matt.* xiii; and in *Acts* ii, when the Church was being founded in Jerusalem, it is said: "They took their meat with gladness;" and in chap. viii, when Philip was preaching, and founding the Church in Samaria, "there was great joy in that city;" and the Eunuch of queen Candace, when he had heard the faith from the same Philip, and had been baptized, "went on his way rejoicing;" and in chap. xv,[^4] when blessed Paul had begun to preach to the Gentiles, and to lay the foundations of the Church, "the Gentiles were glad, and glorified the word of the Lord." Now mount Sion is said to be founded, that we may understand that the bodily mount Sion is not here treated of: for that was founded from the beginning of the world; but the spiritual mountain, of which that was the figure. Mount Sion therefore in this place is the Church of Christ, which is called mount Sion for the eminence of doctrine and the perfection of life which is in the Church.

[Margin: The Church is called mount Sion, and why?]

[Margin: The Church is the city of the great King.]

The same Church is also called "the sides of the north," because as the bodily mount Sion is situated in the land of promise toward the northern part, and is as it were a certain wall defending that region from that cold and furious wind which is called the North wind: so also the Christian Church is as it were a wall set against the spiritual North wind, that is, against the blasts of the unclean spirits: for they who rest in the bosom of the Church, that is, who acquiesce in the doctrine which is taught in the Church, and obey the laws of the same Church, are not easily hurt by the North wind breathing pernicious doctrines through examples. Lastly the same Church is called "the city of the great King," which maketh most for her nobility: for the Christian Church hath for her proper king Christ, who is the Prince of the kings of the earth, and King of kings, and Lord of lords. For the others who are set over the Church are ministers of Christ, as the Apostle speaketh, I *Cor.* iv: "Let a man so account of us as of the ministers of Christ, and the dispensers of the mysteries of God." And he who hath the supreme power in the Church calleth himself the Vicar of Christ, and nameth himself not only the servant of God, but also the servant of the servants of God. As to the words, the difficulty is only in that word *fundatur* (is founded): for St. Ambrose and St. Augustine read *dilatans* (enlarging); St. Jerome rendereth from the Hebrew *specioso germine* (with a fair shoot); St. John Chrysostom readeth *bene radicans* (rooting well). Others will have the Hebrew word to signify a boundary, or a clime, or a region, or a branch. But St. Ambrose and St. Augustine seem to have read in the Greek text εὐρύνων, which signifieth *bene dilatans* (enlarging well), for εὐρίζων, which signifieth *bene radicans* (rooting well). And certainly the latter reading is the truer, because it agreeth with the Vulgate, the most ancient Latin edition: for, as Chrysostom expoundeth, *radicare* (to root) and *fundare* (to found) are the same. And it is the same,

<!-- p. 314 (PDF 325) -->

*fundatur a Deo mons Sion* (mount Sion is founded by God), and *fundat Deus montem Sion* (God foundeth mount Sion); there is this difference only, that the Greek word hath joined to it the adverb *bene* (well), which the Latin word lacketh. But in the Latin word there is understood *bene* (well), or *pulchre* (fairly), as it is in the Hebrew. From that which is subjoined, "with the joy of the whole earth," it is easily gathered that mount Sion is not only founded, but also fairly and well founded. For therefore the whole earth exulteth, because it seeth this building so fairly and so solidly founded. The version of St. Jerome hath its ground in the Hebrew word יפה נוף *iephe noph,* which can be rendered *specioso germine* (with a fair shoot), or *pulchris ramis* (with fair branches); and the sense will be, that mount Sion is adorned with fair branches, or with fair trees; but that word נוף *noph,* which yet signifieth many and so diverse things according to the opinions of the more recent writers, that it is referred even to regions, and boundaries, and climes, why may it not also be referred to founding and rooting, as the Seventy elders, most ancient and most learned, judged? Therefore we judge that the Hebrew words, agreeably to the version of the Seventy Interpreters, and to the exposition of Chrysostom, of Theodoret, and of St. Jerome in the *Commentary on the Psalms,* can be rendered *bene fundatur* (is well founded).

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. IN HER HOUSES SHALL GOD BE KNOWN, WHEN HE SHALL PROTECT HER.**[^5]

He addeth a second reason why the Lord is great, and greatly to be praised in his city Jerusalem, because he not only founded her well, but also continually protecteth and exalteth her. God, saith he, who founded the Church after the manner of a royal city, shall then chiefly "be known in her houses," that is, he shall then be known by all that dwell in her houses, or he shall then be known to dwell in her houses, when in the time of persecutions he "shall take her up" to be protected and exalted.

[Margin: God properly dwelleth in temples.]

For the word *domibus* (houses), there is in the Hebrew the word ארמנות *armenoth,* which signifieth not any houses whatsoever, but *palatia, basilicas, templa* (palaces, basilicas, temples). Hence therefore we understand that God dwelleth chiefly in houses sacred to God himself, such as are basilicas and temples, which are properly dedicated to God. And for the words *cum suscipiet eam* (when he shall take her up), there is in the Hebrew למשגב *lemisgab,* which signifieth *in refugium* (for a refuge), or *in auxilium* (for a help), or *in exaltationem* (for an exaltation). Accordingly that, *cum suscipiet eam,* is to be explained *cum suscipiet defendendam et exaltandam* (when he shall take her up to be defended and exalted), or *cum susceptor ejus erit* (when he shall be her protector), that is, her helper and defender: for in *Ps.* xlv, where it is read, "the God of Jacob is our protector," for *susceptor* (protector) there is in the Hebrew משגב *misgab,* as in this place.

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. FOR BEHOLD THE KINGS OF THE EARTH ASSEMBLED THEMSELVES: THEY GATHERED TOGETHER.**

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. SO THEY SAW, AND THEY WONDERED, THEY WERE TROUBLED, THEY WERE MOVED: TREMBLING TOOK HOLD OF THEM.**[^6]

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. THERE WERE PAINS AS OF A WOMAN IN LABOUR. WITH A VEHEMENT WIND THOU SHALT BREAK IN PIECES THE SHIPS OF THARSIS.**

These three verses are expounded in a different manner by the Latins, Ambrose, Jerome, Augustine; and by the Greeks, Chrysostom, Theodoret, Euthymius: for the Latin Fathers expound them of the Gentiles converted to the faith, but the Greek of the Gentiles assaulting the faith. Both expositions are true, and the one is not at variance with the other. According to the Latins, therefore, this will be the sense: God shall be known to be in the Church from the conversion of the Gentiles, "for behold the kings of the earth," and their peoples with them, converted by the power of the word of God preached by the Apostles, and by the virtue of the signs and wonders wrought by the same, "assembled themselves, and gathered together," that is, out of divers sects and superstitions they came together into the unity of faith, and were assembled into one Church, as into one people, "yea, that they might be made one heart and one soul,"[^7] as is said of the first Christians in *Acts* iv. And their conversion was after this manner, that "they, seeing" the signs and wonders, "so wondered," and, knowing the greatness of their error and of their sins, in that they worshipped idols for God, "they were troubled and they were moved," through true penance: whence also "trembling took hold of them," considering the peril of eternal damnation in which they had so long been. And no light "trembling," but a most grievous one, "took hold of them," such as is the pain of a woman in labour, which yet endeth in great joy. For when "she that is in labour hath brought forth a son, she remembereth no more the anguish, for joy that a man is born into the world,"[^8] as is said *John* xvi; so also the pain of the penitent endeth in unspeakable gladness, when the grace of adoption, which is the seed and pledge of eternal salvation, is poured into the heart of the penitent; and then is fulfilled that which followeth: "With a vehement wind thou shalt break in pieces the ships of Tharsis;" for the Holy Ghost,[^9] dwelling in the justified[^10] man, and inflaming him with a vehement ardour of charity, "breaketh the ships of Tharsis," by which pride, luxury and avarice are signified: for the ships, hastening with swelling sails through the tossing waves unto Tharsis, brought gold and silver, as is said III *Kings* x and II *Par.* ix. The swelling of the sails denoteth pride, the tossing of the ship luxury, the bringing of gold and silver avarice. Or, according to the exposition of the Greeks, the sense is: God "shall be known" in the Church from the wonderful victory of the Christian religion over the impious persecutors. For "behold the kings of the earth," having heard the fame of the Christian religion, "assembled themselves, they gathered together," that is, from divers places and from divers superstitions they "gathered together" against the Church of Christ, that they might oppress and extinguish her by their persecutions. But "seeing" the fortitude of the holy martyrs, and their wonderful constancy even in the frail sex, and in tender age, and also the signs and wonders

<!-- p. 315 (PDF 326) -->

which God wrought by them, and that the number of the Christians was increased by persecutions and deaths, "they wondered, they were troubled, they were moved," partly with indignation, partly with pain, "and trembling took hold of them," as the pain of a woman in labour, when they saw the temples of the idols forsaken, and the kingdom of Christ grow against their will, and while they resisted with all their strength. And this came to pass because the Lord "with a vehement wind," with a wonderful manifestation of his power, broke idolatry in pieces, as a tempest of wind is wont in a very short time to scatter and break in pieces ships upon the sea. As to the words, that word *terræ* (of the earth), when it is said *reges terræ* (the kings of the earth), is not had in the Hebrew copy, but the Seventy Interpreters added it for the sake of explaining. And that particle *sic* (so) is to be joined with the verb following, not with the foregoing: for the sense is, "they, seeing, so wondered," that is, they saw, and so they wondered. That word, *commoti sunt* (they were moved), is rendered from the Hebrew by St. Jerome *admirati sunt* (they wondered). But the Hebrew word signifieth indeed *admirari* (to wonder), but it signifieth also *commoveri* (to be moved). And since it had already been said, "they wondered, they were troubled," the Seventy rightly rendered the verb following *commoti sunt* (they were moved), both lest the same thing should be twice repeated, and because this is the natural order, that when an unlooked-for evil cometh upon us, there ariseth in us first wonder, then trouble, lastly commotion, or flight. When it is said, "with a vehement wind thou shalt break in pieces the ships of Tharsis," there is understood *quia* (because), or some other like particle: for the reason is given why there were pains there, as of a woman in labour. Now the breaking of the ships can be understood in two ways: *first,* so that a particle of likeness be understood in this manner: "With a vehement wind thou shalt break them in pieces," as a strong wind breaketh ships; *secondly,* so that the sense be: "With a vehement wind thou shalt break in pieces the ships of Tharsis," that is, thou shalt scatter not only the land forces of the adversaries, but also their fleets set in array. For as the impious assault the Church by land and by sea, so also God shall fight for the Church by land and by sea.

[Margin: What is to be thought of the word Tharsis?]

Of the word *Tharsis,* which the Greek and the Latin Interpreter left as they found it in the Hebrew text, there is a great question, some holding that it signifieth Cilicia, others Carthage, others India, others the sea itself. But this last is improbable, since Jonas went up into a ship that he might go to Tharsis, as we read *Jon.* chap. i. But if Tharsis signified the sea, Jonas would already have been in Tharsis when he was in the ship; whither then was he sailing, that he might go to Tharsis? Of the other significations, that seemeth the truer whereby India is denoted for Tharsis, since from Tharsis there was brought to Solomon every third year a great abundance of gold, and silver, and ivory, as is said III *Kings* x and II *Par.* ix. But from Cilicia or Carthage metals of that kind are not brought, neither is there need of three years to sail from Jerusalem to Cilicia or Carthage. But in this place great ships are called *the ships of Tharsis,* such as those were wont to be in which men sailed to Tharsis, that is, to India.

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. AS WE HAVE HEARD, SO HAVE WE SEEN, IN THE CITY OF THE LORD OF HOSTS, IN THE CITY OF OUR GOD: GOD HATH FOUNDED IT FOR EVER.**

They are the words of the children of the Church, who rejoice that they now learn by experience, concerning the stability of the Church, that which before they had heard promised. And we especially can say these things; for we have heard that Christ said: "Upon this rock I will build my church, and the gates of hell shall not prevail against it," *Matt.* xvi; and now after sixteen hundred years, after so many and so grievous persecutions of pagans and heretics, we see that the Church could not fail. The Prophet therefore saith in the person of the faithful: "As we have heard" it foretold by the Prophets and Apostles, "so have we seen" it fulfilled in the Church, which is "the city of the Lord of hosts," to wit, of him for whom all created things do warfare, and who is therefore called "the Lord of hosts" (*Dominus virtutum*), that is, of armies, who is "our God." And that which we have heard and seen is, that "God hath founded it for ever," so that there is no danger lest it be ever overthrown. And from this place it is gathered openly enough that in this Psalm the city of God is not taken for the earthly Jerusalem, as some of the more recent writers will have it: for that was long ago overthrown by the emperor Titus, and of that it was foretold by the Lord: "They shall not leave in thee a stone upon a stone," *Luke* chap. xix.

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. WE HAVE RECEIVED THY MERCY, O GOD, IN THE MIDST OF THY TEMPLE.**

[Margin: What great works in the Church are to be ascribed to God?]

By these words the people of God confesseth that the great works which God hath done and doth in the Church are to be ascribed not to their own strength or merits, but to the mercy of God alone; therefore he subjoineth: "We have received thy mercy, O God, in the midst of thy temple," that is, this stability of the Church, and other innumerable good things, which as we have heard them promised, so now we see them fulfilled, we have received from thy hand, we have not ourselves procured them for ourselves. We confess therefore "in the midst of thy temple," that is, publicly and before all, that we have received "thy mercy;" to it we ascribe all good things. In the Hebrew there is the verb דמינו *diminu,* which signifieth not so much *suscipere* (to receive) as *æstimare* (to esteem); wherefore St. Jerome rendereth: *Æstimavimus misericordiam tuam in medio templi tui* (We have esteemed thy mercy in the midst of thy temple). But the sense is the same, as St. Chrysostom rightly expoundeth, who weigheth the Hebrew and the Greek word: for that, *suscepimus* (we have received), is to be referred to the knowledge and esteem of the benefit, as we have said. St. Augustine readeth *in medio populi tui* (in the midst of thy people); he had a Greek copy faulty by reason of the likeness of the letters, since he read λαοῦ, *populi* (of the people), for ναοῦ, *templi* (of the temple). In the Hebrew there is no likeness of letters from which the error could have flowed.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. ACCORDING TO THY NAME, O GOD, SO ALSO IS THY PRAISE

<!-- p. 316 (PDF 327) -->

UNTO THE ENDS OF THE EARTH: THY RIGHT HAND IS FULL OF JUSTICE.**

The Prophet goeth on in the person of the faithful to praise God for the benefits received, and as in the beginning he had said: "Great is the Lord, and exceedingly to be praised," so now he saith: "According to thy name, so also is thy praise unto the ends of the earth," that is, according to the greatness of thy name is also the greatness of thy praise. For as the name of God became known in the whole earth for the great works which he wrought in founding and protecting the Church: so also God is praised through the whole world, even to the uttermost bounds of the earth. He addeth also the matter of the praise, saying: "Thy right hand is full of justice." For therefore is God, most magnificent, everywhere praised by the proclamation of all nations, because he most justly defendeth the godly, and punisheth the impious. Now "the right hand" of God is said to be "full of justice," not because it is not also full of mercy, but because injustice hath no place with him. "For the Lord is just in all his works,"[^11] as is said *Ps.* cxliv.

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. LET MOUNT SION REJOICE, AND THE DAUGHTERS OF JUDA BE GLAD; BECAUSE OF THY JUDGMENTS, O LORD.**

From this, that the right hand of the Lord is full of justice, the Prophet exhorteth the people of God to exult, because that people above the rest knoweth by experience the justice of the Lord: "Let mount Sion," saith he, "rejoice," that is, the people of God, or the Church of God, which is signified by that mountain, "and the daughters of Juda be glad," that is, let not only the men, but the women also among the people of God rejoice, "because of thy judgments, O Lord," to wit, seeing the justice which thou hast exercised in protecting the faithful and destroying the unbelievers. Perhaps also by mount Sion are understood the perfect, the strong; by the daughters of Juda, the imperfect and weak. Or by mount Sion is understood the citadel and head of religion, such as Jerusalem[^12] was among the Jews; by the daughters of Juda are understood the remaining members of the Church, such as were the towns subject to Jerusalem: for often in the Scriptures, and especially in the book of *Josue,* by daughters are understood the strongholds, or towns, subject to the greater cities.

[Margin: Verse 11.]

**11. SURROUND SION, AND ENCOMPASS HER: TELL YE IN HER TOWERS.**

[Margin: Verse 12.]

**12. SET YOUR HEARTS ON HER STRENGTH; AND DISTRIBUTE HER HOUSES, THAT YE MAY RELATE IT IN ANOTHER GENERATION.**

[Margin: Wherein doth the spiritual city differ from a material city?]

At the end of the Psalm he exhorteth to the building and fortifying of the holy city, of which he had said: "With the joy of the whole earth is mount Sion founded, the city of the great King." For this city is not like a material city, which is founded and built once; but even to the end of the world it is to be founded, and built, and fortified out of living stones, which are daily to be set in, until the perfect city be dedicated at the last day. For so the Apostle speaketh, *Ephes.* ii: "In whom you also are built together into an habitation of God in the Holy Spirit."[^13] And chap. iv: "And he gave some apostles, and some prophets, and other some evangelists, and other some pastors and doctors, for the perfecting of the saints, for the work of the ministry, for the edifying of the body of Christ." And St. Peter, I *Pet.* ii: "Unto whom coming, as to a living stone, rejected indeed by men, but chosen and made honourable by God: be you also as living stones built up, spiritual houses."[^14] He saith therefore: "Surround Sion, and encompass her," that is, gird the holy city with walls, where haply they are wanting. Now the walls of the city are said to be perfect men, who defend the rest of the people from their enemies. "Tell ye in her towers," that is, preach publicly from a high place: for (as we said above) the preaching of the Gospel is the instrument whereby the city is founded and built. "Set your hearts on her strength," that is, think earnestly of the fortification and strength of the city, lest it lie open to the incursions of enemies. "And distribute her houses," that is, after the walls are founded and built, let the houses be founded and built; and since for this work many are required, distribute to divers workmen their parts, that they may build houses, and so the city may by little and little be filled and grow: and so it shall come to pass "that ye may relate it in another generation," that is, that, the people of God being multiplied by you, ye may hand on to posterity the knowledge of God.

[Margin: Verse 13.]

**13. FOR THIS IS GOD, OUR GOD UNTO ETERNITY, AND FOR EVER AND EVER: HE SHALL RULE US FOR EVERMORE.**

This is that which is to be related to posterity, to wit, that "this God," who did wonderful things in the holy city Jerusalem, is himself "our God unto eternity," neither shall we ever depart from him, nor shall he depart from us; we shall be his people for ever, and he "our God unto eternity," and he "shall rule us for evermore." St. Jerome rendereth these last words from the Hebrew: *Ipse reget nos in morte* (He shall rule us in death). But it is easy to see whence the variety of reading arose: for the Hebrew word, if it be read *holamoth,* signifieth *in sæcula* (for evermore), and so the Seventy Interpreters read; if it be read with other points *halmuth,* it signifieth *ad mortem* (unto death), and so St. Jerome read, and the Rabbins read to-day. But both senses are true, and come to the same: for if God rule us even unto death, he will without doubt rule us for evermore: for after death we shall not be able to fall away from him. They who expound this Psalm of the earthly Jerusalem labour greatly in the explanation of verses 11 and 12. But they who expound it to the letter of the Church suffer no straits.

[^7]: Translator's note: the words within the quotation marks are partly Bellarmine's own; the Vulgate, *Acts* iv, 32, has *Multitudinis autem credentium erat cor unum et anima una* (And the multitude of believers had but one heart and one soul).

[^8]: Translator's note: Bellarmine's text has *parturiens peperit filium*; the Vulgate, *John* xvi, 21, has *cum autem pepererit puerum* (but when she hath brought forth the child).

[^9]: Translator's note: the Latin *spiritus* is both "wind" and "spirit"; the verse has *in spiritu vehementi,* and Bellarmine here takes it of the Holy Spirit. In the words that follow he has *confringit naves Tharsis* (breaketh the ships of Tharsis) for the verse's *conteres naves Tharsis.*

[^10]: Translator's note: the print has *hominem justificantem* (the man justifying); the sense points to *justificatum* (justified), which is translated.

[^11]: Translator's note: Bellarmine's text has *Justus enim Dominus est in omnibus operibus suis*; the Vulgate, *Ps.* cxliv, 17, has *Justus Dominus in omnibus viis suis, et sanctus in omnibus operibus suis* (The Lord is just in all his ways: and holy in all his works).

[^12]: Translator's note: the print has *Jurusalem.*

[^13]: Translator's note: Bellarmine's text has *in Spiritu Sancto*; the Vulgate, *Ephes.* ii, 22, has *in Spiritu* (in the Spirit).

[^14]: Translator's note: the print has *domus spirituales* (spiritual houses); the Vulgate, I *Pet.* ii, 5, has *domus spiritualis* (a spiritual house).
