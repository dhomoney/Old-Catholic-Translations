BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XLI.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*Unto the end, understanding for the sons of Core.*

[Margin: An admonition to be committed to memory by the singers of the Church.]

The title admonisheth the sons of Core, to whom the Psalm was given to be sung, that they understand what they sing, that they may make the hearers also to understand: which admonition is to be committed to memory by the singers of the Church: for singing in the Church ought to serve the spirit, and not the delight of the ears alone. And as they who sing with understanding and devotion carry away the minds of the hearers unto God, so they who bring theatrical measures into the Church make of the house of God a stage of the world. See St. Jerome on chap. v *to the Ephesians*. Some will have the argument of the Psalm to be, that David, an exile in the time of Saul, complaineth that he cannot go up three times in the year to Jerusalem to the feasts of the Lord; but in the time of Saul the tabernacle of the Lord was not in Jerusalem, but in Silo, and to that place the Israelites went three times in the year. Moreover so great a desire as is expressed in this place seemeth not possible to be referred to a tabernacle made with hands and a wooden ark. Wherefore we think the argument of the Psalm to be, as the holy Fathers Jerome, Augustine and Chrysostom teach, that David,

<!-- p. 273 (PDF 284) -->

inflamed with divine love, endeavoureth to express the vehement desire by which he was held of attaining to the vision of God, which is the true and everlasting felicity, while yet he lived amid the temptations and dangers of his pilgrimage. But while he expresseth his own desire, he teacheth at the same time what are the proofs whereby the true love of God may be known.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. AS THE HART PANTETH AFTER THE FOUNTAINS OF WATER; SO MY SOUL PANTETH AFTER THEE, O GOD.**[^1]

[Margin: Love is of a fiery nature.]

[Margin: Four qualities of the hart.]

[Margin: Of what kind is the love of God?]

Love is a thing of a fiery nature, and thereby it cannot be kept within, but breaketh forth by words and sighs. David therefore, that he may in some manner express his love, compareth himself to a thirsty hart, saying: "As the hart panteth after the fountains of water," namely to come to them, "so my soul panteth *to come* unto thee, O God;" which is a most fitting similitude; for the hart hath four qualities: first, he is most hostile to serpents, and wageth perpetual war with them; next, when he is sought by the hunters, he fleeth with the swiftest course to the highest mountains; then that commandment of the Apostle, *Gal.* vi: "Bear ye one another's burdens," he observeth by a certain instinct of nature: for, as St. Augustine writeth, both in this place and in sermon 21 *Of the Words of the Apostle*, harts are said, either when they walk in their herd, or when by swimming they seek other parts of the earth, to lay the burdens of their heads one upon another; lastly, if at any time he is wearied, either from the fight with serpents, or from the flight to the mountains, or from the carrying of the burden in walking or in swimming, he is wont most ardently to thirst for waters, and from seeking them he is neither held back by any pleasantness of meadows, nor frighted by any difficulty of ways. Such altogether is the lover of God; for he both wageth war perpetually with the serpents of carnal desires; and when he is pressed by temptations, or persecutions, he fleeth to the mountain of contemplation, and patiently beareth the troubles of the weak; but chiefly he thirsteth after God with the utmost desire, and is hindered by no earthly felicity, or adversity, from always panting after God. Such was David, although a soldier; such was Paul, such Peter, such the other apostles and the martyrs, such all who, acknowledging their exile, whether walking through prosperity or through adversity, never slackened their course towards their country.

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. MY SOUL HATH THIRSTED AFTER THE STRONG LIVING GOD; WHEN SHALL I COME AND APPEAR BEFORE THE FACE OF GOD?**

[Margin: Three things win love, which are eminent in God.]

He declareth more openly what it is to desire God, and at the same time openeth why God is so greatly to be desired. There are three things, as Chrysostom hath noted, which are wont to kindle love, and thereby thirst and desire: beauty, benefits and love; for both beautiful things in a manner compel themselves to be loved, and they who bestow benefits provoke men to love them, and they who love deserve to be loved in return. But if these three meet in one, that is, if some person most beautiful and most noble load thee with the greatest and with daily benefits, and the same make very much of thee, and love thee most ardently, how shall he not kindle in thy breast a most vehement flame of love? That these three, therefore, meet in God David teacheth in this place, and therefore he saith that he thirsteth after God, that is, that he burneth with the love and desire of him. "My soul, saith he, hath thirsted after God," that is, after a thing most beautiful, most noble, most excellent, which alone embraceth all good things in itself. *Strong*, that is, after a thing not fleeting and perishable, but stable, permanent, eternal. *Living*, that is, after a thing working, understanding, loving, which loadeth us with continual benefits, which maketh much of us, and which loveth us with incredible love. This thirst, therefore, after a thing so good, so beneficent, so loving of me, compelleth me to say with my whole heart: "When shall I come and appear before the face of God?" that is, when will be the end of the pilgrimage; when the beginning of the fruition? From the Hebrew it might be turned more clearly: *Quando veniam et videbo faciem Dei?* (When shall I come and see the face of God?) for that alone it is which can fill the thirst of the rational soul.

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. MY TEARS HAVE BEEN MY BREAD DAY AND NIGHT, WHILST IT IS SAID TO ME DAILY: WHERE IS THY GOD?**

He who truly and inwardly thinketh on the three things which we have said, namely, that God is beautiful above all beauty, and that all things which we have flow from him as from a fountain, and that he himself esteemeth us so highly, and so loveth us, that he gave his only-begotten Son for us: it cannot be but that he be overwhelmed with tears of desire, while the vision of so great a good is deferred. David therefore, who earnestly contemplated these things, rightly addeth: "My tears have been my bread day and night," that is, I was sustained by tears, as by bread, continually through days and nights, that is, the whole time of this pilgrimage, or through the days of prosperity and the nights of adversity; for my soul refused to be comforted by earthly felicity, and no less refused to be made sorrowful by earthly adversity; but at both times my tears have been to me meat and drink, "whilst it is said to me daily" by the wicked and unbelieving: "Where is thy God?" after whom thou thirstest? to whom thou sighest? why lovest thou him whom thou seest not? But it might also be expounded thus, *whilst it is said to me daily, where is thy God,*" that is, whilst daily I go round about surveying all things, seeking him whom my soul loveth, and it is said to me by my spirit, by my thought: "Where is thy God?" for all these things which thou hast surveyed in contemplation are the works of God, all beautiful indeed, but not as thy God. Where then is thy God? where wilt thou seek, that thou mayest find? When wilt thou come, and see the face of thy God?

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. THESE THINGS I REMEMBERED, AND POURED OUT MY SOUL IN ME: FOR I SHALL GO OVER INTO THE PLACE OF THE WONDERFUL TABERNACLE, EVEN TO THE HOUSE OF GOD.**

[Margin: The soul of man a ladder by which God is reached.]

[Margin: In what things the soul maketh us like to God.]

[Margin: The soul hath free will.]

[Margin: The image of the most holy Trinity in the soul.]

He goeth on in explaining his desire: "These things, saith he, I remembered," which have been said of the seeking of God, "and poured out my soul in me." This pouring out can be taken in three ways. *First*, so that the sense be: "I poured out my soul in me," that is, I emptied my soul within me of all earthly delight, that, being about to enter the wonderful tabernacle and the very house of God, I might fill it with the delights of my Lord. *Secondly*, so that the sense be: "I poured out my soul in me," that is, I enlarged my soul, I stretched forth my desire, that it might be capable of so great a good as it will find in that wonderful tabernacle, where is the unfailing plenty of the house of the Lord. *Thirdly*, so that the sense be: "I poured out my soul in me," that is, I poured out my soul above me, I passed beyond the soul itself in contemplation, as it is said, *Lam.* iii, 28: "He shall sit solitary, and hold his peace: because he shall lift himself above himself."[^2] For truly there is in this pilgrimage no ladder more fit for reaching in some manner the wonderful tabernacle and the very house where God is seen, than our soul, which is the image of God: for it is higher than heaven and deeper than the abyss; and he who knoweth how to contemplate his own soul, and then hath learned to pass beyond it, and through it to ascend to the exemplar, he shall go over to the place of the wonderful tabernacle, even to the house of God. And, that we may briefly touch upon this ascent, the soul is a spirit, and thereby it far surpasseth all bodies: so therefore God also is a spirit, and the creator of spirits and of bodies; and so he far surpasseth, not only bodies, but also spirits. Next, whereas the soul is simple and indivisible, yet it is in the whole body, and whole in every part, filling all the members, and occupying no place: so therefore God also is one only, and most simple, and undivided, and yet he filleth the whole world and all created things, everywhere whole, everywhere present, nowhere enclosed. Thirdly, the soul is not moved in the body, and it beareth, moveth, ruleth, quickeneth and giveth sense to the whole body, which is known in death: for when the soul departeth, the body forthwith falleth, and loseth in a moment motion, sense, beauty and whatever good it had: now what the soul is to the body, that God is to the universe of things; not that God is properly the soul of the world, as certain philosophers seem to have thought, but that he seemeth to have a likeness of the soul: for he, remaining immoveable in himself, "upholdeth all things by the word" of his power, "and in him we live, and move, and are." Fourthly, the soul is understanding, and is indeed wholly intellect, containing eminently the knowledge of all the senses, and it knoweth many things besides, to which no sense of the body reacheth: so God is wholly intellect, containing eminently the knowledge of all men and angels, and infinite things besides, which surpass our understanding. Fifthly, the soul understandeth many things not only speculatively, but also practically, and thence arise the works of human wit, plainly wonderful, in divers arts, in buildings, gardens, fountains and very many other things of that kind, so that often the works of human art seem to contend with the works of nature: so God also understandeth not only speculatively, but also practically, and without instruments, without labour, and without space of time, by the sole beck and command of his will he made all things. Sixthly, the soul hath free will, and thence it moveth the members of the body at its beck: so God most freely hath dominion over all created things; whence David saith, *Ps.* cxviii: "All things serve thee." Nor is the soul the image of God only as to essence, but also (though more obscurely) as to the trinity of persons:[^3] for in the soul there is the understanding mind, as it were the Father; there is the knowledge produced, as it were the Word of the Father; there is also the love produced by the mind through the knowledge, as it were the Spirit. There is also in the soul the fruitfulness of memory, there is the understanding and the will, which in some sort represent the three divine persons. The soul therefore is poured out above itself, and passeth beyond itself in contemplation, that it may be able to go over to the wonderful tabernacle; therefore the Prophet addeth: "For I shall go over into the place of the wonderful tabernacle, even to the house of God." By the place of the wonderful tabernacle we understand the heavenly Jerusalem, which is a tabernacle not made with hands, eternal in heaven, where is the house of God, of which he said in *Ps.* xxvi: "One thing I have asked of the Lord, this will I seek after; that I may dwell in the house of the Lord all the days of my life." St. Jerome turneth from the Hebrew *tacebo* (I shall be silent), for that which we have, *admirabilis* (wonderful). But the Seventy Interpreters did not read אדדם, as it is now in the Hebrew copies, which signifieth *tacebo* (I shall be silent), but they read אדרת, which signifieth *admirabilis* (wonderful), and the difference is only in two like letters, as can be understood from the shape of the characters. Now it was easy for the copyists to change two letters into others like them, and it is to be believed that the Seventy Interpreters had better Hebrew copies than we have. Otherwise they could not have turned *admirabilis* (wonderful) for *silebo* (I shall keep silence), unless they had been most unskilful.

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. WITH THE VOICE OF JOY AND PRAISE; THE NOISE OF ONE FEASTING.**

The Prophet declareth that in that ecstasy of his, in which he poured out his soul above himself, and in contemplation went over to the place of the wonderful tabernacle, even to the house of God, he did not go over silent, but exulting and praising with wonder. "With the voice, saith he, of joy and praise," add: I shall go over into the place of the wonderful tabernacle, that is, I shall go over wondering at the glory of that tabernacle, and out of wonder I shall break forth into the voice of joy and praise, namely the praise of the maker of that tabernacle; which voice will be the noise of one feasting, that is, a glad and festal voice, such as are

<!-- p. 275 (PDF 286) -->

heard at solemn and festal and nuptial banquets. For neither can the mind of him that contemplateth be lifted up to consider the glory of the heavenly Jerusalem without the greatest wonder and joy. As to the words, that *sonus* (the noise) is in the Greek of the genitive case; and St. Augustine readeth *soni* (of the noise), whence it may be that our Interpreter put *sonus* for *sonitus*, of the genitive case; but it may also be that he meant to signify that the voice of joy and praise was *the noise of one feasting*. In the Hebrew, for *sono* (noise), there is המון *hamon*, which St. Jerome turneth *multitudinis* (of a multitude); but that Hebrew word signifieth both, *noise* and *multitude*. But that, *epulantis* (of one feasting), both in the Hebrew text, חוגג *chogeg*, and in the Greek, ἑορτάζοντος, is *festa celebrantis* (of one keeping a feast), and so also Augustine and Euthymius read. But the Latin Interpreter seemeth to have meant to declare of what kind that feast was, and to have said "of one feasting," that is, the feast of a solemn banquet, such as is a nuptial one, at which many instruments of music and songs of love are used; and *vox epulantis* (the voice of one feasting) is found in the Commentary of St. Jerome on this place, lest any one should think that it hath lately crept into the Vulgate edition.

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. WHY ART THOU SAD, O MY SOUL? AND WHY DOST THOU TROUBLE ME?**

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. HOPE IN GOD, FOR I WILL STILL GIVE PRAISE TO HIM: THE SALVATION OF MY COUNTENANCE, AND MY GOD.**

Out of that lifting up of the mind and spiritual joy he comforteth his tears, saying: "Why art thou sad, O my soul?" that is, why are thy tears thy bread day and night, "whilst it is said to thee: Where is thy God? why" with such unceasing weeping "dost thou trouble me? Hope in God," that is, although thou seest not God, whom thou ardently desirest to see, yet hope in him, "for I will still give praise to him," that is, because the time is not yet past, there will yet be a time when, being present, I shall praise God, and shall confess his mercies, and shall say to him: "The salvation of my countenance and my God," that is, thou art the salvation of my countenance, while thou enlightenest my countenance with thy brightness, and makest my face to see thy face, and me to know even as I am known, and out of clear knowledge to say: "Thou art my God." In the Hebrew there is, as St. Jerome also turneth it: *Salutes vultus ejus, Deus meus ad me ipsum* (The salvations of his countenance, my God within myself), etc.; but the Hebrew text is manifestly corrupted in this place, and one letter, namely *vau*, not set in its own place, hath bred all this confusion. Which is manifest from the repetition of these words, which are found in the end of this Psalm, and in the end of the following Psalm. Therefore it is not to be read פניו אלהי, as it is read here, but פני ואלהי, as it is read in the repetition; and the true Hebrew reading to the word is this, *salutes vultus mei, et Deus meus* (the salvations of my countenance, and my God). Now he saith *salutes* (salvations) in the plural number, not *salus* or *salutare* (salvation), because, although our salvation is one, yet it containeth many good things, which may also be called *salvations*.

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. MY SOUL IS TROUBLED WITHIN MYSELF: THEREFORE WILL I REMEMBER THEE FROM THE LAND OF JORDAN AND HERMONIIM, FROM THE LITTLE HILL.**

[Margin: It is doubted what mountain Hermoniim is.]

He expoundeth the changes of his sorrow and of his consolation: of sorrow, from the fear and dangers of this life; of consolation, from the hope and promise of the life to come. He saith therefore: "My soul is troubled within myself," that is, although I have said to my soul: "Hope in God," yet my soul, looking back upon myself, upon my weakness, upon the little light and little strength which are in me, is shaken with great fear, and with that terror troubleth me. "Therefore will I remember thee from the land of Jordan and Hermoniim, from the little hill," that is, therefore, that I may apply a remedy to this terror and trouble, I will remove my eyes from myself, and turn them to thee; and from this Jordan, which I see in this land, "I will remember thee," that is, I will remember the river which maketh thy city joyful, and the torrent of thy pleasure, of which they are made to drink who are with thee; and from this little hill, which is called *Hermoniim*, which I have before my eyes, "I will remember thy holy mountain," in which thou dwellest with thy holy angels, and from this remembrance I will comfort my soul and my desire. What mountain Hermoniim properly is, and whether it be different from mount Hermon, is not altogether clear: yet it is likely that they are different mountains, and situate in different places. For both St. Jerome in the *Life of St. Paula* distinguisheth these two mountains, and it is certain that mount Hermon is a very great mountain, and Hermoniim is said in this place to be little. Perhaps therefore Hermoniim was some mountain in Galilee not far from the Jordan, since David joineth these two together.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. DEEP CALLETH ON DEEP, AT THE NOISE OF THY FLOOD-GATES.**

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. ALL THY HEIGHTS AND THY BILLOWS HAVE PASSED OVER ME.**

He goeth on to relate the temptations and afflictions of this life, and useth the metaphor of an inundation of waters, alluding to the great flood which was in the time of Noe. "Deep, saith he, calleth on deep," that is, the greatest inundation and depth of waters came upon me, and as one passed another came upon it, as if called by the former; and these great inundations came "at the noise of thy flood-gates," that is, at the din and sound of the opening of the flood-gates of heaven, whence the inundation of waters descended. "And all thy heights," that is, all the highest floods, and "all thy billows have passed over me," that is, all that inundation, and the whole flood came upon me. He alludeth, as I have said, to the general flood, in which "the flood gates of heaven were opened," that is, so vast a rain descended, as if certain flood-gates had been opened in heaven, and thence all that water had descended with great force: and from that inundation there was made a

<!-- p. 276 (PDF 287) -->

deep, that is, the greatest height and depth of waters. But this metaphor signifieth nothing else but the vast temptations and dangers with which divine Providence suffereth his elect to be exercised. Now the greatness of these dangers only spiritual men understand, such as David was, who know the manifold snares of the devil, and are aware how much it importeth to fall from the grace of God: and this seemeth to be the literal exposition, although the godly expositions of some are not to be rejected, who will have *deep calleth on deep* to signify: The deep of misery calleth on the deep of the divine mercy. Likewise, the deep of human malice calleth on the deep of the divine justice. Likewise, the deep of the secrets of the Old Testament calleth on the deep of the mysteries of the New Testament. Likewise, the deep of the human heart, which is unsearchable, calleth on the deep of the knowledge of God, who searcheth the reins and hearts. This only is to be added, that by the word *invocat* (calleth on) is not signified *implorat* (imploreth), but *vocat* (calleth), as is gathered from the Hebrew word: for the sense is, *abyssus abyssum vocat* (deep calleth deep).

[Margin: Verse 11.]

**11. IN THE DAYTIME THE LORD HATH COMMANDED HIS MERCY; AND A CANTICLE TO HIM IN THE NIGHT.**

He returneth to the change of consolation, after relating, and in a wonderful manner amplifying, the greatness of the temptation: "In the daytime, saith he, the Lord hath commanded his mercy," that is, after the inundations of waters and those dreadful deeps have passed, in the day of prosperity "the Lord hath commanded," that is, hath sent, "mercy," comforting and visiting. "And a canticle to him in the night," that is, but in the night time, namely in the time of temptation and tribulation, the canticle to him shall not cease, that is, the prayer and thanksgiving offered to him. Which is more clearly explained in the following verse.

[Margin: Verse 12.]

**12. WITH ME IS PRAYER TO THE GOD OF MY LIFE. I WILL SAY TO GOD: THOU ART MY SUPPORT.**

This namely is the canticle of the night, a prayer that is secret, and of the heart rather than of the mouth. "With me, saith he, is prayer to the God of my life," that is, in my bosom is continual prayer to God, the author of my life; to whom with faith and confidence I will say: "Thou art my support," that is, in thee I trust, to thee I flee, thou wilt take me up when I am falling, thou wilt raise me when I am fallen.

[Margin: Verse 13.]

**13. WHY HAST THOU FORGOTTEN ME? AND WHY GO I MOURNING, WHILST MY ENEMY AFFLICTETH ME?**

He wondereth at the changes of divine providence in governing his servants, as if he should say: Since thou, O God, art truly my support, "why hast thou forgotten me?" that is, whence cometh it to pass that temptations and tribulations so overwhelm me, that thou seemest to have forsaken me, whereas thou art my hope and my strength? Whence, I say, cometh it to pass "that I go mourning, whilst my enemy afflicteth me," whereas thou art my protector and my helper?

[Margin: Verse 14.]

**14. WHILST MY BONES ARE BROKEN, MY ENEMIES WHO TROUBLE ME HAVE REPROACHED ME;**

[Margin: Verse 15.]

**15. WHILST THEY SAY TO ME DAY BY DAY: WHERE IS THY GOD? WHY ART THOU CAST DOWN, O MY SOUL? AND WHY DOST THOU DISQUIET ME?**[^4]

[Margin: Verse 16.]

**16. HOPE THOU IN GOD, FOR I WILL STILL GIVE PRAISE TO HIM: THE SALVATION OF MY COUNTENANCE, AND MY GOD.**

Not only, saith he, doth my enemy afflict me, while thou seest and lookest on, who art my support; but also "whilst my bones are broken," that is, whilst I come to the utmost feebleness and weakness, so that I can now scarcely bear temptations, "my enemies who trouble me have reproached me," whilst they say to me continually: "Where is thy God?" that is, those very men who persecute and trouble me have reproached me with the confidence which I have in thee, as if I had hoped in thee in vain: for they say: "Where is thy God," whom thou didst boast to be thy helper and protector? So we read that it was cast in the teeth of Tobias: "Where is thy hope, for which thou gavest alms, and buriedst the dead?" and below: "It is evident thy hope is come to nothing, and thy alms now appear," *Tob.* ii. So also we read that Christ was reproached, *Matt.* xxvii: "He trusted in God; let him deliver him if he will have him." So therefore his unbelieving enemies reproached David also, when he was set in tribulation; yet he, although troubled and made sorrowful for an hour, reproved himself and said: "Why art thou cast down, O my soul? and why dost thou disquiet me? Hope thou in God, for I will still give praise to him: the salvation of my countenance, and my God;" which words are explained in the exposition of verses 6 and 7.
