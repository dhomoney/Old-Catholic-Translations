BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XLIV.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*Unto the end, for them that shall be changed, for the sons of Core, for understanding. A canticle for the Beloved.*

[Margin: Why are the faithful called they that are changed?]

With the greatest agreement all the expositors, Latin, Greek and Hebrew, teach that this Psalm containeth the praises of the Messias and of his Spouse the Church, so that it is as it were a spiritual marriage song; and to this argument the title answereth aptly enough: for by *them that shall be changed* the Prophet understandeth the faithful peoples, who make the Church; and they are said to be about to be changed, because they were to be changed by justification in this world, and by the resurrection in the other; for they who adored idols were to adore the true God; and they who led a wretched and mortal life on earth were to lead a happy and everlasting one in heaven. By *the Beloved* all understand Christ, of whom the heavenly Father saith more than once, *Matt.* iii and vii:[^1] "This is my beloved Son," and whom no man knoweth who doth not most ardently love him, since he is all sweet and beautiful, as being "the unspotted mirror, and the brightness of eternal light," as is read in *Wis.* vii. The sense therefore of the title will be this: A canticle given to the sons of Core, to be sung with understanding and wisely unto the end, the matter whereof is for them that shall be changed, and for the Beloved, that is, for the Church and for Christ, for the Spouse and for the Bridegroom. In the Hebrew is read *pro liliis* (for the lilies), where we have *pro iis qui commutabuntur* (for them that shall be changed); but the Hebrew word ששנים can signify both, namely the faithful as *lilies,* and *they that shall be changed,* as St. Jerome confesseth in the Epistle *to Principia the virgin,* in which he diligently explaineth this whole Psalm: and fitly enough they who are to be changed can be called lilies, since of the Church, which is the multitude of those to be changed, the Bridegroom saith in *Cant.* chap. ii: "As the lily among thorns, so is my love among the daughters."

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. MY HEART HATH UTTERED A GOOD WORD: I SPEAK MY WORKS TO THE KING;**[^2]

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. MY TONGUE IS THE PEN OF A SCRIVENER THAT WRITETH SWIFTLY.**

[Margin: The difference between prophecies and other divine writings.]

These two verses contain the preface of the whole Psalm, in which preface the Prophet teacheth that this Psalm is pure prophecy dictated by the Holy Ghost, and that nothing here is wrought out by his own wit: for although all divine Scripture is the word of God, and inspired by the Holy Ghost, yet there is a great difference between prophecies, and histories or epistles: for in putting forth prophecies the sacred writers did not labour in thinking, and discoursing, and recalling anything to memory; but simply spoke, or wrote, the things which God showed them: which Baruch testifieth of Jeremias, *Jerem.* xxxvi, namely that "he spoke so, as if he were reading in some book that which he said." But when the sacred writers wrote histories, or epistles, God indeed inspired into them the will to write, and directed them, that they might write rightly and without any error, yet so that they themselves also exercised memory,

<!-- p. 292 (PDF 303) -->

[Margin: Why is this prophecy called a good word?]

[Margin: The generation of the eternal Word is here shadowed forth.]

and wit, that they might remember the things done, and think upon the manner and order of writing: which the author of the second book of Machabees testifieth of himself, chap. ii. David therefore, when in the Psalms he sang the histories of the benefits of God, or of his own calamities, or also of the whole people's, exercised memory and wit, and composed the Psalms not without labour of his own, although he was always stirred up and directed by God: but when he sang, or wrote, pure prophecies, as he did in this Psalm, he acknowledged nothing as his own, save the simple ministry of the tongue, or of the hand. This therefore is what he saith in this preface, which he himself explained more clearly in II *Kings,* chap. xxiii, when he saith: "The spirit of the Lord hath spoken by me, and his word by my tongue." He saith therefore: "My heart hath uttered (*eructavit,* hath belched forth) a good word," that is, my mind out of the fulness and abundance of divine enlightening and of heavenly revelations hath brought forth abroad to the ears of men this Psalm, which containeth a good word, namely a speech pleasing and wholesome to all. But for a fuller explanation some things are to be noted. *First,* that the Prophet used the word *eructare* (to belch forth), that he might show that he speaketh in this Psalm not by his own will, and his own invention: for a belch is not sent forth voluntarily, and by choice, but, whether a man will or no, it breaketh out from the fulness of the stomach. *Secondly,* that the Prophet would also show that he hath not expressed in this Psalm all that, God enlightening him, he knew; but hath brought forth some small thing out of that fulness: for a belch is a sign of repletion, but it is a slight breath breaking forth abroad from that repletion. For the Prophets see many things which it is not granted to man to utter. Whence Isaias also said: "My secret to myself," *Isa.* xxiv; and they who receive revelations from God are wont for the most part to confess this, that they have not words wherewith to explain those revelations so as they themselves see them: and perhaps for this cause the Prophet said that "his heart had uttered a good word," not good words in the plural number. *Thirdly,* that this Psalm is called a good word, because it containeth not a prophecy of evil things, as of the destruction of the city, of the captivity of the people, and the like, which were wont to be foretold in many places by the Prophets; but the proclaiming of prosperous and happy things, which are to bring great gladness and pleasure. *Fourthly,* that God in these words so described the flowing forth of this word from the heart of David, that he alluded to the production of the eternal Word, and would lead us as it were by the hand to the understanding of the eternal generation of the divine Word: for truly the eternal Father needed not the help of a consort to produce the Son, nor did he produce him by choice, nor did he beget more sons; but out of the fulness of the heart, or out of most perfect understanding, he naturally produced the Word of the mind, and the one only Word, and that supremely good, so that he could properly say: "My heart hath uttered a good word." There followeth: "I speak my works to the king." There are not wanting those who expound these words thus: "I speak my works; to the king," that is, I confess my sins to God; or, I speak these songs of the king; or, I consecrate my work to the king; or, I address the king in this Psalm: which expositions, as I do not blame, so I judge that our explanation is better joined with the words going before and following; for in our opinion this second part of the first verse giveth the reason why David said: "My heart hath uttered a good word;" for it is as if he said: I simply confess to my king, who is God, all my works; and I attribute not to myself what is not mine; therefore I said not: I wrote this Psalm, but: "My heart hath uttered a good word," that is, it came forth not from my choice, but from the fulness of thy enlightening. Which is most clearly explained in the following verse, when it is said: "My tongue is the pen of a scrivener that writeth swiftly," that is, my tongue brought forth this Psalm, but it did so, not as my tongue, not as a member of my body, which is moved at the command of my will; but as the pen of the Holy Ghost, as of a scrivener that writeth swiftly. Now he saith that his tongue is the pen of a scrivener that writeth swiftly, and saith not that his tongue is the tongue of a spirit that speaketh swiftly, because he would show that his tongue in putting forth prophecy is a separate instrument, as in truth a pen is; and not a conjoined instrument, as are the members of the body; or he would signify that this prophecy was not to pass away into the wind, as the things which are pronounced with the mouth; but was to remain for ever, as the things which are written down with the pen. And that, *that writeth swiftly,* signifieth that the Holy Ghost needeth not time to consider what, and how, is to be written; for they write slowly who in the writing think what sentence, and in what words, they ought to write.

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. THOU ART BEAUTIFUL ABOVE THE SONS OF MEN: GRACE IS POURED ABROAD IN THY LIPS; THEREFORE HATH GOD BLESSED THEE FOR EVER.**

He beginneth to praise Christ, and first he praiseth him from beauty, then from eloquence, next from fortitude and might, thirdly from the virtues of the soul, lastly from the divine and royal dignity, and power, to which he addeth also outward things, namely the nobleness of the garments and of the palace. Now he beginneth from beauty, either because he describeth the Bridegroom, and in a Bridegroom beauty is chiefly desired: or because he goeth forward from the lesser to the greater, and beauty yieldeth to eloquence, eloquence to might, might to the virtues, the virtues to the divinity. He saith therefore: "Beautiful in form (*speciosus forma*) above the sons of men." The sentence seemeth abrupt, and obscure, since he hath not added who this beautiful one is; but, as we noted above, therefore was it said: "My heart hath uttered a good word," that is, out of the fulness of contemplation something flowed forth abroad, not all.

<!-- p. 293 (PDF 304) -->

[Margin: Why is Christ called beautiful above the sons of men?]

[Margin: The force and efficacy of the speech of the Word of God.]

The sense therefore is: It is no wonder if thou, O Christ, art called Beloved, "for thou art beautiful in form above the sons of men;" now he saith, "above the sons of men," and addeth not above the angelic spirits, because God the Word was made not an angel, but man, as if he said: Thou, O Beloved, art man, but beautiful in form above men. And truly Christ is fair above men: for if thou regard the divinity, thou wilt see him endued with infinite beauty; if thou regard the spiritual beauty of the soul, Christ is beautiful above all created spirits; if thou regard the splendour of his glorified body, he is more beautiful than the sun, and the sun and moon wonder at his beauty. There followeth: "Grace is poured abroad in thy lips," by which words he praiseth the same Christ from the grace of speech; which in a wonderful manner commendeth beauty itself. Now it is said, "grace poured abroad in the lips," that it may be shown that the grace of speech was abiding and natural to Christ, not gotten by study, or exercise. In the Gospels we read, *Luke* iv: "They wondered at the words of grace that proceeded from his mouth." And *John* vi:[^3] "Never did man speak like this man." St. Peter, St. Andrew, St. James, St. John, St. Philip, but chiefly St. Matthew, had experience of the force of his words, and of the energy lying hid in his speech, who at a simple calling, being suddenly most effectually drawn and persuaded, leaving all things, followed him. But it is more wonderful that the winds, and the sea, and the very fevers and lastly the dead obeyed his voice: although neither ought it to seem wonderful, if the substantial and divine Word, through the flesh assumed, spoke words of grace most effectual and most sweet. There followeth: "Therefore hath God blessed thee for ever." These words can make a twofold sense, and each right: for that *propterea* (therefore) can be taken for *quia* (because), or *propterea quod* (for the reason that), so that the sense is: "Thou art beautiful above the sons of men, and grace is poured abroad in thy lips," for the reason that "God hath blessed thee for ever," so that the eternal blessing of God is the cause of so great beauty and so great grace. Now by blessing in this place is signified the grace of the hypostatic union, which is to endure for ever; for thence as from a fountain flowed all the gifts which were most abundantly poured into the humanity of Christ. But *propterea* can also, as Chrysostom and others expound, be taken simply for a particle signifying the effect, so that the sense is: "*Because* thou art beautiful above the sons of men, and grace is poured abroad in thy lips, therefore hath God blessed thee for ever," that is, because thou art lovely and gracious above all, therefore God hath loved thee above all, and blessed thee for ever. From which words it ought not to be gathered that Christ was not loved, and blessed by the Father, before his beauty and grace of speech appeared, but that these were a merit, or new title of love: in which manner Christ himself saith, *John* x: "Therefore doth the Father love me: because I lay down my life, that I may take it again." And the Apostle, *Philip.* ii: "He humbled himself unto death, even to the death of the cross: for which cause God also hath exalted him, and hath given him a name which is above all names." Yet the former exposition seemeth to be preferred.

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. GIRD THY SWORD UPON THY THIGH, O THOU MOST MIGHTY.**

He passeth from the praise of beauty and eloquence to the praise of warlike fortitude, and after the manner of poets he doth not simply relate, but in the manner of one praying, or inviting, he saith: "Gird thy sword upon thy thigh, O thou most mighty," that is: Come, O Beloved of God, who art not only most beautiful and most gracious, but also most mighty and most strong, gird thyself with thy arms, and come, deliver thy people. And what his arms are, he explaineth in the following verse, saying:

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. WITH THY COMELINESS AND THY BEAUTY SET OUT, PROCEED PROSPEROUSLY, AND REIGN.**

[Margin: The highest and true beauty is spiritual.]

[Margin: Which is the wisest soul?]

Those words: "With thy comeliness and thy beauty," can be joined with the foregoing, so that the sense is: "Gird thy sword, O thou most mighty, with thy comeliness and thy beauty." They can also be joined with the following, so that the sense is: "With thy comeliness and thy beauty set out, proceed prosperously, and reign;" but in either way it is signified that the arms of Christ are his comeliness and beauty. That this may be understood, it is to be considered that the true and highest beauty, as Augustine speaketh, is spiritual beauty, which never fadeth, and which pleaseth the eyes not of men only, but also of the angels, and of God himself, who cannot be deceived. Now spiritual beauty can be known from bodily beauty. For as the beauty of bodies consisteth in the proportion of the members and the sweetness of colour, so spiritual beauty consisteth in justice, which is a certain proportion; and in wisdom, which shineth after the manner of light, or rather, as the Wise Man speaketh, *Wis.* chap. vii: "Being compared with the light, she is found before it." That therefore is the most beautiful soul, which hath justice in the will, wisdom in the understanding. Now these two both render the soul beautiful, and thereby lovely to God, and at the same time are most mighty arms, with which Christ overcame the devil: for Christ fought with the devil, not by his omnipotence, as he could, but by wisdom and justice; by wisdom overcoming craft, and by justice overcoming malice. For the devil by his craft drove the first man to provoke God by disobedience, and to take away honour indeed from God, but from himself, and from the whole human race, the blessed life: and with this craft was joined malice, or envy; for the devil did this because he was tormented with envy, that man seemed one day to be about to ascend to heaven, whence

<!-- p. 294 (PDF 305) -->

[Margin: In what things did Christ overcome the devil?]

he himself had fallen; but the wisdom of Christ overcame the craft, because by his obedience rendered to God in human nature he gave back to God an honour much greater than God had lost by the disobedience of Adam; and at the same time by the same obedience he exalted the human race to a glory much higher than that would have been from which it had fallen: Christ moreover by his charity, which is true and perfect justice, overcame the envy and malice of the devil: because he loved his enemies, and on the very cross prayed for his persecutors, and would suffer and die, that he might reconcile his enemies to God, and of enemies make them his friends and brethren, and coheirs. This therefore is what he now saith: "With thy comeliness and thy beauty," that is, with thy comeliness, which consisteth in the fairness of wisdom and the beauty of justice, girded and furnished as with sword and bow, "set out, proceed prosperously, and reign," that is, direct thy course to the battle against the devil, prosper in the battle, and, the Prince of this world being overcome and subdued, get thee a kingdom, that thou mayest thenceforth reign in the hearts of men by faith and charity. By the word *intende* (set out) St. John Chrysostom expoundeth, *bend the arrows* (*intende sagittas*), which exposition is probable, because a little after it is said, "thy arrows are sharp." This is certain from the original text, that by *intende* cannot rightly be expounded *attende* (attend), or *considera* (consider); but only, *intende sagittas,* or *intende,* that is, *dirige iter* (direct thy way). As to the words, St. Jerome rendereth thus from the Hebrew: *gloria tua et decore tuo prospere ascende* (with thy glory and thy comeliness ascend prosperously). But the Seventy Interpreters took otherwise, or read otherwise, the same Hebrew words, the same letters being kept. For where the Hebrews repeat *decore tuo* (with thy comeliness), because they read והדרך *vahadareca,* the Seventy read *vahadrec,* which signifieth *et intende* (and bend), or *dirige* (direct), from the word דרך *darac.* And the word צלח *tselach,* which St. Jerome took for an adverb, and rendered *prospere* (prosperously), the Seventy took for a verb of the imperative mood, and translated *prospere procede* (proceed prosperously), or *prosperare* (prosper). And truly that word can be taken in either way. Lastly the word רכב *recab,* which St. Jerome rightly translated *ascende* (ascend), the Seventy translated *regna* (reign), because they regarded the sense rather than the word: for by the ascending they interpreted the kingdom. The same Hebrew word can also, and yet more properly, signify *sede in curru* (sit in a chariot), or *in equo* (on a horse), which can be referred to the kingdom. For when kings were inaugurated, they rode the royal horse or the royal mule, as is gathered from the first chapter of the third book of *Kings;* and to sit in a triumphal chariot is proper to kings who have overcome their enemies in battle.

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. BECAUSE OF TRUTH AND MEEKNESS AND JUSTICE: AND THY RIGHT HAND SHALL CONDUCT THEE WONDERFULLY.**

[Margin: Two things are necessary to a king.]

[Margin: Christ is meekly just.]

He explaineth the reason why Christ ought deservedly to reign, namely because he hath the royal virtues: as if he said: "Proceed prosperously, and reign,[^4] because of truth and meekness and justice," that is, because the royal virtues are not wanting to thee. The Hebrew copy explaineth this place more clearly, so that it can be instead of a commentary; for it hath thus: *propter verbum veritatis et mansuetudinem justitiæ* (because of the word of truth and the meekness of justice). From which words we teach[^5] that two things are necessary to a king, that he be truthful, or faithful in his words, and just in his works: which is also said of God the supreme king in *Ps.* cxliv: "God[^6] is faithful in all his words, and holy in all his works." But because the justice of a judge is wont to have harshness annexed, which is as it were a blemish of justice, therefore to the justice of Christ, which is most perfect, meekness is joined. For Christ is meekly just, that is, he judgeth most justly, but without peevishness, without harshness, without arrogance; he doth not frighten away his subjects, but winneth them to himself. Thus therefore the words of our edition are to be explained: "reign because of truth," which thou always keepest in thy words and promises, "and meekness and justice," that is, because of the meekness which thou always minglest with justice. "And thy right hand shall conduct thee wonderfully," that is, and reigning with these virtues, thou shalt see wonderful advances of thy kingdom, nor shalt thou need the help of others; but thy right hand itself, thy strength and thy power, shall of itself suffice to conduct thee wonderfully, and make thee go forward in enlarging the kingdom, until thou put all thy enemies under the footstool of thy feet. In the Hebrew it is read a little otherwise as to the words, but the sense is the same; for thus St. Jerome translateth: *Et docebit te terribilia dextera tua* (And thy right hand shall teach thee terrible things), that is, thy right hand, overcoming and laying low the enemies, shall teach thee, that is, shall show thee, and make thee see, terrible events, namely the slaughters of thy enemies.

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. THY ARROWS ARE SHARP (UNDER THEE SHALL PEOPLE FALL) INTO THE HEARTS OF THE KING'S ENEMIES.**

[Margin: The word of God is a sword and an arrow.]

He explaineth the manner in which the right hand of Christ the king is to conduct him in enlarging the empire, saying: This shall be done, because *the arrows* which thou shalt shoot with thy right hand *are most sharp,* and therefore they shall pierce *the hearts of thy enemies,* and *the people,* the enemies themselves, *shall fall under thee,* and shall be made subject to thy power. By the name of arrows are understood the words of God, or the preaching of the word of God: for this is the general instrument which Christ useth to spread his kingdom; for hence he himself saith in *Ps.* ii: "But I am appointed king by him over Sion his holy mountain, preaching his commandment." Now the word of God is called both a sword, and an arrow, and a hammer, and the like, because it hath a likeness with all these arms. And it is called a sharp arrow, because it hath great efficacy to pierce hearts, and much greater than hath the human eloquence of any great orator whatsoever. "For the word of God, as the Apostle saith, *Heb.* iv, is living and effectual, and more piercing than any two edged sword." And that, *under thee shall people fall,* is to be read by parenthesis; and the people wounded with the arrows are said to fall under this King,

<!-- p. 295 (PDF 306) -->

because they are not wounded that they may be altogether destroyed, but that they may die to sin, and live to justice, and in that manner remain subject to Christ the king, and serve him, whom to serve is to reign. Lastly that, *into the hearts of the king's enemies* (*in corda*), in the Hebrew and Greek copy, and in many Latin manuscripts, is read *in the heart* (*in corde*), not *into the hearts:* but it mattereth little whether *in corde* or *in corda* be read; for it is both true that the arrows of the word of God pierce the hearts of sinners, and true that the same arrows, once fixed in the heart, stick there, and feed and keep the wound of love, and in that manner make of enemies and sinners friends and saints. In the Greek copies is read, *sagittæ tuæ acutæ, potentissime* (thy arrows are sharp, O most mighty): but that *potentissime* is not in the Hebrew. From which we understand that the author of the Latin Vulgate edition had a Greek copy more correct than are those which are now carried about; or at least that he perceived that that word was not put in the text by the Seventy Interpreters, but had crept in from elsewhere.

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. THY THRONE, O GOD, IS FOR EVER AND EVER: THE SCEPTRE OF THY KINGDOM IS A SCEPTRE OF UPRIGHTNESS.**

[Margin: Christ is most openly called God by David.]

He cometh at length to the supreme dignity of the Messias, and most openly calleth him God, and saith that the throne of his kingdom is eternal; which place St. Paul, *Heb.* i, brought to prove that Christ is so much greater than the angels as the lord is greater than the servants, and God than created things. He saith therefore: "Thy throne," O Christ, "O God," shall not be temporal, as was that of David and Solomon; but shall remain "for ever and ever. And the sceptre of thy kingdom" (*virga regni tui,* the rod of thy kingdom), that is, thy sceptre, shall be always most right and most just: for it is a Hebrew phrase, *virga directionis* (the rod of uprightness), for *virga rectissima* (a most right rod); as also *Deus scientiarum* (the God of knowledges), for *Deo scientissimo* (the God most knowing).

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. THOU HAST LOVED JUSTICE, AND HATED INIQUITY: THEREFORE GOD, THY GOD, HATH ANOINTED THEE WITH THE OIL OF GLADNESS ABOVE THY FELLOWS.**

[Margin: Christ is exalted above all angels and men.]

[Margin: Christ was anointed by God the Father in respect of the humanity.]

This place also we can understand in two ways, as that of the third verse, *therefore hath God blessed thee for ever.* For that *therefore* can signify *the effect,* so that the sense is, because "thou hast loved justice, and hated iniquity," by obeying unto death, even the death of the cross, "therefore God hath anointed thee with the oil of gladness," that is, hath glorified thee, "and exalted thee to his right hand, and given thee a name which is above all names, that in thy name every knee should bow, of those that are in heaven, on earth, and under the earth," *Philip.* ii. Which glorification is rightly called "the anointing of gladness," because through it all sorrow received an end. Rightly also is it said "above thy fellows," because although the angels also have been glorified, and men are to be glorified, yet no one hath been exalted, or is to be exalted, to the right hand of the Father, and no one hath received, or is to receive, a name above all names, Christ alone excepted, who is the head of men and angels, and is at once God and man. But again that *therefore* can signify *the cause,* and be put for *because,* or *for the reason that,* so that the sense is: "thou hast loved justice, and hated iniquity," *for the reason that* God hath anointed thee with the oil of spiritual grace above thy fellows, namely because he hath anointed thee with the grace of the hypostatic anointing, and thence it came to pass that in thee the spirit was poured out without measure, but all the rest have received of thy fulness a measure of the spirit; which exposition St. Augustine followeth, and St. Basil seemeth to follow it, who, expounding this place, saith that the love of justice, and the turning away from iniquity, was natural to Christ, because he had it from his nativity, or rather from his conception, and from an inward principle: but that the rest[^7] acquire the virtues by exercise and labour. Now St. Augustine observeth against the Hebrews and the heretics who deny the divinity of Christ, that in that repetition, *Deus, Deus tuus* (God, thy God), the former word is of the vocative case, the latter of the nominative, in which manner also St. Jerome teacheth that this place is to be understood in the Epistle *to Principia.* And so the sense is: God thy Father hath anointed thee, O Christ God, with the oil of gladness; and the same Augustine affirmeth that this most clearly appeareth in the Greek text, wherefore it is to be thought that in the Greek texts which we now have omega hath been changed into omicron by the carelessness of the copyists. Now when God is said to have anointed God, we do not understand God anointed in respect of the divinity, but in respect of the humanity; for God the Father anointed God the Son, because God the Son was made man, and in respect of the humanity received the grace of anointing, yet God the Son himself is said to be anointed, because that man who was anointed is the Son of God, and God.

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. MYRRH AND STACTE AND CASSIA PERFUME THY GARMENTS, FROM THE IVORY HOUSES: OUT OF WHICH THE DAUGHTERS OF KINGS HAVE DELIGHTED THEE IN THY GLORY.**

This verse is most obscure: let us therefore explain first the words, then the meaning. Myrrh is a known juice, bitter, and fragrant; *gutta* (a drop; "stacte" in the verse) is a general name, but in the Greek there is στάκτη, which in Basil, in the explanation of this place, is the drop distilled from myrrh, so that it is as it were the flower of myrrh, that is, the finest part of myrrh. But it seemeth not likely that the Prophet would say: "Myrrh, and the finest drop" of myrrh; in the Hebrew is אהלות *ahaloth,* which in Latin we call *aloe.* Now aloes is a juice likewise bitter and fragrant, but distinct in kind from myrrh; whence in the Gospel we read that for the burial of Christ "Nicodemus" bought "of myrrh and aloes about an hundred pound weight." What therefore our Interpreter called "a drop," we ought to understand to be a drop of aloes, as St. Jerome writeth in the Epistle *to Principia;* or of distilled myrrh, as St. Basil teacheth in the Commentary. Cassia lastly is a shrub,

<!-- p. 296 (PDF 307) -->

[Margin: What odours of virtues did Christ chiefly breathe forth in his passion?]

[Margin: The humanity of Christ can be called both a garment and a house.]

whence a fragrant bark is taken, and it is numbered among the spices, as is cinnamon also. By *the ivory houses* are understood palaces, or sumptuous temples, the walls whereof are covered with ivory; for in the Hebrew there is not בית *baith,* which properly signifieth *a house,* but היכל *hechal,* which signifieth *a palace, a basilica* and *a temple.* The Greek name also, βαρέων ἐλεφαντίνων, signifieth great palaces, as St. Basil saith. And so that is improbable which some will have, that the ivory houses are the cells of the spices, and more improbable that which others will have, that they are the cases, or chests, in which garments, or spices, are kept. But, say they, cases are made of ivory, but not palaces: it is true, but they are not called ivory houses in this place as though they were wholly of ivory, but because they are covered with ivory: in which manner the house of Nero was called the golden house, and the gate of Constantinople the golden gate, because they were gilded, or overlaid with plates of gold, not because they were wholly of gold; and so we understand that of III *Kings* xxii: "The house of ivory which Achab built." And *Amos* iii: "The houses of ivory shall perish." St. Gregory, *Morals,* Book XXXV, chap. xiii, readeth *from the ivory steps* (*a gradibus eburneis*); but that this arose from the ambiguity of the word is gathered from St. Jerome in the Epistle *to Principia.* For he writeth that the Greek word βαρέων is ambiguous, and can signify *steps,* and *palaces,* or *houses,* that thence some of the Latins, deceived by this ambiguity, translated *a gravibus eburneis* (from the heavy ivory things); but because this signifieth nothing, it is credible that it was changed and written *a gradibus eburneis*[^8] (from the ivory steps). By *the daughters of kings* can be understood the multitudes of divers kingdoms. For Scripture in many places calleth the daughter of Jerusalem, the daughter of Babylon, the daughter of the Assyrians, the daughter of Tyre, for the multitude of the people of those cities, or nations. They can also be understood, as the words sound, as the daughters of princes, by whom are figuratively signified lofty souls, as St. Basil saith; for this whole speech is figurative. These things being expounded, that we may come to the sense, by the spices we understand the virtues or gifts of the Holy Ghost, which breathe forth a spiritual odour; and because a little before the Prophet had said to Christ: "God, thy God, hath anointed thee," he rightly subjoineth now: "Myrrh and stacte and cassia," explaining the aromatic odours which flowed forth from that anointing: of which odour Blessed Paul speaketh, when he saith, II *Cor.* ii: "We are the good odour of Christ." And because in his passion Christ chiefly breathed forth the greatest odours of virtues, chiefly of strong patience, of humble obedience, and of burning charity, therefore he named myrrh, bitter but fragrant, which agreeth with patience; aloes also, likewise bitter and fragrant, which agreeth with humility and obedience, of which Blessed Paul: "He humbled himself, becoming obedient unto death," *Philip.* ii; lastly cassia, hot and fragrant, which agreeth with the most burning charity, with which he prayed even for his crucifiers, *Luke* xxvi.[^9] Now these spices flowed from the garments and ivory houses of Christ. "Myrrh, saith he, and cassia from thy garments (*a vestimentis tuis*), from the ivory houses," supply, flow, or did flow. By *the garments* is understood the humanity of Christ, which as with a garment, or veil, covered the divinity; by *the ivory houses* is understood the same humanity, which was as it were a temple or palace most white by reason of purity, in which the divinity dwelt. Nor ought it to seem strange, if the same thing be called a garment and a house: for the Apostle also, II *Cor.* v, joineth these two together, when he speaketh thus: "We know, if our earthly house of this habitation be dissolved, that we have a building of God, a house not made with hands, eternal in heaven; for in this also we groan, desiring to be clothed upon with our habitation that is from heaven, yet so that we be found clothed, not naked; for we also, who are in this tabernacle, do groan, being burthened, because we would not be unclothed, but clothed upon; that that which is mortal may be swallowed up by glory."[^10] Where thou seest that the mortal body is called a house and a tabernacle, and at the same time a garment, of which we would not be unclothed, but clothed upon; and likewise that the heavenly house is called a garment and a habitation. Thus therefore the humanity of Christ, whence the sweetest odours of virtues breathed, can be called a garment, and an ivory house, unless one would have the garment referred to the soul, the ivory house to the body, which Christ himself, *John* ii, called *a temple,* when he saith: "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up." Nor ought it to move us that the Prophet saith in the plural number, "from the ivory houses:" for a great house is one and many by reason of the multitude of chambers, and cells; whence also of one house we say in the plural number, *ædes nostræ* (our house). There followeth: "Out of which the daughters of kings have delighted thee in thy glory (*in honore tuo,* in thy honour)," that is, out of which spices, which breathe from the garments and ivory houses of thy humanity, "the daughters of kings," whether royal and lofty souls, or the multitudes of peoples out of divers kingdoms, "have delighted thee," whilst they run to the odour of thy ointments. For Christ is greatly delighted, when he seeth the multitudes of the Saints, stirred up and drawn by his odour, run after that odour. And truly he who with the spiritual sense of smelling beginneth to perceive the most sweet odour which breatheth from the patience, humility, charity of Christ, cannot but run after it, and suffer any torments whatsoever rather than suffer himself to be separated from him; for he saith with the Apostle: "Who shall separate us from the love of Christ?" *Rom.* viii. But therefore chiefly do the daughters of kings, running after the odour of his ointments, delight Christ, because they do this in his honour, that is, from a pure intention of glorifying God. For the Martyrs, running after Christ by their passion, greatly glorified God; whence John saith, chap. xxi, of the passion of St. Peter foretold to him by Christ: "Signifying, saith he, by what death he should glorify God."

[Margin: Verse 11.]

**11. THE QUEEN STOOD ON THY RIGHT HAND, IN GILDED CLOTHING; SURROUNDED WITH VARIETY.**

[Margin: The Spouse of Christ is every perfect soul, but chiefly the Blessed Virgin Mary.]

[Margin: Holy men are mingled with the angels in the heavenly beatitude.]

Hitherto David hath prophesied of the Bridegroom: now he setteth about to prophesy of the Spouse; and by the Spouse almost all the expositors understand the Church, because the Apostle, *Ephes.* v, most openly teacheth that the Church is the Spouse of Christ. This therefore is the principal sense, that by the Spouse the Church be understood: but all these things which are said of the Spouse can also be applied to every perfect soul, and chiefly to the Blessed Virgin Mary, who, although she is the mother of Christ according to the flesh, is yet also his Spouse according to the spirit, and holdeth the first place among the members of the Church. And so not without reason is this Psalm sung on the Feasts of the Blessed Virgin and of other virgins also; nor without reason is it said of each: "Come, Spouse of Christ." David therefore saith, addressing Christ: "The Queen stood on thy right hand," that is, thy Spouse shall stand by, who is also Queen, since she is the Spouse of thee, who art King, "on thy right hand," that is, nearest to thee, and in the place of highest honour beneath the royal throne: and she shall stand by "in gilded clothing, surrounded with variety," that is, in a precious garment, such as becometh a Queen. But the several words are to be considered. *Astitit* (stood) is put for *assistet* (shall stand by), after the manner of the prophets, who see things to come as though they were already done. Moreover Chrysostom noteth that it is not said *sedit* (sat), but *astitit,* because to sit agreeth with an equal, and therefore the Son sitteth at the right hand of the Father, as equal; but the Church, as inferior, stood by with the heavenly powers, which are said to stand before God. The Hebrew word נצבה *nitsbah* properly signifieth *stood,* and denoteth firmness, as if the Prophet said: Thy Spouse hath firmly cleaved to thee, and is so established and confirmed that there is no danger to her of being put away. *Queen* in the Hebrew is שגל *shegal,* which signifieth a wife and a concubine; thence arose divers interpretations; for some translated: *Astitit uxor* (The wife stood by), others, *astitit concubina* (the concubine stood by); but the Seventy Interpreters, that they might explain that this word is to be understood of a true wife, not of concubines, chose rather to translate *reginam* (queen); for not the concubines of kings, but only the lawful wives are called queens. "On thy right hand:" these words do not signify that the Spouse is so on the right hand of the Bridegroom that the Bridegroom is understood to be on the left, but they place the Bridegroom in the midst and on a higher throne, on his right hand the queen, and on his left the other princes. And perhaps the Holy Ghost would by these words signify the incredible dignity of the Church, which is not only admitted to the kingdom of heaven, that it may stand with the angels before the throne of God, but also goeth before the angels, so that the Church itself of men is on the right hand, and the company of the angels on the left. For although holy men are intermingled with the holy angels, so that they make one city: yet many holy men shall go before many angels in the kingdom of heaven; and there is in the Church of men the Blessed Virgin, who goeth before all the angels; and the head of men and angels is Christ, who is not an angel, but man. Hence St. John Chrysostom rightly admonisheth men to think how ungrateful they are to so great benevolence of God, when they sin; for God hath exalted the human race, and chiefly the Church taken up out of heathenism and idolatry, out of the depth of sins, and truly out of the mire of dregs, to the highest dignity, that it may stand with the angels in the kingdom of heaven, and obtain the name of the Spouse of God. And all this out of the sole charity of the heavenly Bridegroom, who "loved it, and delivered himself up for it, that he might sanctify it, and cleanse it, and present it to himself a glorious Church, not having spot or wrinkle," as the Apostle writeth, *Ephes.* v. "In gilded clothing, surrounded with variety;" for all these words there are in the Hebrew text only these two words, בכתם אופיר, that is, *in auro de ophir* (in gold of Ophir), which words St. Jerome translateth, *in diademate aureo* (in a golden diadem), others in other ways. But the Seventy Interpreters would explain what was included in that general Hebrew word, and rightly said that a precious garment is signified, and all the other ornaments of a Spouse, which are divers according to the variety of the members, as the diadem, the necklace, the girdle, the shoes, and the like; which explanation seemeth to be taken from that which is said a little below: "In golden borders, clothed round about with varieties." Now what these things signify spiritually, the Fathers do not expound in one way; St. Augustine by the gold understandeth *wisdom;* by the garment, *doctrine;* by the variety, *the divers tongues* in which this same doctrine of the Church is preached. St. Basil understandeth indeed by the golden garment the doctrine of the Church; but by the variety, the divers dogmas, moral, natural, speculative, practical, etc. St. Jerome and St. Chrysostom understand by the golden garment the grace of justification, by the variety the divers virtues, faith, hope, love, humility, and others. Which explanation can be confirmed from chap. xix of the *Apoc.,* where the Spouse is said to be "adorned with fine linen, glittering and white;" and St. John expounding saith: "For the fine linen are the justifications of saints." It might be added that the variety of the ornaments of the Spouse consisteth not only in the divers virtues, which are necessary to each, but also in the divers gifts which the Church possesseth according to the variety of the members: for some are the gifts of Apostles, others of Martyrs, others of Virgins, others of Doctors, others of Confessors, and others of others.

[Margin: Verse 12.]

**12. HEARKEN, O DAUGHTER, AND SEE, AND INCLINE THY EAR: AND FORGET THY PEOPLE AND THY FATHER'S HOUSE.**

[Margin: Why is the Church called daughter?]

He now addresseth the Church itself, and faithfully and lovingly admonisheth her. Now he calleth her *daughter,* either because he speaketh in the person of God the Father, as St. Jerome saith, or because he speaketh as one of the Fathers of the Church, as St. Augustine saith. But when by the Spouse

<!-- p. 298 (PDF 309) -->

[Margin: The love of Christ towards us is unspeakable.]

is understood the Blessed Virgin Mary, then David calleth her daughter altogether properly, because she was descended from his flesh. "Hearken, O daughter," saith he, that is, hear the voice of thy Bridegroom, "and see," that is, consider diligently the things which thou hearest, "and incline thy ear," that is, humbly obey his precepts, "and forget thy people and thy father's house," that is, that thou mayest the more easily serve the Bridegroom, forget the world, and the things which are in the world: for the Church was chosen out of the world, and went out of the world; and although she is still in the world, yet she ought not to be of the world, as the Bridegroom also is not of the world: now the world is rightly called the people of those who love worldly goods, and the same world is the house of our old father Adam, who was cast out of paradise into this world; unless it please rather that by the father be understood the devil, who is the father of all men of iniquity, according to that of *John* viii: "You are of your father the devil." And certainly they who shall think upon the things which we said above of the unspeakable love of the Bridegroom, who delivered himself up that he might pluck us out[^11] of the deep pit, and carry us over into the highest heaven, and make us his glorious Spouse, will without difficulty withdraw their love from the world, that they may carry it wholly over to the Bridegroom. Now that word *forget* hath great emphasis: for it signifieth that love is to be withdrawn from the world by the roots, so that the world pass not only from desire and thought, but even from the memory of all, as though it had never been.

[Margin: Verse 13.]

**13. AND THE KING SHALL GREATLY DESIRE THY BEAUTY; FOR HE IS THE LORD THY GOD, AND HIM THEY SHALL ADORE.**

[Margin: God requireth nothing more of his own than obedience.]

He giveth the reason why the Spouse ought to forget her people and her father's house, that she may be wholly intent upon the love and obedience of the heavenly Bridegroom: namely because so it shall come to pass that the king shall desire to see her beauty, and to have it with him. And because the true beauty of the Spouse is inward, as is explained more clearly a little below, where it is said: "All the beauty[^12] of the King's daughter is within," and consisteth in the virtues, and chiefly in the obedience of his commandments, or in charity, on which all the commandments depend; therefore he addeth: "For he is the Lord thy God," that is, therefore will he love thy beauty, which is placed in obedience to him, "for he is the Lord thy God." For a lord requireth nothing more of his servants, nor God of his creatures, than obedience. And that we may understand that this Bridegroom is Lord absolutely, and true God, he addeth: "And him they shall adore," that is, thy Bridegroom is not such that thou canst pretend equality with him, but he is indeed thy Bridegroom by grace, but thy Lord, and the Lord of all creatures, by nature, and to him adoration is due from all. In the Hebrew text there is not *Deus* (God), but only *Dominus* (the Lord), where we have: "For he is the Lord thy God." But not only the Seventy, but also St. Jerome, translated *Dominus Deus tuus* (the Lord thy God); and from the adoration due to him it is gathered that he is not Lord only, but also God, that is, not a lord of whatsoever kind, but the supreme Lord. In the same Hebrew text, as also in the Greek, there is, *et adorabis eum* (and thou shalt adore him); but in the sense there is no disagreement: for all creatures ought to adore God, wherefore the Spouse herself also ought to adore; nor is this reading, *adorabunt eum* (they shall adore him), a new one: for it is found in St. Augustine, St. Basil, St. Chrysostom, and others; nay, St. Jerome in the Epistle *to Principia* saith that the Seventy translated, *adorabunt eum.* Wherefore the Greek reading, which now hath *adorabis eum,* is not from the edition of the Seventy Interpreters, or is corrupted. And because, as we have often said, the Seventy Interpreters had the best Hebrew copies, it is to be believed that the Hebrew reading hath since that time been changed by the error of the copyists, which could easily be done, since the Hebrew words *adorabunt* and *adorabis* differ not, save in the last letter, namely *iod* and *vau,* which could easily be changed by reason of the very great likeness between them.

[Margin: Verse 14.]

**14. AND THE DAUGHTERS OF TYRE WITH GIFTS, YEA, ALL THE RICH AMONG THE PEOPLE, SHALL ENTREAT THY COUNTENANCE.**

Because he had said: "And him they shall adore," namely the Bridegroom, he addeth that the Spouse herself also, as Queen, is to be honoured with gifts and supplications. And the daughters of Tyre, saith he, with gifts shall entreat thy countenance, that is, and the daughters of the Gentiles, who were before the enemies of thy Bridegroom, shall be made subject to his empire, and shall come to thee, and shall entreat thy face, not only with prayers of words, but also with presents and gifts. "All the rich among the people," that is, what he said of the daughters of Tyre, understand chiefly of the more powerful, such as the rich above all are wont to be. In this place also almost every word requireth an explanation of its own. *Et filiæ* (And the daughters) in the Hebrew is ובת *ubath, et filia* (and the daughter), and so St. Basil and St. Chrysostom read, and the Greek copy now hath it so; but yet St. Jerome and Augustine in the Commentary read: *Et filiæ.* If we read: *Et filia Tyri* (And the daughter of Tyre), the city, or the people of Tyre, will be signified, in which manner we say, the daughter of Babylon, the daughter of Jerusalem; if we read: *Et filiæ,* the women of the city of Tyre will be signified; now he maketh mention of women rather than of men, because he addresseth the Spouse: for women are wont to approach more familiarly to a queen than men to a king. But as by the Spouse we do not understand in this place a true woman, but the Church, which embraceth men and women, so also by the daughters of Tyre are understood all the Gentiles, whether they be male or female. The Hebrew word of Tyre, צור *tsor,* is ambiguous: for it can signify both strong, or mighty, and at the same time also the city which is called Tyre. But the Seventy Interpreters interpreted it Tyre, and the Latin Interpreter followed that interpretation; and all the Greek and Latin Fathers expound it, except

<!-- p. 299 (PDF 310) -->

St. Jerome in the Epistle *to Principia,* who chose rather both to read and to expound *filiam fortissimam* (the most strong daughter). Moreover Tyre was a great and famous city of the Gentiles, and on the borders of the land of promise, and therefore from a neighbouring city the Prophet named all the Gentiles. "With gifts:" he calleth gifts the offerings which the Gentiles converted to the faith offered for the buildings, or ornaments of churches, for the nourishment of the poor, and the like good works. "Shall entreat thy countenance:" St. Basil, and certain others, by the countenance of the Spouse understand the head of the Church, that is, Christ; but the simpler exposition is that by the countenance of the Spouse the Church itself be understood: for it is a Hebrew phrase, to entreat the countenance of any one, for that which is, to entreat any one, as I *Kings* xiii: "I have not appeased the face of the Lord;" and *Ps.* xciv: "Let us come before his face (*faciem ejus*) with thanksgiving;" and *Ps.* cxviii: "I entreated thy face." Now we are said to entreat the face, because when we pray any one, we look upon his face, and from the sadness or cheerfulness of the face we understand whether he whom we pray is favourable to us or angry. "All the rich among the people." This is an apposition, as if he said: The daughters of Tyre with gifts shall entreat thy countenance; not all with gifts, but the rich only.

[Margin: Verse 15.]

**15. ALL THE GLORY OF THE KING'S DAUGHTER IS WITHIN IN GOLDEN BORDERS, CLOTHED ROUND ABOUT WITH VARIETIES.**

[Margin: The outward adorning of churches in the celebration of Masses is not to be blamed.]

Because David hath often spoken of the beauty and adorning of the Spouse, lest any one should haply think those things to be bodily, he meeteth in this place carnal thoughts of that kind, and saith: "All the glory," whether of the comeliness of the face, or of the precious garments, of this "Queen, is within, and if she is said to be adorned with golden borders, and clothed round about" with a garment of divers colours, all those things are spiritual, are inward, are to be sought in the heart. Hence St. Peter admonisheth Christian women, I *Pet.* iii, that after the likeness of this Spouse they use the inward adorning. "Whose adorning, saith he, let it not be the outward plaiting of the hair, or the wearing of gold, or the putting on of apparel; but the hidden man of the heart in the incorruptibility of a quiet and a meek spirit." Nor yet is the outward adorning of churches to be blamed in the celebration of the sacraments, in the adorning of altars, in the sacred solemnities: for here it is not treated of the material church, but of the men who are the people of God, and the members of Christ, whose chief beauty and adorning consisteth in the virtues, as we said above; from which virtues nevertheless good works ought to spring outwardly, "that they who see them may glorify our Father who is in heaven," as the Saviour teacheth, *Matt.* vi.[^13] Moreover by the golden borders nothing seemeth able to be more fitly understood than charity itself, which is compared to gold, because it is the most precious and most excellent of all the virtues; and it can be called like to borders, because as garments end in the borders, and are stayed by the same borders, so the end of all the commandments and virtues is charity, and on charity all the commandments depend, and by the same all the virtues are formed, and perfected. Now what the garment of divers colours is, we said above. And of this garment of divers colours the Apostle seemeth to speak, when he saith: "Put ye on the bowels of mercy, benignity, humility, modesty, patience," etc., *Col.* chap. iii. As to the words, that pronoun *ejus* (of her) is not now in the Hebrew, or Greek text; yet it is read by St. Jerome in the Epistle *to Principia,* and in the Commentary on the Psalms, as from the version of the Seventy, and it is read also by St. Augustine; and the Hebrew word, if it be read with a point in the last letter, will signify *gloria ejus* (her glory). Yet it is true that that *ejus* is to be referred to the Hebrew phrase, and according to the Latin phrase is redundant. Now that the Spouse is called the king's daughter ought not to be strange; for the spouse of the king's son is commonly called *the daughter of the king his father,* because the bridegroom and the spouse are two in one flesh. I am not ignorant that in the Hebrew text the pointing is different from the Greek and Latin pointing, but the pointing which was added by the Rabbins to the Hebrew copy is not much to be regarded, and the Seventy Interpreters are more to be believed than all the later Rabbins.

[Margin: Verse 16.]

**16. AFTER HER SHALL VIRGINS BE BROUGHT TO THE KING: HER NEIGHBOURS SHALL BE BROUGHT TO THEE.**

[Margin: The spouses of Christ who have vowed their virginity to God.]

Although the Spouse of Christ is one only, and she singularly beloved, that is, the universal Church, yet after her there are also spouses

[Margin: Virginity outside the Church hath not the prerogative of a spouse.]

of Christ, by a certain chief prerogative beyond the rest of the faithful, those souls which in the flesh have vowed their virginity to God, and have studied to please him alone. Of whom the Apostle saith, I *Cor.* vii: "He that is without a wife is solicitous for the things that belong to the Lord, how he may please God: but he that is with a wife is solicitous for the things of the world, how he may please his wife, and he is divided; and the unmarried woman and the virgin thinketh on the things of the Lord, that she may be holy both in body and in spirit: but she that is married thinketh on the things of the world, how she may please her husband." Of these the Prophet now speaketh, and in these two verses commendeth virginity, which wonderfully pleaseth Christ the virgin, as him who feedeth among the lilies. "After her, saith he, shall virgins be brought to the king, Christ," that is, after the principal Spouse, the Church, there shall also be brought to the bridal chamber of the heavenly Bridegroom all those souls which have dedicated their virginity to God. "Her neighbours shall be brought to thee," that is, those virgins only shall be brought to thee the Bridegroom, who shall have been nearest to thy principal Spouse the Church, that is, who shall have been joined and united to her, which is said, as the holy Fathers Basil and Augustine, and others expound, to shut out heretics and schismatics: for virginity outside the Church hath not the prerogative of a spouse. And because we said at the beginning that the things which we read in this Psalm of the Spouse can be understood also of Blessed Mary the mother of the Lord, who is by right called the Queen

<!-- p. 300 (PDF 311) -->

of virgins: we can also expound the things which follow of her, so that the sense is, that all true virgins are to be brought to Christ after her, that is, after the mother of the Lord; and that her neighbours, that is, they who have drawn near to her by the imitation of everlasting integrity, are to be brought to the nuptials of the same Bridegroom, and king, Christ.

[Margin: Verse 17.]

**17. THEY SHALL BE BROUGHT WITH GLADNESS AND REJOICING: THEY SHALL BE BROUGHT INTO THE TEMPLE OF THE KING.**

He explaineth the joy of so manifold nuptials: "They shall be brought, saith he, with gladness and rejoicing," that is, the virgins shall be brought to the nuptials with the great applause and jubilation of the whole heavenly Jerusalem. Perhaps also there is noted in this place that canticle which it is lawful for the choir of virgins alone to sing in the heavenly kingdom: of which we read thus in the *Apoc.* chap. xiv: "And they sung as it were a new canticle, before the throne, and before the four living creatures, and the ancients; and no man could say the canticle, but those hundred forty-four thousand, who were purchased from the earth: these are they who were not defiled with women: for they are virgins, these follow the Lamb whithersoever he goeth." Happy the souls which follow the Lamb walking in the virginal way, and with gladness and rejoicing say the new canticle, namely one unknown to the ancient Fathers, which no other can say; and with this jubilation they shall be brought into the temple of the King, that is, into the heavenly tabernacle, which can be called both a palace for its magnificence, and a temple for its holiness.

[Margin: Verse 18.]

**18. INSTEAD OF THY FATHERS, SONS ARE BORN TO THEE: THOU SHALT MAKE THEM PRINCES OVER ALL THE EARTH.**

[Margin: The Apostles gave laws to the whole world, which no one besides them could do.]

[Margin: Bishops are the princes of the Church in the place of the Apostles.]

Hitherto the Prophet hath expounded the dignity and ornaments of the Bridegroom and the Spouse: now he expoundeth the fruit of the nuptials, saying that of this marriage there shall be born a most happy offspring, which is to rule the whole world. Now it is doubtful to whom the Prophet speaketh, to the Bridegroom, or to the Spouse: some more recent writers say that he speaketh to the Bridegroom, led by this reason, that in the Hebrew those pronouns, *tuis* (thy) and *tibi* (to thee), are of the masculine gender; but that this speech is directed to the Spouse the holy Fathers teach, Basil, Chrysostom, Jerome, Augustine, and with them Euthymius in the Commentaries. St. Jerome, in the Epistle *to Principia,* affirmeth that it can be expounded in either way: which indeed is true; but the common opinion of the Fathers is to be preferred: nor is the Hebrew text repugnant to this opinion, if the points be removed, or changed, as the skilled easily understand. Because therefore the Prophet had admonished the Spouse to forget her people and her father's house, that is, her forefathers; now he comforteth her, promising abundance of sons in the place of the fathers whom she hath left, and whom she was bidden to forget; and at the same time, as we said, he foretelleth that the fruit of the nuptials of the heavenly Bridegroom and of the Church[^14] his Spouse shall be most happy. "Instead of thy fathers, sons are born to thee," that is, instead of the fathers whom thou hadst, and who are now dead, that is, instead of the Patriarchs, and Prophets, and instead of the fathers whom thou hast left in heathenism, and whose laws thou wast bidden wholly to forget, "sons are born to thee," that is, the Apostles, and the Disciples of Christ, who shall be of so great excellence that they can give laws to the whole world; therefore "thou shalt make them princes over all the earth." For truly the Apostles, the first sons of the Church, gave laws to the whole world, which no temporal monarch could do. For, as St. John Chrysostom noteth, the monarchs of the Romans could not give laws to the Persians, nor the Persians to the Romans; but the Apostles gave laws both to the Romans, and to the Persians, and to all other nations. And as in the first age the Church, instead of the Patriarchs her fathers, had the Apostles for sons: so also in the following age instead of the Apostles her fathers she hath the bishops for sons, who, if not each singly, yet all together are princes over all the earth; and in this manner, through the successors of the bishops, the Church always hath sons instead of fathers, whom she may establish in the sees and dignity of the fathers.

[Margin: Verse 19.]

**19. THEY SHALL REMEMBER THY NAME, O LORD,[^15] THROUGHOUT ALL GENERATIONS.**

He concludeth the Psalm, saying that these spiritual nuptials sung in the Psalm, and the fruit of the nuptials, that is, the most noble offspring to be born of this holy wedlock, shall have no other end than the perpetual praise of God. For, saith he, those sons, who shall be put in the place of the fathers, and shall themselves also become fathers of sons, "shall remember thy name," that is, shall sing thy power and grace "throughout all generations." In the Hebrew and Greek copy there is in the singular, *memor ero nominis tui* (I will remember thy name), and so the Greek Fathers read, and so also it is in very many Latin manuscript copies; but because St. Jerome and St. Augustine in the Commentaries read, *memores erunt nominis tui* (they shall remember thy name), and the Church in the ecclesiastical office, when she celebrateth the feast days of the Apostles, so singeth these verses, it did not seem expedient that in the Bible revised in the time of Sixtus V and Clement VIII this place should be changed, especially since in the sense there is no difference. For they who read, *memor ero nominis tui,* will have this to be said by the Prophet, who promiseth God that he will at all times sing the praises of God for so mighty benefits bestowed on the human race, which are explained in this Psalm. But because David was to die, and could not himself by himself sing the praises of God throughout all generations, they say that he would signify that he would sing through others, who, succeeding one another unto the consummation of the world, were to sing these Psalms. Now this exposition is not different from the former; for it is the same to say: *I will remember thy name, and will sing thy praises through the memory, and tongue of others,* who shall succeed one another; and: *Thy sons,* who shall succeed one another, *shall remember thy name, and shall perpetually sing these thy praises written by me.* Now St. John Chrysostom noteth that David the prophet,

<!-- p. 301 (PDF 312) -->

besides many other things which he foresaw and foretold, foresaw and foretold this also of the eternity of his songs, namely that at no time ever was the celebration of them to be intermitted; which prophecy we see to have been fulfilled and to be daily fulfilled.

[Margin: Verse 20.]

**20. THEREFORE SHALL PEOPLE PRAISE THEE FOR EVER; YEA, FOR EVER AND EVER.**

From this, that the Apostles, and their successors the bishops, shall always remember the name of the Lord, and shall sing and preach his praises, the Prophet rightly gathereth that this also shall come to pass, that the faithful peoples, instructed by the bishops, shall confess to the Lord his praises "for ever, yea, for ever and ever," in this life, and in that to come: for although in the heavenly country prayers and tears shall cease, yet praising and glorifying shall not cease.
