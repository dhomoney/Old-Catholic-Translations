BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XLVIII.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*Unto the end, a psalm for the sons of Core.*

In the title there is nothing new. The argument is a most profitable exhortation to follow after virtue, and to flee vices.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. HEAR THESE THINGS, ALL YE NATIONS: GIVE EAR, ALL YE INHABITANTS OF THE WORLD.**[^1]

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. ALL YOU THAT ARE EARTHBORN, AND YOU SONS OF MEN: BOTH RICH AND POOR TOGETHER.**

[Margin: This discourse regardeth all men.]

This is a preface to win attention, which is taken from this, that the discourse to be delivered befitteth all men equally, and not those present only, but those to come also. Therefore the whole human race is called together to hear; and since neither could any place hold so great a multitude, nor could any voice of a speaker be heard by so many, we ought to understand that the Prophet foresaw that his Psalms would be sung in every land, and in every time to come; and that he therefore invited all without exception to hear. "Hear," saith he, "all ye nations" (or, as it is in the Hebrew, *omnes populi,* all ye peoples), to wit, because the things which are to be said pertain to all; "give ear, all ye inhabitants of the earth:"[^2] he declareth the foregoing words; for that which he had said: "Hear," he declareth by adding, "give ear," that is, do not so hear that the speech only sound about the ears, and pass by; but so hear that the speech be received within the ears, and stick there, that it may be attentively considered. And that which he said: "All ye nations," he explaineth by adding, "all ye inhabitants of the world," to wit, lest by nations the Gentiles alone be understood; or lest by peoples, as it is in the Hebrew, those alone should be understood who dwell in cities. He calleth therefore to hear all who dwell in the whole world, whether they be Gentiles or Hebrews, whether they dwell gathered in cities, or scattered in mountains and deserts. And lest he seem to address the living and present alone, he addeth something more general, saying: "All you that are earthborn, and you sons of men," that is, hear, all you who draw your origin from the earth and from men: for all men, as well those who are as those who shall be, have a common mother, the earth, and a common father, Adam; or they have a common matter out of which they are, the earth, and a common active principle, man. In the Hebrew there is בני אדם *bene Adam, filii Adam* (sons of Adam), where we have *terrigenæ* (earthborn); but it is credible that the Seventy Interpreters did not read *Adam,* but אדמה *adamah,* which word signifieth *terram* (earth): for it is easy for the last letter to have fallen out of our Hebrew copies. He adjoineth lastly: "Both rich and poor together," to show that what is to be said in this Psalm pertaineth to all equally, not more to the rich than to the poor; and that in this theatre no account is to be had of the difference of persons, as none shall be had in that assembly to which we shall be called together for judgment at the last day. For this discourse, which in this Psalm the Holy Ghost holdeth to men by the Prophet, would altogether require such an assembly as there shall be at the last day.

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. MY MOUTH SHALL SPEAK WISDOM: AND THE MEDITATION OF MY HEART UNDERSTANDING.**[^3]

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. I WILL INCLINE MY EAR TO A PARABLE; I WILL OPEN MY PROPOSITION ON THE PSALTERY.**

This is the other part of the preface, in which the Prophet again prepareth the hearer from another twofold head, from the dignity of the matter, and from the dignity of the teacher. The dignity of the matter is shown from this, that the speech is to be of wisdom and prudence, and a speech not simple, but parabolical and enigmatical, such as it becometh a speech of most weighty things to be, that it be not despised, nor understood save by diligent and attentive hearers. "My mouth," saith he, "shall speak wisdom," that is, shall teach those things which make a man wise; "and the meditation of my heart prudence," that is, and the meditation of my heart, which breaketh forth through the mouth, shall teach those things which make men prudent: for he declareth the former part of the verse by the latter; for when he had said: "My mouth shall speak," lest we should think that he would speak rashly whatsoever came into his mouth, he addeth: "And the meditation of my heart," supply, shall speak, that is, my mouth shall bring forth that which my heart hath conceived by long meditation. And because he had said that he would speak wisdom, lest any man should think that the speech would be of speculative things, and such as pertain little to human actions, he added *prudence.* He signifieth therefore that the speech is to be held of things which contain wisdom and prudence together, and which make a man wise in contemplating the highest causes, and prudent in ordering his life most rightly. He toucheth next the greatness of the teacher, when he addeth: "I will incline my ear to a parable; I will open my

<!-- p. 321 (PDF 332) -->

proposition on the psaltery," as if he should say: It is not I that am about to speak the things which ye shall presently hear, but the spirit of the Lord shall speak by me; for I first hear attentively my inward Teacher, then I speak the things which I have heard. "I will incline my ear to a parable," that is, I will submit my ear to the spirit speaking, and will hear obediently; and so I will open on the psaltery my proposition, to wit, that which is inspired and communicated to me by God. By a parable in this place is understood an enigma, that is, an obscure proposition, which requireth attention and study that it may be understood. This is plain from the Hebrew words, which signify *proverbium* (a proverb) and *ænigma* (an enigma); and the like are had *Judges* xiv, where Samson proposed that sentence to be explained: "Out of the eater came forth meat, and out of the strong came forth sweetness;" which sentence he calleth a problem and a proposition: and in that place are had the same Hebrew words, חידה, *chidah,* which are had here. And that which is added, *in psalterio* (on the psaltery), or *in cithara* (on the harp), as the Hebrew word properly soundeth, pertaineth also to the preparing of the hearers: for David signifieth that he will propose his most weighty doctrine with song and sound, that the sweetness of the music may temper the weight of the doctrine, and that most profitable precepts may be received more pleasantly and more eagerly.

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. WHY SHALL I FEAR IN THE EVIL DAY? THE INIQUITY OF MY HEEL SHALL ENCOMPASS ME.**

[Margin: What shall terrify us in the day of judgment?]

This is the proverb, or enigma, which, after so careful a preface, the Prophet proposeth to the whole human race and solveth. But the solution is no less obscure than the proposition, so that the proposition as well as the solution may be called enigmatical. But yet these words are enigmas to men preoccupied with the love of this world, for to the true servants of God they are most open. He saith therefore: "Why shall I fear in the evil day?" as if he should say: I propose to all of you who inhabit the whole world this question to be solved. What is it that can terrify me "in the evil day," that is, in the day of the divine judgment, which is called, *Soph.* i, "a day of wrath, a day of tribulation and distress, a day of calamity and misery, a day of darkness and obscurity,[^4] a day of clouds and whirlwinds, a day of the trumpet and alarm"? Or (which is the same) what can make me secure in the day of that judgment, upon which dependeth the sum of all my goods and evils? A great problem assuredly, and one which toucheth all men without exception. Now the Prophet solveth his enigma when he saith: "The iniquity of my heel shall encompass me," that is, that which shall make me fear in that evil day is not poverty, because the judge is not corrupted by gifts; it is not lowness of birth, or of state, because the judge regardeth not persons; it is not the malice of witnesses, or of advocates, because the judge knoweth all things; it is not the power of adversaries, because the judge feareth no man; lastly, it is nothing else but iniquity; and not any iniquity whatsoever, but only "the iniquity of my heel," that is, the iniquity of the end of my life, iniquity persevering even to the end of life: for if that be found in me in that day, it "shall" altogether "encompass me" after the manner of a rampart, or of a wall, so that I can in no way escape, because then there will be no more place for penance, or for pardon. And on the contrary, that which can make me secure and fearless in the evil day will not be riches, or nobility, or the diligence of advocates, or the power of friends, but justice alone; and not any justice whatsoever, but the justice of my heel, that is, of the end of my life, whether innocence shall have persevered with me even unto death, or, iniquity being blotted out by true penance, true justice shall have succeeded in its place. I know that these verses are expounded by others in many other ways: but I study brevity, and leave to the reader the judgment of the divers opinions.

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. THEY THAT TRUST IN THEIR OWN STRENGTH, AND GLORY IN THE MULTITUDE OF THEIR RICHES.**

After the Prophet had said that nothing[^5] is to be feared but sin, that a man may be secure in the evil day, he now addeth that there are many who, not understanding this, trust in their own strength, and thereby glory in the riches which they have got with great pains, thinking that by this means they will be most safe in any evil day whatsoever; and at the same time he showeth that they are altogether deceived, and that his problem and its solution are most true. He saith therefore: "They that trust in their own strength," that is, who fear not the evil day, relying upon their own power and strength, as are very many of the children of this world, they also in consequence "glory in the multitude of their riches;" for they think that all things can be overcome and taken by storm with money. And truly in this world there is great force in wealth and riches, so that the Wise man hath truly said: "All things obey money," *Ecclus.* xviii.[^6] But in the evil day no money will any more be found, nor, if it were found, could it profit. Therefore the Prophet subjoineth:

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. NO BROTHER CAN REDEEM, SHALL MAN REDEEM? HE SHALL NOT GIVE TO GOD HIS RANSOM.**[^7]

[Margin: The blood of Christ alone can be the price of a soul.]

He showeth that the confidence of them that trust in money is vain. For he saith: "No brother can redeem," that is, no man will be able with riches however great to redeem his brother in the evil day. "Shall man redeem:" this is to be read with a question, as St. Basil admonisheth, "no brother can redeem, shall man redeem?" or the negation is to be repeated after the manner of holy Scripture: *Frater non redimit, non redimet homo* (No brother can redeem, nor shall man redeem). For the sense is: "No brother can redeem," much less shall another man redeem. "He shall not give to God his ransom," that is, he will be able to deliver neither another nor himself; for although he abound very greatly in money, and offer it all to appease God in the evil day, it will profit him nothing. For, as the Lord speaketh in the Gospel, *Matt.* xvi: "What exchange shall a man give for his soul?" for the price of a human soul surpasseth all the goods of this world, and therefore the blood of the Only-begotten of God alone, which was of infinite worth, could be the price of souls. Therefore he who, this great price being neglected,

<!-- p. 322 (PDF 333) -->

shall come to the evil day held in the captivity of the devil, "shall not give to God his ransom."

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. NOR THE PRICE OF THE REDEMPTION OF HIS SOUL: AND SHALL LABOUR FOR EVER, AND SHALL STILL LIVE UNTO THE END.**

He declareth what he had said: "He shall not give to God his ransom;" for the appeasing of God (*placatio Dei*) is the price of redemption, which the Only-begotten of God alone could give; of whom St. Basil understandeth the words following: "He shall labour for ever,[^8] and shall still live unto the end," that is, he shall labour in this world for a time, and shall live afterwards for ever. But Saints Ambrose and Chrysostom expound these same words of any godly and wise man whatsoever, whom they oppose to the foolish and impious man trusting in the multitude of his riches: for the godly and wise man laboureth for a time in keeping the commandments, but shall live for ever. But St. Augustine expoundeth them of that same man of whom it was said a little before, "he shall not give to God his ransom;" and will have this to be the sense: He who trusteth in the multitude of his riches "shall labour for ever," because his labour shall have no end, and shall live a short time, because his life shall be unto the end, and not beyond. And according to this exposition the text of the Prophet seemeth to be better continued, and the words *in sæculum* (for ever) and *in finem* (unto the end) are more properly expounded: for to labour *in sæculum* is not to labour in this world, or to labour a short time, but it is to labour for ever; and to live unto the end is not to live without end, but it is to live even to the end appointed and determined, so that the natural end of life be neither prevented nor extended. The Hebrew words, for *laborabit in sæculum* (he shall labour for ever), seem to have *quiescet in sæculum* (he shall rest for ever): for חדל *chadal* signifieth *quiescere* (to rest). But it is not likely that the Seventy Interpreters rendered a sense altogether contrary; therefore I suspect that they read חרל *charal*: for the letters ד and ר are most like. Now the verb חרל *charal* signifieth *angustiari* (to be straitened) and *cruciari* (to be tormented), and as it were *urticis pungi* (to be stung with nettles): which fitly enough agreeth to the lovers of riches, which the Lord in the Gospel, *Matt.* xiii, compared to thorns. Therefore they who trust in their riches not only shall not give the price of the redemption of their soul, but shall labour in torments with the rich glutton even for ever; and shall live a life of pleasure, which alone seemeth to them to be life, even unto the end appointed by God. About the different pointing[^9] of the Hebrew text I do not trouble myself, since it was set according to the pleasure of the Rabbins, to whom we do no wrong when we prefer before them the Seventy Interpreters, who are more ancient and more learned.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. HE SHALL NOT SEE DESTRUCTION, WHEN HE SHALL SEE THE WISE DYING: THE SENSELESS AND THE FOOL SHALL PERISH TOGETHER.**

[Margin: To those to whom God will not give eternal life, he giveth a long one.]

[Margin: True destruction is not the death of the body, but of the soul.]

Because he had said that the impious man would live unto the end, to wit, by the natural course; he addeth, confirming this very thing: "He shall not see destruction, when he shall see the wise dying," that is, he shall continue the course of his life even to extreme old age, although he see some wise and just men snatched away before the time, and not continuing the course of life unto the natural end; for often God giveth to those to whom he will not give eternal life a long temporal life, as Lazarus in the Gospel, *Luke* xvi, is read to have died before the Glutton. But although this temporal life sometimes falleth long enough to the impious, at length it is ended, and that cometh to pass which followeth in the Psalm: "The senseless and the fool shall perish together." Therefore the sense of the whole verse is: "He shall not see destruction," that is, the fool trusting in riches shall not die before the time, when he shall see some wise men dying before the time; yet at length together with the wise the fool and the senseless also shall come to the end of temporal life. St. Augustine expoundeth that, *He shall not see destruction,* a little otherwise; for he will have the sense to be: He shall not understand what true destruction is; for true destruction is not the death of the body, but the death of the soul. It might also be said, "he shall not see destruction," that is, he shall not think that he also is to die, when he shall see the wise dying: which opinion is most true; for this is human blindness, that whereas we daily see men dying, even better and younger than ourselves, yet we can scarce be brought to think in earnest that we also are to die; yet to the letter in the holy Scriptures, "not to see destruction" is not to be corrupted, *Ps.* xiii:[^10] "Thou wilt not give thy holy one to see corruption;" and *John* viii: "If any man keep my word, he shall not see death for ever." Now what difference there is between the fool and the senseless, St. Basil teacheth: for he saith that he is senseless who is unfit for human and common things, to wit, who lacketh human wisdom, or prudence; but that he is called a fool who lacketh the knowledge of divine things, and is intent upon earthly things alone: and St. Basil seemeth to have looked not so much to the Greek as to the Hebrew words. For truly the Hebrew word כסל signifieth him who lacketh even human wisdom; but the word בער signifieth a man like to a beast; for beasts think of earthly things alone: whence also a little after the Psalm saith: "Man when he was in honour did not understand; he is compared to senseless beasts."

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. AND THEY SHALL LEAVE THEIR RICHES TO STRANGERS: AND THEIR SEPULCHRES SHALL BE THEIR HOUSES FOR EVER.**

[Margin: By strangers sons also can be understood.]

The holy Prophet had called them that trust in riches fools and senseless, as the Lord also calleth them in the Gospel, *Luke* xii, when he saith to a certain rich man: "Thou fool, this night shall they require thy soul of thee: and whose shall those things be which thou hast provided?"[^11] Therefore he proveth that they are truly fools, from this, that they know not how to use riches, and leave them to those whom they know not. *They shall perish,* saith he, the senseless and the fools, such as are they that trust in riches, and, that they may manifestly betray their folly, they shall leave their riches to strangers. Where St. Augustine rightly noteth that by strangers may be understood even the sons and grandsons of impious men: for

<!-- p. 323 (PDF 334) -->

these also, as strangers, quickly forget those whose wealth they have obtained; and although they should not forget, yet they could not help the impious dead: as that rich man of the Gospel had five brethren in the world, and yet could not be helped by them, that he should not burn in hell fire, *Luke* xvi. Accordingly those five brethren were reckoned among aliens: but Lazarus alone, who could have helped him, would have been truly a neighbour, and not an alien, if he had been willing to do mercy to him. The Prophet adjoineth: "And their sepulchres shall be their houses for ever," that is, they themselves indeed, dying, left their riches to strangers, but kept nothing for themselves save a most narrow house, that is, a sepulchre, in which they must lie "for ever," that is, even to the end of the world: for often *æternum* (for ever) in the Scriptures signifieth the whole duration of some time, or state; and this phrase is declared in the words following: "Their dwelling places to all generations." For that which he had called a sepulchre, and an everlasting house, the very same he now calleth a dwelling place *in progenie et progenie* (to all generations; to the word, in generation and generation), that is, a habitation where their bodies shall remain until generations and generations be accomplished: which without doubt shall be even to the consummation of the world. In the Hebrew there is not *sepulcra* (sepulchres), but *interiora* (inward parts): but it is credible that the Seventy Interpreters did not read קרבם, as it is now read, and signifieth *interiora eorum* (their inward parts), but with the letters transposed קברם, and it signifieth *sepulcra eorum* (their sepulchres).

[Margin: Verse 11.]

**11. THEIR DWELLING PLACES TO ALL GENERATIONS: THEY HAVE CALLED THEIR LANDS BY THEIR NAMES.**[^12]

[Margin: The children of this world write their names upon earth, because they were not in heaven.]

Of the former part of the verse it hath been spoken in the explanation of the verse above. And that which is added signifieth that after the death of senseless men nothing remaineth upon earth besides their names. For mortals who know this life alone endeavour in many ways to commend their names to eternity: for either they inscribe their names upon walls; or they give them to cities; or they set them in books written by themselves; or lastly they take care that their sons, or grandsons, bear their names to perpetuate their memory. So the children of this world write their names upon earth, because they hope not that they are written in heaven.

[Margin: Verse 12.]

**12. AND MAN WHEN HE WAS IN HONOUR DID NOT UNDERSTAND; HE IS COMPARED TO SENSELESS BEASTS, AND IS BECOME LIKE TO THEM.**

On occasion of the senseless man, and of him that trusteth in riches, the Prophet digresseth to reprove the human race itself, as if he should say: "And *so* man when he was in honour did not understand," that is, man, honoured by God above the other living creatures with understanding and reason, with free will, with the image of the divinity, with the immortality of the soul, with dominion over all things which are upon earth, did not understand this his honour; but "he is compared to senseless beasts," which have no understanding. "And is become like to them," while after the manner of cattle he looketh not forward to the future, but gapeth after present things alone; and subjecteth himself to the passions of the body, which he ought to have commanded; lastly he despiseth true and everlasting goods, that he may enjoy false and perishing ones, whereas on the contrary he ought to have looked down upon all temporal things, that he might procure for himself everlasting life. In the Hebrew copy there is not *intellexit* (he understood), but ילין *ialin, commorabitur* (he shall abide): but the Seventy Interpreters did not read *ialin,* but יבין *iabin,* which is *intellexit* (he understood). And it was easy in the progress of time for one letter to be changed into the other.

[Margin: Verse 13.]

**13. THIS WAY OF THEIRS IS A STUMBLINGBLOCK TO THEM: AND AFTERWARDS THEY SHALL DELIGHT IN THEIR MOUTH.**

He goeth on to explain, or to bewail, the misery of the human race. "This way of theirs," saith he, "is a stumblingblock to them," that is, this life which they lead like to the life of brutes; or this way which they follow, while they live after the manner of beasts, this law, these manners, this course of life "is a stumblingblock to them," that is, they afford them many occasions of stumbling, and are the cause of great ruin to them, who yet "afterwards shall delight in their mouth," that is, they shall please themselves in their crimes, and shall glory when they have done evil; which pertaineth to the height of misery and calamity. In the Hebrew there is not *scandalum* (stumblingblock), but *insipientia* (senselessness); but it is easy to see whence the discrepancy arose, since the Seventy Interpreters had not in their Hebrew copies כסל, which signifieth *insipientiam* (senselessness), but כשל, which signifieth *scandalum* (stumblingblock). And that the copies which the Seventy had were truer than those which later men had, we believe cannot be denied.

[Margin: Verse 14.]

**14. THEY ARE LAID IN HELL LIKE SHEEP: DEATH SHALL FEED UPON THEM.**

[Margin: With less labour doth God thrust the impious down into hell than sheep are led to the shambles.]

Because he said that men were made by sin like to senseless beasts, he addeth that their end will be, to be slaughtered after the manner of senseless sheep: where is shown both the multitude and the weakness of the men who after death are thrust down to hell: for sheep are cast by flocks into the folds, and are led without any resistance to the shambles. Therefore with less labour will God thrust down into hell, to everlasting death, the multitude of the impious, though they have been most rich and most mighty in the world, than a shepherd is wont either to thrust simple and unarmed little sheep into the fold, or to lead them out to slaughter. And that which is said, "death shall feed upon them," may have a twofold sense: one, that it be signified that death will be after the manner of a shepherd, and will lead men after the manner of sheep to foul and parched pastures, where they may find nothing but groans and pains; the other, that it be signified that death, after the manner of a hungry wolf, will feed upon and consume impious men after the manner of sheep appointed for food. The former sense agreeth with the Greek word ποιμανεῖ, the latter with the Latin word *depascet* (shall feed upon), and the Hebrew word רעה *raha* very well receiveth both; but in the matter there seemeth to be no discrepancy. For to this at

<!-- p. 324 (PDF 335) -->

length both senses come, that the death of the impious, never to be ended, is signified.

[Margin: Verse 15.]

**15. AND THE JUST SHALL HAVE DOMINION OVER THEM IN THE MORNING; AND THEIR HELP SHALL DECAY IN HELL FROM THEIR GLORY.**

He goeth on to relate the misery of the impious descending to hell, and saith that "in the morning," that is, in the beginning of the new and future world, which shall begin at the general resurrection, they will be altogether subject to the just: for the just at the last day, which will also be the beginning of the world to come, will be the judges of the impious, and will have dominion over them for ever; and there will be nothing which can help the impious on that day against the just: for all "their help," which lay in strength and power, "shall decay," and wither, and become unprofitable in hell, "from their glory," that is, after their glory, which they had in this world, while they dwelt in glorious palaces. That, *in the morning,* showeth that the whole time of this world is a certain night. Whence the Apostle saith, *Rom.* xxiii:[^13] "The night is passed, and the day is at hand." In the night of this world injustice often seemeth to prevail; but in the morning it will appear where the just are, and where the unjust. In the Hebrew the second part of the verse seemeth to be had far otherwise; for thus St. Jerome rendereth: *Et figura eorum conteretur in inferno post habitaculum suum* (And their figure shall be worn away in hell after their habitation): for help is one thing, figure another, and glory one thing, habitation another. But of the first word there is no great difficulty: for the Hebrew word צור *tsur,* although it can signify *figuram* (figure), from the verb יצר *iatsar,* which is *formare* (to form), yet properly *tsur,* which word is had in this place, signifieth *petram* (a rock), and metaphorically *robur* (strength) and *potentiam* (power). Therefore the Seventy Interpreters rightly rendered *auxilium* (help), which consisteth in strength and power. The other word, which in Hebrew is זבול *zebul,* seemeth properly to signify *habitaculum* (a habitation), not any whatsoever, but a glorious one, such as heaven is: for this word is had in two places only, *Isa.* chap. lxiii, 13:[^14] "Look down from heaven, and behold from thy holy habitation and the place of thy glory." And *Hab.* chap. iii, 11: "The sun and the moon stood still in their habitation." In this place therefore the holy Prophet opposeth hell, to wit, a most dark and most vile habitation, to the glorious and splendid houses which the impious possessed in this world. But the Seventy Interpreters rendered *a gloria ipsorum* (from their glory), meaning by glory the glorious houses which the impious exchange for hell; this therefore will be the sense of the whole verse: "And the just shall have dominion over them," to wit, over the impious laid in hell, "in the morning," that is, when the day of the resurrection shall dawn, and the beginning of the world to come; and the power of the impious "shall decay," that is, shall be worn away, and shall be unprofitable, like a garment consumed and torn. "In hell from their glory," that is, when after the glory of splendid habitations they shall begin to have hell, a most foul and most vile habitation, even bodily.

[Margin: Verse 16.]

**16. BUT GOD WILL REDEEM MY SOUL FROM THE HAND OF HELL, WHEN HE SHALL RECEIVE ME.**

[Margin: The descent of Christ into hell is foretold.]

He addeth now what the lot of the godly will be, in whose number he placeth himself, as if he should say: To the impious indeed that will befall which I have said; but to me, and to those like me, the contrary will come to pass. For "God will redeem my soul from the hand of hell," that is, he will deliver me, that I be not detained by hell, when coming he shall take me up. He seemeth in this place to foretell the redemption which is in Christ Jesus, and the descent of the same Christ into hell: for then, the price of his blood being paid, he redeemed from the hand of hell the souls of the just, when descending into hell he received them, and took them up to himself. So the ancient Fathers, Basil, Augustine, Ambrose, Jerome, and Euthymius. Yet this place may be understood with Chrysostom of the lower hell, where the souls of the impious are tormented, so that the sense is: "God will redeem my soul from the hand of hell," that is, God will not suffer my soul to be seized by the hand of hell, nor to be led down to the places of punishment, when he shall receive me from this world by the death of the body.

[Margin: Verse 17.]

**17. BE NOT THOU AFRAID, WHEN A MAN SHALL BE MADE RICH, AND WHEN THE GLORY OF HIS HOUSE SHALL BE INCREASED.**

[Margin: Verse 18.]

**18. FOR WHEN HE SHALL DIE HE SHALL TAKE NOTHING AWAY; NOR SHALL HIS GLORY DESCEND WITH HIM.**[^15]

[Margin: The sorrow of the godly is not true sorrow.]

From what hath been said he concludeth by way of exhortation that the power of the rich of this world is not to be feared, because it is most short: and he addresseth the just who are poor, and oppressed by the rich of this world. "Be not thou afraid," saith he, "when a man shall be made rich," that is, dread not his power, lest for fear of temporal power thou haply offend the eternal omnipotence of the Creator; neither fear thou, if haply thou see not only that the man thy enemy is made rich, but also "the glory of his house increased," through a numerous household, and the manifold dignities of his kinsmen. "For when he shall die," to wit, by death, which no man can escape, and which is common to rich and poor, "he shall not take all things," that is, he shall take nothing, he shall carry away with him nothing of those things which he possessed in this world; "nor shall his glory descend with him," that is, his kinsmen, or his servants, shall not accompany him descending to hell, and much less his honours and dignities. Therefore the riches and glory of the impious last for a very short time; their poverty and confusion are everlasting. But on the contrary the poverty and confusion of the godly are for a moment, their wealth and glory remain for ever and ever; which the Apostle hath beautifully expressed, when he saith, II *Cor.* vi: "As sorrowful, yet always rejoicing." For the sorrow of the godly is not sorrow, but as it were sorrow, such as is that which we sometimes suffer in dreams:

but their gladness is not as it were gladness, but true and solid gladness, now indeed in hope, but afterwards in the reality: and on the contrary, the gladness of the impious from carnal pleasure, or glory, is not gladness, but as it were gladness, such as sleepers enjoy, while they dream that they possess the things which they love. But the sorrow in hell fire will not be as it were sorrow, but most true, most certain, and everlasting sorrow.

[Margin: Verse 19.]

**19. FOR IN HIS LIFETIME HIS SOUL WILL BE BLESSED: AND HE WILL PRAISE THEE WHEN THOU SHALT DO WELL TO HIM.**

He giveth the reason why the glory of the impious doth not accompany them to hell. Because, saith he, as they are wont to praise God when he bestoweth temporal benefits upon them: so God is wont to bless them in this life only. "In his lifetime his soul will be blessed," that is, the soul of the impious man, or the impious man himself, will be blessed in this life only, both by God through the bestowing of temporal benefits, and by men through praises, or rather flatteries. "He will praise thee" (*confitebitur tibi*), that is, he himself also in turn will confess to thee, that is, will praise thee, and give thee thanks, "when thou shalt do well to him;" but if thou permit any evil to befall him, he will straightway blaspheme thee: for he is not of those who say, *Ps.* xxxiii: "I will bless the Lord at all times, his praise shall be always in my mouth;" or *Job* ii:[^16] "The Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away: blessed be the name of the Lord." In the Hebrew there is actively ברך *benedicet* (he will bless), for which we have in the Greek εὐλογηθήσεται, and in the Latin *benedicetur* (will be blessed); but the sense is the same. For thus ought the Hebrew words to be read: *Quia animam ejus in vita ipsius benedicet* (For he will bless his soul in his lifetime), from which we understand that that, *benedicet* (he will bless), is referred to God, and thereby that also, *benedicetur anima ejus* (his soul will be blessed), ought to be referred to God, as we have expounded above. And the sense is the same, whether thou read *anima ejus benedicetur a Deo in vita ipsius* (his soul will be blessed by God in his lifetime), or *animam ejus benedicet Deus in vita ipsius* (God will bless his soul in his lifetime). And that which followeth: "He will praise thee when thou shalt do well to him," in the Hebrew is read *cum benefeceris tibi* (when thou shalt do well to thyself); but from the likeness of the letters the Hebrew text seemeth to be corrupted; for the Seventy Interpreters did not read לך *lac,* which is *tibi* (to thee), but לו *lo,* which is *ei* (to him), and in this latter manner it is read in all the Greek and Latin copies, and the sense is much easier and clearer.

[Margin: Verse 20.]

**20. HE SHALL GO IN TO THE GENERATIONS OF HIS FATHERS: AND HE SHALL NEVER SEE LIGHT.**

The impious man in this life liveth in the light of honours and glory, God filling him with temporal benefits, that he may either reward some good works of his, or allure him to repentance and conversion; but in the other life *he is laid* (as the Scripture speaketh) *unto his fathers,* that he may follow them in punishments whom he followed in enormities; "he shall go in," to wit, the impious man, after this life, "to the generations of his fathers," that is, he shall go in to those places to which the generation of his fathers went before him; "and he shall never see light," because by a just judgment he shall remain in eternal darkness, who, to wit, delighted in the light of honours and of temporal glory, and sought not the light of heavenly glory.

[Margin: Verse 21.]

**21. MAN WHEN HE WAS IN HONOUR DID NOT UNDERSTAND: HE HATH BEEN COMPARED TO SENSELESS BEASTS, AND MADE LIKE TO THEM.**

The Prophet repeateth the twelfth verse, both to show that the chief cause of the misery of men is senselessness, and that deservedly very many men will lack light for ever, and will be in darkness, because they would not use the light of reason; and also, that we may not wonder if very few indeed understand the enigma proposed in this Psalm. For this cometh to pass because "man" by reason of sin "hath been compared to senseless beasts, and made like to them."
