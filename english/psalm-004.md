BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM IV.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*Unto the end, in Hymns. A Psalm for David.*[^1]

This is the title of the fourth Psalm, in which there is a variety between the Hebrew reading and the Greek; for from the Hebrew, as it is now found, St. Jerome translateth *victori* (to the victor), for that which we have from the Greek, *in finem* (unto the end), and St. Jerome is followed by all who interpret the Hebrew word to the word, nor can that word, למנצח *lamnezeach*, be expounded otherwise. This variety hath arisen chiefly from the adding of the points, and from the letter *m*, which perchance came in by the fault of the copyists. For the Seventy Interpreters seem to have read לנצח *lannezeach*, which signifieth *unto the end*, as is plain from the first verse of *Ps.* xii: "How long, O Lord, wilt thou forget me unto the end?" And truly great is the authority of the Seventy Interpreters, of whom it cannot be doubted both that they were most learned, and that they had the best copies. Add, that the meaning also is weightier according to the reading of the Seventy. For according to the Hebrew reading it is signified that this Psalm was given to the Victor in hymns, that is, to the Master of the singers, or to the best singer, who is set over the singing of the hymns. Which thing seemeth not so weighty that it ought to stand in the divine books for an everlasting memory of the matter. But according to the reading of the Seventy Interpreters, it is signified that this Psalm is to be sung even unto the end, that is, perpetually, or very frequently, which is a sign that the Psalm is one of the more profitable. There is added also the mystical understanding, that it is a Psalm pertaining to Christ, who is the end of the Law and of the Prophets. That, *in hymnis* (in hymns), might also be translated: in the playing of

<!-- p. 24 (PDF 35) -->

musical instruments, since נגן *nagan* signifieth an instrument, whence the noun נגינה *neghina*, *a playing*; but it is taken also in many places for praise, or song, and hymn. And so the sense will be: A Psalm of David to be sung among the hymns perpetually, and properly regarding Christ himself; and this same is to be said of the others that follow, inscribed with the same title.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. WHEN I CALLED UPON HIM, THE GOD OF MY JUSTICE HEARD ME: WHEN I WAS IN DISTRESS, THOU HAST ENLARGED ME.**

[Margin: How doth God hear?]

This Psalm David singeth in the person of the Church, or of any faithful soul, which by its own example admonisheth sinners to be converted, and to put their trust in the Lord, to decline from evil, and to do good. He setteth forth therefore his own example, saying: I, when I called upon God in the time of tribulation, was heard. "*For* the God of my justice heard me," that is, the God from whom is all my justice, that is to say, whose grace maketh me just. And he showeth how he was heard, saying: "When I was in distress, thou hast enlarged me." God sometimes heareth by removing the tribulation, sometimes by giving the virtue of patience, and this is a greater benefit; sometimes by giving not patience only, but also joy in tribulation, and this is the greatest benefit, and of this the Psalm speaketh. For tribulation maketh straitness, joy enlargeth hearts: but he who glorieth in tribulation, to him sorrow is turned into joy, and tribulation bringeth him not straitness, but enlargement. Now the change of person from the third to the second is added to signify whence that enlargement cometh, namely, from the entering of God into the heart of him that is in tribulation, according to that: "I am with him in tribulation," *Ps.* xc. And so the sense is: When I called upon God, being pressed by tribulation, he heard me; which I know from this, that thou, O Lord, showing thy most sweet presence to my heart, dost in a wonderful manner turn the straitness of tribulation into the breadth of gladness. "I exceedingly abound with joy," saith the Apostle, "in all our tribulation." Now that, *dilatasti mihi* (thou hast enlarged for me), is a Hebrew phrase for *dilatasti me* (thou hast enlarged me).

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. HAVE MERCY ON ME, AND HEAR MY PRAYER.**

[Margin: This our life is always exposed to dangers.]

He asketh the continuance of grace, as if he said: So, O Lord, mayest thou always have mercy, always hear, as thou hast done hitherto. For the holy Prophet knew that in this life we are always exposed to dangers, unless his mercy not only go before, but also accompany and follow after.

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. O YE SONS OF MEN, HOW LONG WILL YOU BE DULL OF HEART? WHY DO YOU LOVE VANITY, AND SEEK AFTER LYING?**

[Margin: How can the sons of men be called glorious?]

The Hebrew reading differeth much from the Greek, from which our Latin Vulgate is derived; for thus it is read in Hebrew to the word: *Filii viri, usquequo gloriosi mei ad ignominiam diligentes vanitatem, quæritis mendacium?* (Sons of man, how long, my glorious ones, unto shame loving vanity, do ye seek lying?) But it easily appeareth whence the variety hath arisen; for, the points being taken away, which the Hebrew books lacked before the coming of Christ, and one letter being changed into another like it, there ariseth a reading differing in nothing from the Greek. The Hebrew words now sound thus, כבודי לכלימא *chevodi liclimma, gloria mea ad ignominiam* (my glory unto shame). But the Seventy read thus, כבודי לב למה *chevude leb lamma, graves corde usquequo* (heavy of heart how long). And, as we have often said, it is credible that the Hebrew copies were more correct in the times of the Seventy Interpreters than they now are. And so our Vulgate reading is altogether to be kept, seeing that it is found conformable to the Hebrew reading which the Seventy Interpreters had in a more correct state. Nor yet is the disagreement in the sense of great moment; nay, the one reading seemeth to help the other. The meaning therefore is this: "O ye sons of men, how long will you be dull of heart" (*graves corde*, heavy of heart), that is, how long have ye a heart of stone, hard, bowed down to the earth, thinking of nothing but earthly goods? For hearts, according to the saying of the Lord, *Luke* xxi, "are overcharged (*gravantur*) with surfeiting and drunkenness, and the cares of this world";[^2] and because hard hearts receive not thoughts from above, but only earthly and passing ones, therefore they love nothing but earthly and passing things; and since we seek diligently those things only which we love ardently, therefore the Prophet subjoineth: "Why do you love vanity, and seek after lying?" Now earthly goods are called vain, false, because they are neither solid nor stable, although they seem to be such, and for that cause they can deservedly be called false and deceitful, chiefly if they be compared to eternal things. There is added in the Hebrew *sela*, that is, *always*, which is rightly set in this place: for this is greatly to be wondered at, that men not only in the beginning of the world, nor only in the beginning of life, but in every age of the world and in every time of life, after so long experience, pursue human and deceitful things with so great zeal. The Hebrew reading helpeth the Greek and the Vulgate. For the same men who, on account of the corruption of nature, are *sons of men* and *dull of heart*, the same, from the image of the Creator, to which they were formed, can rightly be called glorious and renowned, and therefore it is very shameful for them to love and seek temporal goods, vain and false, which they have under their feet; and not to love, nor seek, the everlasting, solid and true, whence they draw their origin and image. Great is this rebuke, which proceedeth not from a lying man, but from the spirit of truth.

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. KNOW YE ALSO THAT THE LORD HATH MADE HIS HOLY ONE WONDERFUL: THE LORD WILL HEAR ME WHEN I SHALL CRY UNTO HIM.**

This is the chief reason why men ought no longer to cleave to temporal things, namely, because there hath come to us from heaven the Holy One of God, that is, the Son of God, who alone among all men was

<!-- p. 25 (PDF 36) -->

impeccable. Whence also the devil in the Gospel cried out, *Mark* i: "I know who thou art, the Holy One of God." Now this Holy One went about doing good, and enduring evils, despising temporal things, and preaching everlasting things, and at length, walking by this new way, he came to the highest felicity, sitting in heaven, blessed in body and soul for ever. And since he is our leader, and hath gone before to prepare a place for us, without doubt, if we walk in his footsteps, we shall come to true and everlasting felicity. Know ye therefore that the Lord God hath made his holy one, Christ, because he despised temporal things, altogether wonderful, raising him from hell, and setting him in heaven at his right hand. And since he is not only our leader, but also our advocate and mediator, therefore David subjoineth: "The Lord will hear me when I shall cry unto him," that is, now above all I surely trust that I shall always be heard, since I know that there is at the right hand of God a mediator, who maketh intercession for us.

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. BE ANGRY, AND SIN NOT: THE THINGS YOU SAY IN YOUR HEARTS, BE SORRY FOR THEM UPON YOUR BEDS.**

[Margin: Anger is given us, that it may be the whetstone of virtue.]

After a most grave rebuke and an admonition that the sons of men come to a better mind, the Holy Ghost instructeth them what they ought to do, and teacheth them to be kindled with a certain holy anger, and to resist evil desires, and in that manner to shun sin: but if perchance they have sinned, to grieve forthwith and be sorry, and not to be content to decline from evil, but also to offer up the sacrifice of justice by doing good. "Be angry," saith he, "and sin not," that is, when evil and rebellious motions arise, which are the beginnings and roots of all sins, be angry with your corrupt nature, and resist, so that ye sin not. For to this end was anger given us by God (as St. Basil teacheth in his Oration *on Anger*), that it may be the whetstone of virtue. The Hebrew word רגזו *rigzu* signifieth also *fear ye, tremble ye*, which also fitteth this place rightly. For we ought at once to tremble and fear when the head of the serpent appeareth, that is, an evil suggestion. "The things you say in your hearts, be sorry for them upon your beds." In the Hebrew we read to the word: *Dicite in cordibus vestris super cubilia vestra, et tacete* (Say in your hearts upon your beds, and be silent); but in the Greek thus: *Quæ dicitis in cordibus super cubilia vestra, compungimini* (The things you say in your hearts upon your beds, be sorry for them); from which we understand that our reading is to be ordered thus: The things you say in your hearts upon your beds, for these be sorry in silence. The sense therefore is: in the night time, when ye are alone upon your beds, free from all other cares, then in your hearts think over all your faults, and the things which ye say and confess within yourselves in the heart, for these before God, being silent for shame, be sorry. Now that David was wont to think over his sins upon his bed, on account of the fitness of the secrecy and silence of the night, can be understood from those words of *Ps.* vi: "Every night I will wash my bed: I will water my couch with my tears." And so what he himself did, he admonisheth others to do.

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. OFFER UP THE SACRIFICE OF JUSTICE, AND TRUST IN THE LORD: MANY SAY, WHO SHEWETH US GOOD THINGS?**

He now addeth the other part of justice, namely, that they should not only decline from evil, but also do good. And he calleth good works the sacrifice of justice, because just works please God greatly, and by them God is honoured. "That they may see," saith the Lord, "your good works, and glorify your Father who is in heaven," *Matt.* vi.[^3] The Apostle, speaking of alms: "I have received," saith he, "the things you sent, an odour of sweetness"; and of fasting and other bodily good works, *Ephes.* v: "I beseech you," saith he, "that you present your bodies a sacrifice well pleasing."[^4] And he addeth: "And trust in the Lord," to take away presumption, which lieth in wait for good works: we must work well, yet so that we trust not proudly in our own works, as that Pharisee who said, *Luke* xviii: "I give thee thanks that I am not as the rest of men: I fast twice in a week," etc. But let us hope in the Lord, who will grant us to shun sins, to do good works, and to come to the haven of salvation. For as presumption is a poison corrupting the merit of good works, so humility, distrusting its own strength, and placing its hope in the Lord, is as a salt seasoning and preserving all things rightly done. "Many say, Who sheweth us good things?" In the Hebrew copies a new verse beginneth in this place; but in the Greek and the Latin these words pertain to the verse above: but it is not a variety of great moment. The Prophet setteth down in this place the common objection of carnal men; and because the number of fools is infinite, therefore he saith: "Many say," that is, when we preach the contempt of temporal things, and exhort to innocence and justice, very many say, Who will shew us good things, if these which we see and touch are not good? Who hath returned from hell? Who hath ascended into heaven? By that word, *good things*, justice and uprightness can also be understood; for because he had said: "the sacrifice of justice," the wicked object: What is this justice? who will shew us the way of justice?

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. THE LIGHT OF THY COUNTENANCE, O LORD, IS SIGNED UPON US: THOU HAST GIVEN GLADNESS IN MY HEART.**

The Prophet answereth that the way of justice hath been shown us by God, and that we have a master within us to teach us the true good, namely, the light of natural reason: for this light is signed, and impressed indelibly, upon us, that is, in the highest part of man. For we have a lower part, the body, a higher part, the soul: and in the soul itself a lower part, by which we live and feel; and a higher, by which

<!-- p. 26 (PDF 37) -->

we reason and are wise. In this highest part, therefore, is the light by which we are distinguished from the beasts; and this light is derived from the countenance of God, because it maketh us to be to the image and likeness of God. And from this light we can understand, first, the way of justice: for the natural law written in the heart, which not even iniquity itself blotteth out, teacheth that we must not do to another what we would not have done to us by others; and so that we must not steal, must not commit adultery, etc. We can also understand, being helped by the grace of God, that our true good consisteth in being made like unto God: for the good of an image is to be as like as may be to its exemplar. And from this thought there ariseth in the will a mighty gladness, hope and love toward God. For what is sweeter than to think that one is the living image of a thing of infinite beauty, and that one is most dear to one's almighty exemplar? But because not all have experience of this, therefore David saith not: Thou hast given gladness in their hearts; but "Thou hast given gladness in my heart," which same thing all the just and godly can say. Now it mattereth little that in the Hebrew it is: *Signa super nos lumen vultus tui* (Sign upon us the light of thy countenance); and we have: *Signatum est super nos lumen vultus tui* (The light of thy countenance is signed upon us); for the same letters, the points being changed, make *signatum est* (is signed). And this we believe to be the true reading, because the Seventy Interpreters knew better how those letters were to be read than the later Rabbins, who added the points.

[Margin: The good of an image is to be as like as may be to its exemplar.]

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**8. BY THE FRUIT OF THEIR CORN, THEIR WINE AND OIL, THEY ARE MULTIPLIED.**

The Prophet addeth another argument from which men can understand that God is the author of all goods, namely, because it is he who in their seasons multiplieth the fruits of the earth, and bringeth forth all fruits. So also Blessed Paul, *Acts* xiv: "He left not himself without testimony, giving rains and fruitful seasons, filling our hearts with food and gladness." "By the fruit," saith he, "of their corn, their wine and oil, they are multiplied," that is, they are increased, or have grown, in temporal goods, through the abundance chiefly of those three fruits. For the Greek word ἐπληθύνθησαν, which the Interpreter rendered *multiplicati sunt* (they are multiplied), signifieth also *they are increased*, or *have grown*, and that it is so to be expounded the Hebrew text also teacheth, where we have to the word, *frumentum et vinum eorum multiplicata sunt* (their corn and wine are multiplied). It mattereth little also that in the Hebrew there is no mention of oil: for either that word hath fallen out, or the Seventy Interpreters added it for the sake of explanation. For Scripture is wont in many places to join these three together.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. IN PEACE IN THE SELFSAME I WILL SLEEP, AND I WILL REST.**

David concludeth the Psalm, saying: Whatever may be the case of the others, whom I have exhorted to friendship with God, I for my part will altogether trust in God, and wholly rest in him. "I," saith he, "in peace," that is, with tranquillity of mind; "in the selfsame," that is, jointly, alike, together; "will sleep, and will rest," that is, I will lie down secure to sleep, and in very deed I will sleep most quietly. For the words, both Hebrew and Greek, for which we have *dormiam et requiescam* (I will sleep, and I will rest), signify properly *I will lie*, or *I will lie down, and sleep*. But our Interpreter hath not ill translated *dormiam et requiescam*, because he who lieth down that he may sleep can be said to sleep; and he who sleepeth, chiefly with a calm sleep, can be said to rest. These things can also be referred to the rest to come in heaven: for then truly there will be the highest peace from union with God, and the highest and truest rest. That *in idipsum* (in the selfsame), which occurreth very frequently in the Psalms, is in the Hebrew יחדו *iachdau*, which St. Jerome and others translate *pariter* (alike), or *simul* (together); the Seventy Interpreters everywhere render ἐπὶ τὸ αὐτό, that is, *in idipsum*: but the sense is the same; for *in idipsum* is the same as jointly and together. St. Augustine by *idipsum* (the selfsame) understandeth God, who alone is always the selfsame, eternal and unchangeable, according to that of *Ps.* ci: "But thou art always the selfsame": an acute and godly interpretation, but mystical, not literal.

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. FOR THOU, O LORD, SINGULARLY HAST SETTLED ME IN HOPE.**

He giveth the cause why he will cast all his care upon God, or why he said that in the world to come he would sleep and rest in peace. For the reason is, that God by his most true and most faithful promises hath settled him in hope alone, that is, hath made him to dwell there. For this the Hebrew and the Greek word signifieth. And so the just man and friend of God dwelleth in divine hope alone, as in a most safe house; he doth what he can for the present life and for that to come: yet he trusteth not in his own strength, nor in any created thing, but in God alone, and therefore he is not confounded; he sleepeth secure, and shall sleep secure in the world to come.

[^2]: Translator's note: so Bellarmine quotes it (*hujus mundi*). The Vulgate at Luke xxi, 34 reads *curis hujus vitæ*, and the Douay-Rheims "the cares of this life".

[^3]: Translator's note: the words are at Matt. v, 16. The reference to chapter vi is given as printed.

[^4]: Translator's note: the words are those of Rom. xii, 1, where the Vulgate reads *hostiam viventem, sanctam, Deo placentem*, and the Douay-Rheims "a living sacrifice, holy, pleasing unto God". The reference to Ephes. v is given as printed.
