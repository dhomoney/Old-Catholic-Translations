BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XXXII.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*A psalm for David.*

This Psalm hath no title among the Hebrews. But the words, *A psalm for David,* were set there by the Seventy Interpreters, who nevertheless gave warning in express words that they had found no title in the Hebrew copies. The argument of the Psalm is an exhortation to just men, that they praise God for his exceeding great power, providence and goodness: and he exhorteth the just, both because praise is not seemly in the mouth of a sinner, and because the power, providence and goodness of God is most profitable to the just themselves above all.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. REJOICE IN THE LORD, O YE JUST: PRAISE BECOMETH THE UPRIGHT.**

The word *exultatio* (rejoicing), which is set here, signifieth praise with gladness. St. Jerome translateth: *Laudate, justi, Dominum* (Praise the Lord, ye just); but the Hebrew word רננו *rannenu* signifieth both; therefore the plain sense is: Praise with rejoicing, that is, praise not unwillingly, not sadly, not negligently, but with great affection, shouting for joy, rejoicing in your hearts; praise the Lord not with the mind only, but also with the voice. He giveth the reason when he addeth: "Praise becometh the upright," as though he said: You, the just, I invite above all to praise God, because it belongeth to you above all to praise God. By *the upright* he understandeth the just, as we said also at the end of the foregoing Psalm. But it is fitting in this place that the just be called upright, because God, who is uprightness itself, pleaseth the upright, and that which pleaseth is gladly praised. God pleaseth not the crooked, for his works and judgments are most upright, and therefore square not with the wills of men of iniquity: hence men of iniquity and the crooked do not gladly praise God, but find fault with him and blaspheme. For the verb *decet* (becometh), the Hebrew hath the noun נאוה *navah,* seemly, and the sense is: in the mouth of the upright the praise of God is seemly; and to this Ecclesiasticus alluded, chap. xv, when he saith: "Praise is not seemly in the mouth of a sinner." For the word *collaudatio* (praise), the Hebrew hath תהלה *thehillah,* and the Greek, αἶνος, *laus* (praise); but the Latin Interpreter put *collaudatio* (praising together), to signify that the just ought to praise God not only one by one, but also jointly.

[Margin: God, who is uprightness itself, pleaseth the upright.]

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. GIVE PRAISE TO THE LORD ON THE HARP; SING TO HIM WITH THE PSALTERY, THE INSTRUMENT OF TEN STRINGS.**

He exhorteth the just again to confess to the Lord with the confession of praise, not with the voice only, but also with the instruments of music which were in use among that nation. And mystically it is signified that we should praise God not with words only, but also with works, and chiefly with the perfect keeping of the decalogue, which is signified by the psaltery of ten strings, that "other men, seeing our good works, may glorify our Father who is in heaven," *Matt.* vi.[^1] Basil and Augustine note in this place that the harp soundeth from the lower part, the psaltery from the upper. Whence it is gathered that God is to be praised from the knowledge of God which is had from creatures: whence ariseth the sound of the harp; and from that which is had from divine revelation in the holy Scriptures: whence ariseth the sound of the Psaltery.

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. SING TO HIM A NEW CANTICLE, SING WELL UNTO HIM WITH A LOUD NOISE.**

He joineth together, as it were by an epilogue, the things which he had said in the two verses above. He had said that we should praise with the voice and play upon instruments: he joineth both, and warneth that all be done exactly and diligently. "Sing to him a new canticle;" this belongeth to the words: "Rejoice in the Lord," etc. And he biddeth that, rejoicing, we praise God with some canticle not customary and old, but newly composed and fitted to the present matter. "Sing well with a loud noise," belongeth to that which was added: "Give praise on the harp," etc., and he biddeth that this same thing be done well and exactly, and not coldly and with a low voice, but with a great sound, whereby the greatness of the affection may be signified. Therefore the words, *in vociferatione* (with a loud noise), ought not to be referred to the voices of men, but to the voices of the instruments of music. St. Jerome translateth, *in jubilo* (with a shout of joy), and St. Augustine readeth, *in jubilatione* (with jubilation). The Hebrew word בתרועה signifieth *jubilation;* the Greek word ἀλαλαγμῷ *a redoubled cry.* The holy Fathers Basil and Augustine rightly observe that the new canticle is set against the old canticle. The old canticle is that which the old man is wont to sing, who, "born of the flesh, is flesh," and mindeth earthly things, and delighteth in earthly things, and he praiseth God when he receiveth temporal goods from him: but the new canticle is sung by the new man, who, being renewed in the spirit of his mind, mindeth heavenly things, and delighteth in things eternal, and praiseth God always, even when he is scourged, because he knoweth that those things also work together unto good. So also by the words: *Sing well unto him with a loud noise,* we are admonished, while we sing psalms,

[Margin: What the new canticle is, and the old.]

to do this divine work well, attentively, devoutly, with great affection and jubilation of heart. St. Benedict in his rule warneth that psalmody is the work of God, and therefore to be set before all other works. And St. Bernard, sermon 47 *on the Canticles:* I admonish you, dearly beloved, saith he, to be present at the divine praises always purely and strenuously. Strenuously indeed, that as ye stand before the Lord reverently, so ye stand also cheerfully, not slothful, not drowsy, not yawning, not sparing your voices, not cutting off half the words, not leaping over whole ones, not with broken and slack voices sounding somewhat womanish with a stammering through the nose, but with manly sound and affection, as is meet, bringing forth the words of the Holy Ghost.

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. FOR THE WORD OF THE LORD IS RIGHT, AND ALL HIS WORKS ARE DONE WITH FAITHFULNESS.**

He beginneth to explain the reasons why God is to be praised with so great affection, which are taken, as we said, from his goodness, power and wisdom. Of his goodness he saith: "The word of the Lord is right, and all his works are done with faithfulness" (*in fide*), that is, because the words and deeds of the Lord are most just, most faithful and most holy. This is that which is said in other words in *Ps.* cxliv: "The Lord is faithful in all his words: and holy in all his works." By the word of the Lord is understood commandment, prohibition, promise, threatening, and all these are most right and most faithful: for God commandeth nothing but good, forbiddeth nothing but evil, and what he promiseth or threateneth, he most faithfully performeth. Therefore "the word of the Lord is right, and the Lord is faithful in all his words," *Ps.* cxxxiv.[^2] And the works of the Lord are conformable to his words: therefore they are said to be *in fide* (in faith), that is, faithful, just, holy, and God is said to be *holy in all his works.* St. Basil, and St. Augustine in his commentary on this Psalm, understand by faith in this place the Catholic faith, and expound that the works of God are said to be *in fide,* because by the works of God, as by beholding the fabric of heaven and earth, men are moved to faith, namely that they believe in God, the maker of so great a work; or they say that all the works of God are *in fide,* because all good works which please God are in faith as in the seed, or the root: for the things which are done before faith please not God, but those which spring from faith. Both are pious and true opinions; but they seem not to be literal explanations.

[Margin: God commandeth nothing but good, forbiddeth nothing but evil.]

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. HE LOVETH MERCY AND JUDGMENT; THE EARTH IS FULL OF THE MERCY OF THE LORD.**

The holiness of the words and works of the Lord springeth from the holiness of his will: "*For* God loveth mercy and judgment," that is, he loveth first to bestow the gifts of his grace, and afterwards to render rewards or punishments according to the works of every one, and so all the ways of the Lord are mercy and truth. The Hebrew hath: *Diligit justitiam et judicium* (He loveth justice and judgment); but the Seventy Interpreters, knowing that those two words, צדק and חסד, that is, *justice and mercy,* are used the one for the other, often translate *justice,* where they read *chesed;* and sometimes (as in this place) they translate *mercy,* where they read *tsedek;* but if we would interpret the Hebrew word as it soundeth, it must be said that in the first part of the verse the goodness of the divine will is declared on the side of justice; in the other part, namely "the earth is full of the mercy of the Lord," the same goodness is declared on the side of mercy. But according to our reading it must be said that in the first part the goodness is declared on the side of mercy and justice, and in the second it is taught that mercy reacheth more widely than justice, and is, as is said elsewhere, *Ps.* cxviii:[^3] "Over all his works;" for it belongeth to mercy to take away misery and defect: and there is nothing which needeth not the mercy of God, since all created things whatsoever suffer some defect. But corruptible things, which are beneath the moon, suffer so many more and greater defects than the incorruptible things which are above the moon, that in respect of things corruptible the incorruptible seem to have no defect; therefore the Prophet saith: "The earth is full of the mercy of the Lord:" for by the earth are understood all corruptible things; for the earth is the seat not only of mortal men, and of the beasts of the earth, and of all plants, but also of fishes and birds: for although the birds fly in the air, and the fishes pass through the paths of the sea, yet both those and these rest on the earth. Moreover all corruptible things need the manifold mercy of God, creating, preserving, moving, nourishing, repairing. But mortal men, beside all these, need mercy preventing, accompanying, following, pardoning sins, arming against the devils, directing, protecting, etc. Most rightly therefore is it said: "The earth is full of the mercy of the Lord." In this place this also is to be considered, that perfect mercy, to which it belongeth to take away all defects, is proper to God alone, because none can take away all defects but he who is without all defect, and thereby is of infinite perfection, pure act, eternal, almighty; rightly therefore the Church singeth: "O God, whose property it is to have mercy."

[Margin: There is nothing which needeth not the mercy of God.]

[Margin: Perfect mercy belongeth to God alone.]

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. BY THE WORD OF THE LORD THE HEAVENS WERE ESTABLISHED; AND ALL THE POWER OF THEM BY THE SPIRIT OF HIS MOUTH.**

He passeth from the praise of goodness to the praise of power, of which the first and most conspicuous effect is the fabric of heaven. And it heighteneth the power of God, that he not only made so great a mass, but made it without labour, without delay of time, without ministers, without engines, by a word alone, and gave to the heavens an everlasting stability. And the Prophet alludeth to the creation of the world, which is described in chap. i of *Gen.* by these words: "God said: Let there be a firmament made, and a firmament was made, and he called the firmament Heaven."[^4] The Hebrew hath, *verbo Domini cœli facti sunt* (by the word of the Lord the heavens were made); but the Interpreters translated, *firmati sunt* (were established), to declare that the heavens were made incorruptible.

<!-- p. 195 (PDF 206) -->

That which followeth, *and all the power of them by the spirit of his mouth,* might seem a kind of repetition. For *the word* and *the spirit of the mouth* are almost the same, and the firmness of the heavens and the power of the heavens might be taken for the same, were there not in the Hebrew the word צבאם, which signifieth *exercitus eorum* (their army), or *ornatus eorum* (their adornment), as St. Jerome translateth. The Seventy Interpreters take power and army for the same, because in truth an army of soldiers is the *virtus* of a prince, that is, his power and might. By the power, or army, or adornment of heaven are understood the stars, which are as it were an army of the heavenly camps set in array, and at once wonderfully adorn the heaven, and pour forth their powers and divers influences upon these lower things. And although by *the word of the Lord* and *the spirit of his mouth* the command of the Lord may simply be taken, yet without doubt the Holy Ghost willed by these words to intimate the mystery of the most holy Trinity, which was to be revealed in the time of the new Testament. Nor ought it to trouble us that the Prophet seemeth to attribute the creation of heaven to the Word, and the creation of the stars to the Holy Ghost, as though the Lord the Father had made the heavens by the Word, and the stars by the Holy Ghost. For by reason of the unity of the essence, which is the working power, the works of the Trinity are undivided; and therefore when God the Father is said to have created the heavens by the Word, it is understood that he did it not without the Holy Ghost: when the power, or army, of the heavens is said to be from the Spirit of the mouth of the Lord, it is understood to be also from the Word, which proceedeth from the mouth of the same Father, and from which Word the Spirit himself proceedeth.

[Margin: The mystery of the most glorious Trinity here shadowed forth.]

[Margin: The works of the holy Trinity are undivided.]

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. GATHERING TOGETHER THE WATERS OF THE SEA, AS IN A VESSEL; LAYING UP THE DEPTHS IN STOREHOUSES.**

He goeth on to explain the power of God, who not only made the heavens and the stars by the Word, but also drove together all the waters, which in the beginning of the creation of the world covered the earth on every side, into the lower and deeper places of the earth, with the same ease with which a man filleth a single vessel[^5] with water, or layeth up money in a chest or in purses. "Gathering together the waters of the sea, as in a vessel," that is, God is gathering together, or gathereth together, the waters of the sea into one place, as though he were filling a vessel, that is, most easily and without any trouble. The same is repeated in the words following: "Laying up the depths in storehouses," that is, God layeth up the depths, namely the multitude and deepness of the waters which were above the earth and reached even unto heaven; he layeth up, I say, all that immensity of waters in the narrow caverns of the earth, as though he were laying up money in storehouses. That the deep is the multitude of waters which in the beginning of the creation was above the earth, is manifest from chap. i of *Gen.:* "And darkness was upon the face of the deep." By *thesauri* (storehouses, treasures) is signified sometimes abundance of precious things, of gold, silver, precious stones, as in that place: "The kingdom of heaven is like unto a treasure hidden in a field;" sometimes by *thesaurus* is signified the place of such precious things, as a chest or purses, as in those words: "Every scribe instructed in the kingdom of heaven bringeth forth out of his treasure new things and old," *Matt.* xiii; and of the Magi we read: "Opening their treasures, they offered him gifts; gold, frankincense, and myrrh." In this latter signification *thesaurus* is taken in this place. And the Prophet saith in the present tense *congregans* (gathering together) and *ponens* (laying up), although these things were done from the beginning, because God always so preserveth what he made in the beginning, that he may truly be said to do it always. For *vessel,* the Hebrew hath נד *ned,* which signifieth *a heap;* but the Seventy and St. Jerome read נאד *nod,* which signifieth a vessel. And this is the truer reading, and better explaineth the gathering together of the waters into one place, that is, into the caverns of the earth, which the Prophet meant to signify. In the Hebrew and the Greek the preposition *in* is wanting, and it is read: *Congregans sicut utrem aquas maris* (Gathering together the waters of the sea as a vessel); but it is altogether to be understood, and our edition rightly hath, *sicut in utre* (as in a vessel). Nor were there wanting of old Greek copies which had, *in utrem* (into a vessel), as St. Basil noteth; and St. Augustine always readeth *in utrem.* They who read, *sicut utrem,* think that the clouds are signified, which swell like such vessels, and after they have poured forth, sink again: but the literal sense is that which we have set down above.

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. LET ALL THE EARTH FEAR THE LORD, AND LET ALL THE INHABITANTS OF THE WORLD BE IN AWE OF HIM.**

Taking occasion from the things which have been said of the power of God, David exhorteth men to fear God, and to dread to transgress his commandments. "Let therefore, saith he, all the earth fear the Lord" of so great power and majesty; and forthwith he explaineth the same, saying: "Let all the inhabitants of the world (that is, all the earth) be in awe" (*commoveantur*), that is, let them tremble, of him, namely of the Lord when he commandeth anything.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. FOR HE SPOKE AND THEY WERE MADE: HE COMMANDED AND THEY WERE CREATED.**

"For he spoke and they were made: he commanded and they were created." An excellent reason why God alone is to be feared, namely because whatsoever is outside God can do us no hurt, unless God permit; and on the other hand, there is nothing outside God which can defend us from his anger: for all things depend on his beck, from whom they have their being. And the reason why God made all things by a word is, because his word is full of power and authority, so that it is a commandment and a command, which nothing shall resist: therefore it is added: "He commanded and they were created." The Hebrew hath to the word, הוא אמר ויהי, *ipse dixit et fuit, ipse mandavit et stetit* (he spoke and it was, he commanded and it stood). But the sense is the same; for the Hebrew words signify: "he spoke," and every creature was made; "he commanded," and every creature began to exist.

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. THE LORD BRINGETH TO NAUGHT THE COUNSELS OF NATIONS; AND HE REJECTETH THE DEVICES OF PEOPLE, AND CASTETH AWAY THE COUNSELS OF PRINCES.**

The Prophet cometh at length to wisdom, that he may show that God is to be praised on every side:

<!-- p. 196 (PDF 207) -->

"The Lord bringeth to naught the counsels of nations," that is, the wisdom of God so far surpasseth all the wisdom of all men, that whatsoever men determine in taking counsel, although their counsels seem most wise and most prudent, God can in a moment make foolish, undo, and make void. The same is repeated in the words following, when it is said: "And he rejecteth the devices of people," that is, God rejecteth as foolish the devices of all peoples: and not of peoples only, but of the princes themselves also, who abound in counsellors in every kind of learning, he casteth away the counsels and deliberations as plainly foolish and unprofitable. And truly wonderful is the wisdom of God, which catcheth the wise in their craftiness, and in an unspeakable manner sometimes so maketh their counsels foolish, that the things which they judge most profitable to themselves bring upon them both ruin and destruction. The Hebrew hath not the words, *et reprobat consilia principum* (and casteth away the counsels of princes): perhaps they have fallen out by the injury of time, or by the carelessness of copyists.

[Margin: The wisdom of God is wonderful.]

[Margin: Verse 11.]

**11. BUT THE COUNSEL OF THE LORD STANDETH FOR EVER: THE THOUGHTS OF HIS HEART TO ALL GENERATIONS.**

The Lord indeed by his unsearchable wisdom undoeth the counsels of men, that they attain not the end which they set before them. But on the other hand, the prudence of men can do nothing at all against the counsel of God: for whatsoever God hath once decreed shall be stable for ever. "All my counsel shall stand, and all my will shall be done, saith the Lord," *Isa.* xlv.[^6] Now the counsel of God is not a certain consultation going before choice; nor are there many thoughts in God, but by one only and most simple act God decreed from eternity whatsoever he is ever to do. But the Scripture fitteth itself to our weakness and useth our words, therefore it saith: "The counsel of the Lord standeth for ever," that is, what God hath decreed as it were by taking counsel can in no wise be undone, nor hindered from being put in execution. The same is repeated in other words, when it is added: "The thoughts of his heart to all generations," that is, the things which the Lord hath thought to do shall at no time be hindered, but shall be done altogether in that manner in which he himself willed them to be done. Yet the Scripture doth not so fit itself to our infirmity, that there is not truth in its words: for although there is not in God a counsel going before choice, yet there is in God whatsoever of perfection is found in counsel, namely the knowledge of all the means profitable for attaining the end; and although in God there is one and most simple thought, yet it is equal to thoughts without number, which it containeth in itself in a most eminent manner.

[Margin: Of what kind the counsel of the Lord is.]

[Margin: Verse 12.]

**12. BLESSED IS THE NATION WHOSE GOD IS THE LORD: THE PEOPLE WHOM HE HATH CHOSEN FOR HIS INHERITANCE.**

From the things which have been said of the power, wisdom and goodness of God, the Prophet gathereth that blessed is the people whose God is not some unprofitable idol, but that most mighty, most wise, and most good Lord, whose praises have been explained; and then are we truly and perfectly happy and blessed, when both we have that great Lord for our God, and he hath us for his peculiar people; therefore the Prophet joineth both, when he saith: "Blessed is the nation whose God is the Lord," that is, blessed are those men who acknowledge for God none but that one Lord, "by whose word the heavens were established," etc.; and withal: "Blessed is the people whom the Lord hath chosen for his inheritance," that is, blessed are those men whom that same great Lord hath chosen for a peculiar people, and as it were his own possession and inheritance. And these two are so knit together that they cannot be parted; for they alone have the true God for their God, who worship him with faith, and hope, and charity: and they alone worship God with faith, and hope, and charity, whom he himself hath chosen for his inheritance, whom by grace he hath foreordained, called, and justified. A sentence altogether true, and to be laid up in the bottom of the heart: for whatsoever a man hath, although he gain the whole world, he is needy and miserable if he lack God, who alone can fill the whole bosom of the soul; and on the other hand he who hath God, although he lack other things, is blessed and rich, because in God are all things. Besides, man is the Image of God; the good and the whole perfection of an image is to be most like its exemplar: and then shall we be truly like God, and thereby most blessed, when "we shall see him as he is," as is said in I *John* iii; for God himself also is blessed for this cause, that he seeth himself as he is: they therefore who shall never see God shall be always unlike God, and thereby always most miserable. Lastly, whatsoever is beneath God is either meaner than man, as all bodily things; or equal, as the angels, to whom we shall be equal in the resurrection: but nothing can perfect us and make us happy and blessed but that which is better and more perfect than we: they alone therefore are truly blessed who cleave to God, and are made one spirit with him, that is, who love God, and are loved by God, who here indeed meanwhile are blessed in hope, but shall be blessed in very deed, when they shall cleave to God by so happy a bond that they can by no means fall away from him.

[Margin: Whatsoever a man hath, if he lack God, he is needy and miserable.]

[Margin: Verse 13.]

**13. THE LORD HATH LOOKED FROM HEAVEN: HE HATH BEHELD ALL THE SONS OF MEN.**

[Margin: Verse 14.]

**14. FROM HIS HABITATION WHICH HE HATH PREPARED, HE HATH LOOKED UPON ALL THAT DWELL ON THE EARTH.**

He proveth what he said, that blessed is the nation whose God is that Lord who made heaven: and he proveth it from this, that whereas the Lord from heaven, as from a most high watch-tower, seeth men, and knoweth that no man can be saved by his own strength, though otherwise he seem most strong or most mighty; yet God with a fatherly eye looketh in a special manner upon his own people, and

<!-- p. 197 (PDF 208) -->

helpeth and saveth it, so that it was deservedly said in the beginning of the Psalm: "Rejoice in the Lord, O ye just." He saith therefore: "The Lord hath looked from heaven: he hath beheld all the sons of men," (or, as it is in the Hebrew, בני האדם, *vidit omnes filios Adam* (he hath beheld all the sons of Adam)), that is, the Lord from heaven, from whom nothing can be hidden, seeth not only his own people, but seeth all men, and understandeth what each one can do. And the words following signify the same: "From his habitation which he hath prepared," that is, from the seat which he himself hath prepared and established for himself (for "the heaven of heaven is the Lord's: but the earth he has given to the children of men," as it is in *Ps.* cxiii), "he hath looked" and looketh upon all the inhabitants of the earth. St. Jerome translateth from the Hebrew, *de firmissimo solio suo* (from his most firm throne), for that which we have, *de præparato habitaculo suo* (from his prepared habitation): but the Hebrew words admit either signification.

[Margin: Verse 15.]

**15. HE WHO HATH MADE THE HEARTS OF EVERY ONE OF THEM: WHO UNDERSTANDETH ALL THEIR WORKS.**

The Prophet declareth that God did not behold the sons of men from heaven in that manner in which we see things set far off, namely in a confused and gross manner; but that he beheld distinctly and most exactly all their works, that is, what they are able to work, or do work, whether with mind or with body. He beheld therefore all the thoughts, all the desires, all the words, all the deeds, past, present and to come, of all and of every man. And he proveth that God was able to see these things, because he "formed the hearts of every one," that is, he created the souls of every man, and thereby created also the hearts, that is, the minds and wills, from which all the works of men arise: for he who was able to make hearts, the same without doubt is able to search hearts. The Hebrew hath, *qui finxit* יחד *iachad,* that is *pariter, corda eorum* (who hath made their hearts alike); but the word *pariter* (alike) doth not signify *at the same time,* as Origen and others erring believed. For St. Leo, in his epistle *to Turibius,* Bishop of Astorga, chap. x, teacheth that the Christian faith holdeth that souls were not before bodies. Therefore the word *alike* is not referred to the same time, but to the same author; as though the Prophet had said: Who hath made all hearts, none excepted: for he did not himself form one heart, and leave the others to be formed by others, but he alike made all, he alone made all. The Greek hath[^7] κατὰ μόνας, that is *seorsim* (apart), or *solitarie* (alone); which St. Basil referreth to the purity and simplicity of the heart, so that the sense is: who hath made hearts separated from all impurity; or, as Euthymius addeth, separated from the nature of other living creatures. But the ancient Latin Interpreter seemeth to have understood more rightly, who translated *singillatim* (one by one), that is, each one apart: for this agreeth better with the Hebrew reading, and with the words following; for the Prophet saith that God formed all hearts, that he may thence gather that God understandeth all their works; and this reason requireth that God formed all and every heart, not that he separated them from impurity, or from other living creatures. From this place is gathered also that dogma of the Church, namely that God did not create all souls as it were in a confused mass, when he created the soul of the first man, as though all were in that soul, and all are made from it, in the manner in which he did in truth then create after a sort all men, because all were in one, and all are made from one, as to the generation of the flesh; but God created all souls one by one, not one from another, but himself each one out of nothing, distinctly and separately. I know that St. Augustine, in *epist.* 28, doth not think that the creation of souls is solidly proved from this place, because in *Ps.* l it is said: "Create a clean heart in me, O God." But it may be answered that in *Ps.* l sanctification is treated of, not natural creation: but that this place requireth that we understand the words before us of natural creation, since it is thence gathered that God searcheth all hearts, because he himself formed all.

[Margin: Souls were not before bodies.]

[Margin: Verse 16.]

**16. THE KING IS NOT SAVED BY A GREAT ARMY: NOR SHALL THE GIANT BE SAVED BY HIS OWN GREAT STRENGTH.**

He explaineth what he saw, who seeth all things: namely he saw this, that no one can be delivered by his own strength from the evils which stand round about us, but that all need the mercy of God: and he setteth the example in those who seem most able to glory in their own strength, as though he said: God saw that "the king is not saved by a great army" (*per multam virtutem*), that is, great strength, that is great power, a great army, great store of money, sufficeth not a king to defend his own safety or life. "Nor shall the giant be saved by his own great strength," that is, as the might of others who serve him sufficeth not a king, so his own might sufficeth not a most strong man. The Hebrew hath not the word *gigas* (giant), but in general the word for *a mighty* and *strong man;* but the Seventy Interpreters put the word *giant,* because giants are wont to be most strong, and terrible to other men, as we read of the giant Goliath in I *Kings,* chap. xvii.

[Margin: Verse 17.]

**17. VAIN IS THE HORSE FOR SAFETY: NEITHER SHALL HE BE SAVED BY THE ABUNDANCE OF HIS STRENGTH.**

There are three things which can snatch a man from instant danger, the strength of others, as guards of soldiers, his own strength, the swiftness of a horse: those, if he must resist; this, if he must flee. The Psalmist had taught that the two former suffice not; now he addeth that neither doth the third suffice, and we have an example of all in the books of *Kings.* For huge forces of soldiers could not defend king Saul from death; the boy David slew Goliath, a most mighty giant; Joram the son of Achab, fleeing in a swift chariot, a swifter arrow slew. "Vain (*fallax*, deceitful) is the horse for safety," that is, he is deceived who trusteth his safety to the swiftness of horses: for that swiftness can in many ways be hindered, or forestalled, and therefore is deceitful. "By the abundance of his strength

<!-- p. 198 (PDF 209) -->

he shall not be saved," that is, the horse by his strength, wherein he aboundeth, or even if he abound therein exceedingly, which strength consisteth in swiftness, shall not be saved with his rider, that is, he shall deliver neither himself nor his rider from danger. The Hebrew hath it actively, *non salvabit* (he shall not save), namely his rider; but the same Hebrew verb, the points being changed, may become of the passive conjugation, and in that manner the Seventy Interpreters read, whom the Latin Interpreter followed. And indeed the reading, *non salvabit,* which St. Jerome hath, is clearer. But the reading of our edition, *non salvabitur* (he shall not be saved), requireth that something be understood, namely, he shall not be saved with his rider; for there can be no doubt but that the safety of the man, rather than the safety of the horse, is treated of in this place. Moreover the things which have been said of bodily safety are much more to be understood of spiritual salvation, for the obtaining of which no man is sufficient without the preventing, accompanying, and following grace of God.

[Margin: Without grace no man can be saved.]

[Margin: Verse 18.]

**18. BEHOLD THE EYES OF THE LORD ARE ON THEM THAT FEAR HIM: AND ON THEM THAT HOPE IN HIS MERCY.**

[Margin: Verse 19.]

**19. TO DELIVER THEIR SOULS FROM DEATH; AND FEED THEM IN FAMINE.**

The Prophet concludeth his reasoning, whereby, as we said above, he proveth that blessed is the nation whose God is the Lord. For God indeed seeth all men, and understandeth how little they can do unless he help: yet in a special manner he directeth his eyes upon just men, that he may give them help from heaven, both that they may be delivered from the danger of death, and that they may be fitly upheld in life. "Behold the eyes of the Lord are on them that fear him: and on them that hope in his mercy." By them that fear and them that hope are beautifully described those who are truly just and friends of God. For fear without hope is servile fear, hope without fear is presumption. Fear with hope declareth true charity, that is, the free love wherewith God is loved as a friend, as a father, as a spouse: for he who burneth with such love both vehemently feareth lest he offend the beloved, and securely trusteth, and hopeth, that the mercy of the beloved will never fail him. "To deliver their souls from death; and feed them in famine." This is the cause why the Lord looketh with a fatherly eye upon them that fear and hope in him, namely that he may bestow on them these two benefits, deliverance from the danger of death, and food while they live. Because the just fear to offend God, God delivereth them from the fear of being themselves hurt, chiefly touching life, which is the foundation of all goods; because they hope in his mercy, he showeth them perpetual mercy, while he feedeth them in famine. Which two benefits may be understood as well of bodily and temporal safety, as of spiritual and everlasting salvation. "The Lord delivereth souls from death," that is, the life of them that fear him, while by a singular providence he delivereth them from divers dangers of death, from which they could not have been delivered by their own strength or that of their friends. Then, when they are thus delivered, he upholdeth them in life, providing them with all things needful for life, and chiefly in the time of famine, when many others labour under the want of all things. But spiritually he delivereth their souls from death, while either he suffereth them not to fall into sin, which is the death of the soul; and if haply they have sinned, forthwith recalleth them by wholesome penance to grace, which is the life of the soul; and in both ways he delivereth their souls from everlasting death. And them that live to God by the indwelling Holy Ghost he feedeth in famine, while in this desert, through which we go toward the land of promise, a desert plainly barren and without water, he feedeth them with manna raining from heaven, and giveth them to drink of water springing from the rock, that is, he refresheth and upholdeth them with heavenly consolations. But he feedeth, he doth not fill, and he allayeth thirst, he doth not quench it, because fulness is kept for another life, in which we shall be satisfied when the glory of the Lord shall appear, and shall be inebriated with the plenty of the house of the Lord, and shall be given to drink of the torrent of pleasure.

[Margin: Hope without fear is presumption.]

[Margin: Verse 20.]

**20. OUR SOUL WAITETH FOR THE LORD: FOR HE IS OUR HELPER AND PROTECTOR.**

Hitherto the Prophet hath spoken to the just servants of God, and hath exhorted them to rejoice in the Lord, and to praise God, a most indulgent and most merciful father. Now he bringeth in the just answering and saying: "Our soul waiteth for the Lord." The just understand what the spirit meaneth, when he inviteth them to rejoice and to praise, namely that the intention of the Holy Ghost is, that hereby they be encouraged to persevere in justice, and to cleave to God, and not to depart from him for any persecutions how great soever, and lastly to praise God more in deed than in words. They answer therefore that, being strengthened by so many benefits of God, they will abide most constantly in his fear and love. "Our soul, say they, waiteth for the Lord," that is, whatsoever shall befall, it shall not separate us from the charity of God, nor will we look to any other comforter; but we will wait patiently for consolation from heaven, knowing that it is written, *Hab.* ii: "If it make any delay, wait for it: for it shall surely come, and it shall not be slack."[^8] Now the soul is said to wait for the Lord by a Hebrew phrase, whereby that is attributed to the soul which belongeth to the whole man, chiefly when a spiritual matter is treated of. So said Isaias, chap. xxvi: "Thy name, and thy remembrance are the desire of the soul; my soul hath desired thee in the night."[^9] And Jeremias, *Lam.* iii: "The Lord is good to them that hope in him, to the soul that seeketh him."[^10] And the most blessed Virgin, *Luke* i: "My soul doth magnify the Lord." "For he is our helper and protector." The just give the reason why they have determined so constantly to wait for the Lord, namely because they know, and have learned by experience, that by the Lord they are helped in good things, and protected from evil things, most mightily and most faithfully.

**21. FOR IN HIM OUR HEART SHALL REJOICE: AND IN HIS HOLY NAME WE HAVE TRUSTED.**

After the just have answered to the primary intention of the Holy Ghost, they answer now to the secondary, which was, that they should rejoice in the Lord, as was said in the first words of the Psalm. They say therefore that they will do this most gladly: "In him our heart shall rejoice" (*lætabitur*), that is, we will rejoice (*exultabimus*) in the Lord, "and in his holy name we have trusted" (*speravimus*, we have hoped), that is, because in his holy name we have hoped. For because they hoped in the Lord, they were helped and protected by him, and therefore, being taught by their own experience how good and mighty the Lord is, they rejoice in him, and give praise to his name. As to the words, the Hebrew hath, *quia in eo lætabitur cor nostrum, quia in nomine sancto ejus speravimus* (for in him our heart shall rejoice, for in his holy name we have trusted): but the first *quia* (for) is put for *et* (and), or for *certe* (surely), or is without force. As on the other hand in the Latin edition the word *et,* when it is said, *et in nomine sancto ejus speravimus,* is put for *quia.* The clearest reading therefore would be: And in him our heart shall rejoice, because in his holy name we have trusted. Moreover to hope in the name of the Lord is not to hope in the letters, or the sound of the name, but to hope in the Lord signified by the name; and this manner of speaking seemeth to have a certain greater force than if it be said simply, to hope in the Lord, for to hope in the name of the Lord is to hope in the Lord himself, called by name and properly; and the name of the Lord is called holy, because it is worthy to be honoured by all, and therefore the greatest crime is committed when it is blasphemed.

[Margin: It is the greatest crime to blaspheme the name of the Lord.]

[Margin: Verse 22.]

**22. LET THY MERCY, O LORD, BE UPON US, AS WE HAVE HOPED IN THEE.**

The Psalm is concluded by a prayer, as often elsewhere, and it is a prayer most fitly cohering with the words above, and with the whole Psalm; for because it hath been said several times that God hath mercy on them that hope in him, and the just say that they have hoped in the Lord, and they always need new mercy by reason of continual dangers, therefore they conclude: "Let thy mercy, O Lord, be upon us," that is, let it not cease, but continue, yea, let new mercy be always added upon us; "as we have hoped in thee," that is, as we have hoped would come to pass from thy goodness, and have promised ourselves.
