BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XXIX.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*A psalm of a canticle, at the dedication of David's house.*

This title showeth that the Psalm was sung both with the voice and with musical instruments, which we noted in the preface out of Blessed Hilary, and St. Basil teacheth the very same in his Commentary on this Psalm. From the same title we gather that this Psalm was composed by David, when his house in Jerusalem, most magnificently built, was dedicated. For then David understood that his kingdom had been confirmed and established by God, and therefore in thanksgiving he sang this hymn to God. This history is in Book II of *Kings*, v, and Book I of *Paralipomenon*, xiv. Now this Psalm seemeth to have a twofold sense, as the Psalm above, one historical, the other prophetical, both literal. And indeed rightly after the finishing of the tabernacle, which is the Church militant on earth, there followeth the dedication of the house, which is the Church resting firmly in heaven, whose foundation, which is Christ, hath already been dedicated, since he hath come to immortal and glorious life.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. I WILL EXTOL THEE, O LORD, FOR THOU HAST UPHELD ME: AND HAST NOT MADE MY ENEMIES TO REJOICE OVER ME.**[^1]

David, his kingdom being confirmed, the citadel of Sion fortified, and the city called from his name the city of David, lastly the royal house being most magnificently built, acknowledging God the author of so great benefits, offereth him the tribute of thanksgiving, and saith: "I will extol thee, O Lord," that is, although thou, O Lord, art most high, so that in very deed thou canst not become higher, yet among those who less acknowledge thy height, I will extol thee by my proclaiming, and will do what in me lieth, that all may acknowledge thee to be the Most High. "For thou hast upheld me," namely from the lowest place, and from the depth of lowliness thou hast taken me up, and exalted me to the throne of the kingdom. For the Hebrew word דלה *dalah* signifieth *to exalt*. And so he saith: Thou hast exalted me, and I will exalt thee, not ascribing my exaltation to my own strength, but to thy magnificence; thou hast exalted me, and I will humble myself, that I may exalt thee. "And hast not made my enemies to rejoice over me:" this followed from that exaltation, that the enemies of David, who were very many, and for a very long time sought him to slay him, had no matter of rejoicing from his destruction, which they most greedily desired, but on the contrary of grieving from that exaltation, which they strove with all their strength to hinder. But now in the prophetical sense David speaketh in the person of Christ, and of the elect, both all, and each, whom he foresaw as it were exalted in the heavenly kingdom, of whose number he himself also was to be. "I will extol thee, O Lord, for thou hast upheld me," that is, now truly I will extol thee, O Lord, in thought and voice, since from my exaltation I know thy most sublime height: for from the lowest earth, and the depth of miseries, from mortality itself thou hast taken me up, and exalted me to the glory of the resurrection and of immortality, and even to the heavenly and everlasting kingdom. "And hast not made my enemies to rejoice over me," because thou hast not suffered them to obtain their desire, with which the devils, and evil men, panted after my everlasting destruction. The wicked Jews indeed rejoiced, when they wrung from Pilate the sentence of death against Christ, and not seldom the evil are glad, when they can spoil the good either of their goods, or even of life; but their gladness is so short, and bringeth so long a grief, that it ought deservedly to be called a dream of gladness, rather than true gladness.

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. O LORD MY GOD, I HAVE CRIED TO THEE, AND THOU HAST HEALED ME.**

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. THOU HAST BROUGHT FORTH, O LORD, MY SOUL FROM HELL: THOU HAST SAVED ME FROM THEM THAT GO DOWN INTO THE PIT.**

The Prophet calleth to mind his straits, which he suffered before he obtained the kingdom, that he may show that to be true, which he said a little before: "And hast not made my enemies to rejoice over me." "O Lord my God, I have cried to thee," when I was so often in dangers of death, and for that cause was wasting away with sickness of mind: but thou, O Lord, hast healed that sickness of mine, and hast so delivered me from death near at hand, as if thou hadst brought me forth from hell itself. Now that phrase, *thou hast saved me from them that go down into the pit*, signifieth the same, but is a somewhat more obscure manner of speaking. The sense is: "Thou hast saved me," or, as it is word for word in the Hebrew, *thou hast quickened me* "from them that go down into the pit," that is, thou hast raised me from the dead. For by them that go down into the pit the dead are understood. Now David is said to have been raised from the dead, because he had been delivered from a death near at hand and most certain. But according to the prophetical understanding, Christ can say plainly according to the letter, *thou hast healed me*, that is, the wounds received on the cross, and *thou hast brought forth from hell*, that is from Limbo, my soul, and *thou hast quickened me*, by the resurrection, and so hast raised me from the dead. Lastly all the Saints will be able to say, when the everlasting

<!-- p. 170 (PDF 181) -->

house shall be dedicated, *thou hast healed me*, with the most perfect health of body and soul, and *thou hast brought forth my soul from hell*, because thou hast not suffered me to go down into the hell of the damned, and *thou hast saved*, or *quickened me from them that go down into the pit*, because thou hast given me life and everlasting salvation. The same is in *Ps.* cii: "Who healeth all thy diseases, who redeemeth thy life from destruction."

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. SING TO THE LORD, O YE HIS SAINTS: AND GIVE PRAISE TO THE MEMORY OF HIS HOLINESS.**

Both David, having received temporal benefits, and every one of the saints, having received everlasting benefits, judging it little if he alone give thanks to God, exhorteth others who have received like benefits, not to cease to praise God. That phrase, *give praise to the memory of his holiness*, hath this sense: Confess with the confession of praise, that is, praise his holy memory, namely that he hath been mindful of you with a holy memory, a pious memory, a fatherly memory, not to punish, but to justify; for everywhere in the Hebrew tongue a noun substantive is put in the second case, for an adjective in the first case. For what[^2] we often read, *in his holy mountain*, or *in his holy temple*, is in the Hebrew, *in the mountain*, or, *in the temple of his holiness*. And so St. Jerome, who in this place set, *memoriæ sanctitatis ejus* (to the memory of his holiness), in *Ps.* xcvii, the last verse,[^3] set, *memoriæ sanctæ ejus* (to his holy memory). And truly this is a great benefit of God, and to be recalled with most grateful memory, that whereas we, who always need the help of God, often forget him; he, who needeth not our goods, always remembereth us, and chiefly remembered us, when he sent to us his Son, the Saviour of the world, which very thing the same David wondereth at in *Ps.* viii, when he saith: "What is man that thou art mindful of him?"

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. FOR WRATH IS IN HIS INDIGNATION; AND LIFE IN HIS GOOD WILL.**

He giveth the reason why he said that the holy memory, which God hath of us, is to be praised. For the reason is, that God, if he punisheth us, doth it out of indignation, which our sins provoke, that is, out of just judgment; but otherwise in his will and choice there is not punishment, but life. And so by wrath is understood punishment and chastisement, which is called wrath, because it is the effect of wrath: by indignation is understood the just judgment of God, as St. Basil and others expound. In the Hebrew there is, *for a moment is in his indignation, and life in his good will*. But the Seventy Interpreters seem not to have read רגע *regah*, which signifieth *a moment*, but רגז *regaz*, which signifieth *wrath*; perhaps also they read *regah*, and rendered *wrath*, because that word signifieth not only *a moment*, but also *a cutting*, and so they understood the sense to be, that in the indignation of God there is cutting, that is slaying and death, but in the will of God there is

[Margin: The indignation of the Father against Christ was most short.]

life: but for *cutting* they set *wrath*, because very often in Scripture *wrath* is put for *punishment*. But the sense also, which St. Jerome expresseth from the Hebrew, is not to be despised: for truly in respect of the elect, of whom this Psalm treateth, *a moment is in the indignation of God*, that is, most short and momentary is the indignation of God against the elect when they sin, because he straightway pardoneth the penitent, for in his will there is not wrath, but life. Moreover all these things fit wonderfully Christ the head of the elect. For "the Father spared not his own Son," *Rom.* viii; but yet the wrath, that is, the punishing, was in Christ from indignation against our sins, and that chastisement was most short, but the life and resurrection of Christ was plainly from the will and choice of God.

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. IN THE EVENING WEEPING SHALL HAVE PLACE, AND IN THE MORNING GLADNESS.**

St. Jerome rendereth, *in matutino laus* (in the morning praise), but the Hebrew word רנה *rinnah* signifieth properly *jubilation*, or *exultation*, in which praise is joined with gladness; and so there is no disagreement between the versions. The sense therefore is, that hence it is proved that wrath or a moment is in the indignation of God, but that in his will and good pleasure is the life of his elect, because after the weeping born of chastisement and penance, there shall straightway succeed gladness, and the praise of jubilation from remission and reconciliation: for between evening and morning, that is, between night and day, there is nothing in the midst. Now rightly is the night time given to weeping, and the day time to gladness, both because when we sin, the light of divine grace departeth from us, when we are reconciled, it returneth; and because the time of this life, in which we bewail our sins, and for desire of our country groan and sigh, is a night time, because we see not God, who is the sun of justice: but the time of the life to come, "in which we shall rejoice with joy unspeakable and glorified," as St. Peter speaketh, I *Epist.* chap. i, will be a day time, because we shall see God as he is. But in Christ this prophecy was fulfilled to the letter, for at evening he died with sorrow, and in the morning he rose again with gladness.

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. AND IN MY ABUNDANCE I SAID: I SHALL NEVER BE MOVED.**

The change of wrath and life, of weeping and gladness, which the Prophet showed in the verses above in general, he now showeth in particular, speaking both in his own person, and in the person of the elect of God. And first in his own person he saith that before he was exalted to the kingdom, he once abounded with so great peace, and plenty of temporal goods, that he thought these would be with him perpetually. Now he seemeth to look to that time, in which after the slaying of Goliath the Philistine, as it is in I *Kings* xviii, he was loved by the king, by the king's son, and by all the people, so that a little after he was made a tribune, and the king's son-in-law. Of this time therefore he saith:

<!-- p. 171 (PDF 182) -->

"And in my abundance I said," that is, while I abounded with all goods: "I shall never be moved;" that is, so well founded is this my felicity, that it seemeth it will last always.

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. O LORD, IN THY FAVOUR, THOU GAVEST STRENGTH TO MY BEAUTY.**

He giveth the reason why he said: "I shall never be moved," and he doth it by an apostrophe to God, as if he said: Therefore I said: "I shall never be moved," because thou, "O Lord, gavest strength," that is might and force, "to my beauty," that is, to my felicity, and that, "in thy favour," that is in thy good pleasure, because so it pleased thee, so thou didst will and decree. In the Hebrew there is, *to my mountain*, for *to my beauty*: but the Seventy did not read הררי *hareri*, that is, *to my mountain*, but, הדרי *hadari*, *to my beauty*, a single letter, and that a very like one, being changed: and, as we have often said, in ambiguities of words of this kind, the reading of the Seventy Interpreters and of the Vulgate edition seemeth altogether to be preferred.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. THOU TURNEDST AWAY THY FACE FROM ME, AND I BECAME TROUBLED.**

Here now he declareth the change of things, which yet dependeth on the providence of God. "Thou turnedst away," saith he, "thy face from me," that is, a little after, in the midst of the course of felicity thou didst leave me without thy favour, and didst suffer me to fall into the hatred of the king. "And I became troubled;" for thence arose exile, flight, danger of death, and other evils. But all these things agree far better still with the changes which the elect suffer, while they are pilgrims on earth. "And I," saith any one of the elect, "in my abundance," that is, while I abounded with the grace of God, and spiritual consolation, "said: I shall never be moved;" "though I should die with thee, I will not deny thee," said one of the chief of the elect, *Matt.* xxvi. "O Lord, in thy favour, thou gavest strength to my beauty," that is, my strength was not of myself, but of thee, because thou hadst given strength to my beauty, that is, the beauty of my soul, which ariseth from the light of wisdom and justice, was preserved by thy help. "Thou turnedst away thy face from me, and I became troubled," that is, that thou mightest punish my presumption, thou didst leave me to myself, and straightway I fell, and was troubled. As to Christ, these three verses can be attributed to Christ, for his body, which is the Church, that is, so that he saith all these things in the person of his members. They can also be attributed to Christ in his own person. For as he himself said on the cross, *Matt.* xxvii: "My God, why hast thou forsaken me?" so he can say: "Thou turnedst away thy face from me," not with the turning away of enmity, but with the turning away of defence and help in the time of the passion. And so the sense will be: "And in my abundance I said: I shall never be moved,"[^4] that is, my humanity, anointed above all its fellows with most abundant grace, being namely hypostatically

[Margin: The changes which the elect suffer in this life.]

united to God the very fountain of graces, said: "I shall never be moved," that is, nothing will be able to hurt me, and nothing to trouble me. "O Lord, in thy favour, thou gavest strength to my beauty,"[^5] that is, because thou, O Lord, to my beauty and excellence, by which I surpass all men and angels, didst add might and power, namely the indissoluble knot of the hypostatic union, and that "of thy will," which no one can resist. "Thou turnedst away thy face, and I became troubled," that is, nevertheless, the bond of the hypostatic union yet remaining, and the beauty of grace unhurt, thou didst turn away for a time, for the salvation of men, thy face from my defence: thou wouldst that the chalice of the most bitter passion should not pass from me, but that I should drink it; therefore "I became troubled," I began to fear, to be heavy, and to be sad, and I said, *Matt.* xxvi: "My soul is sorrowful even unto death." Nor ought it to be gathered hence that the soul of Christ was ignorant of any thing, or that any thing unlooked for befell it. For it is true that nothing could hurt it, or trouble it, unless itself had willed; but he admitted upon himself the hands of them that raged, and troubled himself. And as Christ said to the Father: "Thou turnedst away thy face from me," so he might have said: I turned away the face of my divinity from the help of my humanity, and knowing and willing I became troubled.

[Margin: Nothing unlooked for could happen to Christ.]

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. TO THEE, O LORD, WILL I CRY: AND I WILL MAKE SUPPLICATION TO MY GOD.**

[Margin: Verse 11.]

**11. WHAT PROFIT IS THERE IN MY BLOOD, WHILST I GO DOWN TO CORRUPTION?**

[Margin: Verse 12.]

**12. SHALL DUST CONFESS TO THEE, OR DECLARE THY TRUTH?**

All these things seem to be taken for the time past, nor is it unusual that among the Hebrews the future is put for the past. And so David in the historical sense saith that he, in the time of tribulation and danger, cried to the Lord, and among other things alleged, that his death was not profitable to the Lord, because being dead he could not praise God. "To thee, O Lord, will I cry," that is, when, thou turning away thy face, I became troubled, I despaired not of thy mercy; but I cried to thee, and making supplication, said: "What profit is there in my blood, whilst I go down to corruption," that is, what will the shedding of my blood profit thee, O Lord, while I, slain by my enemies, rot in the sepulchre? Certainly dust shall not confess to thee with the confession of praise. According to the higher and prophetical sense, Christ saith that in the passion he cried to the Lord, and prayed, which was fulfilled, when, as the Apostle speaketh, *Heb.* v: "With a strong cry and tears, he offered up prayers to him that was able to save him from death." Then therefore he saith: "What profit is there in my blood, whilst I go down to corruption?" that is, what profit will there be to the glory of God, and the salvation of mankind in my blood shed on the cross, while after the manner of other men, my body rotteth and is corrupted in the sepulchre? For

<!-- p. 172 (PDF 183) -->

"unless Christ had risen again, our faith would be vain," as the Apostle saith, I *Cor.* xv; and neither would he himself, alive again, have declared to the Apostles the truth of God, nor would mortal men, who are of themselves dust and ashes, have been made spiritual, and children of God, to confess to the Lord, and declare to other men the truth, that is, the faithfulness and justice of God. Lastly these same words can be attributed to each of the elect, who after falling into fault, led by penance, have cried to God for the remission of sin, that they might be able to confess to the Lord, and declare to other sinners the truth of the promises of God.

[Margin: Verse 13.]

**13. THE LORD HATH HEARD, AND HATH HAD MERCY ON ME: THE LORD BECAME MY HELPER.**

From this verse it is manifest that the words of the tenth verse were rightly explained of the time past. The Prophet therefore affirmeth, both in his own person, and in the person of Christ and of the elect, that his cry was heard by God. In the Hebrew is read: *Hear, O Lord, and have mercy on me, O Lord, be thou my helper.*

But the same Hebrew words, under other points, give the reading which we have. And so it is certain that the Seventy Interpreters read otherwise than St. Jerome and the later Hebrews read: nor is it to be doubted that the authority of the Seventy Interpreters ought to prevail in the reading of the same words.

[Margin: Verse 14.]

**14. THOU HAST TURNED FOR ME MY MOURNING INTO JOY: THOU HAST CUT MY SACKCLOTH, AND HAST COMPASSED ME WITH GLADNESS.**

This is the effect of the hearing. David, from a wretched exile made a mighty king; Christ, rising from the dead, and triumphing over death itself; each of the elect, coming from the vale of tears to the heavenly country, most justly saith: "Thou hast turned for me my mourning into joy." In the Hebrew, for *into joy*, there is *into a choir*, but by the choir, in Hebrew מחול *machol*, is understood a musical instrument. The sense is the same: but the Hebrew word seemeth to answer somewhat more elegantly to the word going before, as if it were said in Latin: *Convertisti planctum in cantum* (Thou hast turned mourning into song). That which followeth: "Thou hast cut my sackcloth, and hast compassed me with gladness," signifieth the changing of the garment of mourning into a garment befitting gladness. Now the Prophet saith, *thou hast cut*, and not *thou hast put off*, or *stripped*, because that which is put off remaineth whole; that which is cut is destroyed, so that it cannot be of use thereafter. And so there is signified in this place the sackcloth of misery, or of mortality, and passibility, plainly destroyed, so that it shall never more be of use in the Saints, and much less in Christ, who "rising again from the dead, dieth now no more," as is said *Rom.* vi.

[Margin: Verse 15.]

**15. TO THE END THAT MY GLORY MAY SING TO THEE, AND I MAY NOT REGRET: O LORD MY GOD, I WILL GIVE PRAISE TO THEE FOR EVER.**

This is the last end of the glory of Christ and of all the Saints, the praise of God. "Blessed are they that dwell in thy house, O Lord, they shall praise thee for ever and ever." That phrase, *et non compungar* (and I may not regret), is in the Hebrew, ולא ידם *velo iddom*, *and may not be silent*, so that the sense is, that my glory may sing to thee praise and thanksgiving, and never be silent, never cease from praise; to which is rightly subjoined: "I will give praise to thee for ever," that is, I will always praise, I will never be silent. But although this reading agreeth well enough with the rest of the words, yet the same Hebrew word ידם *iddom* signifieth *to be silent*, and *to cut off* or *cut down*; and this signification the Seventy Interpreters chose, and read in the first person, *et non compungar* (and I may not be pricked), which is the same with this, *et non succidar* (and I may not be cut down). Now the sense is: Let my glory sing to thee, and not my groaning, from fear of death, or of sin; or let my glory sing to thee, and let me no more be pricked with the sting of death, or with sins.
