R. BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM II.

## ARGUMENT.

This Psalm lacketh a title, but is ascribed to David by the Apostles, *Acts* iv. Some judge this Psalm to be not the second, but a part of the first, because in the Acts of the Apostles, chap. xiii, in certain copies this Psalm is cited under the name of the *primi* (first) Psalm; but more copies, and better, have: *Sicut in Psalmo secundo scriptum est* (As in the second Psalm it is written), and the Greek edition agreeth, τῷ δευτέρῳ. Now this whole Psalm is a most manifest prophecy of the kingdom of Christ, and is explained by the Apostles, *Acts* chap. iv and xiii, and *Heb.* i and v, so that they seem altogether to err who strive to explain this Psalm, to the letter, of David.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. WHY HAVE THE GENTILES RAGED, AND THE PEOPLE DEVISED VAIN THINGS?**

David, foreknowing in spirit that the Messias would come, and that many persecutions would arise against him, and that he would nevertheless reign most happily, beginneth with a rebuke of the persecutors; and the sense is, that in vain have the Gentiles and the Hebrews, both princes and peoples, raged with indignation against the Messias, and thought upon a way of hindering his kingdom. In the Hebrew the first verb is of the past tense, the second of the future: but because the speech is of the same thing, the Seventy Interpreters rendered both verbs by the past tense, and St. Jerome rendered both by the future tense. And indeed the meaning is always true; for the thing itself was in itself to come, but to the Prophet it was in a manner past, since he beheld it as though past. Yet absolutely the reading of the Seventy Interpreters and of the Vulgate edition is to be preferred, both because in the *Acts of the Apostles*, chap. iv, we read in the past, *fremuerunt* (have raged) and *meditati sunt* (have devised); and because reason requireth that the signification of the first verb be the rule of the signification of the second, not the contrary. By the word *gentes* (nations), when it is said, *Why have the Gentiles raged*, the Gentiles seem to be meant. For so the Apostles understood it, *Acts* iv, and the Hebrew word גוים *goym* is in many places of the Scriptures taken for the Gentiles, or heathens. But by the people, when it is added: "And the people devised vain things," the Hebrews are to be understood, as is plain from the same chap. iv of *Acts*. The words also are very properly fitted both to the Gentiles and to the peoples of the Hebrews. For the Gentiles are said to have raged, as living creatures void of reason; the Jewish peoples to have devised vain things, because they took counsel together, that they might destroy Jesus.

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. THE KINGS OF THE EARTH STOOD UP, AND THE PRINCES MET TOGETHER, AGAINST THE LORD, AND AGAINST HIS CHRIST.**

[Margin: Kings and Emperors stirred up persecutions against Christ.]

He had said, in general, that the Gentiles and the peoples had raged with indignation, and devised vain things; now he explaineth in particular who those Gentiles and peoples are, and against whom they were moved with indignation, and saith that the raging began not from the common people, but from the heads, that is, from kings and from princes. For the first who raged was Herod the king, as we have it in *Matt.* ii; which Herod indeed was a foreigner, so that he could be reckoned among the Gentiles. Him the princes and peoples of the Jews followed. For, Herod being troubled, "all Jerusalem was troubled with him." Then at the time of the Lord's passion, again another Herod, and Pontius Pilate, with the princes and peoples of the Jews, rose up against Christ. Lastly, after the passion and resurrection of Christ, persecutions were ever begun by kings, or emperors, and fostered by the peoples following their leaders. Now although the indignation and raging was properly against Christ, not against God, nay rather under colour of the glory of God and as it were out of zeal for the divine honour, yet in truth they who assailed Christ assailed the true God. For Christ by divine works showed himself the Son of the true God, and he that hateth the Son, without doubt hateth the Father also. As touching the words, *astiterunt* (stood up) properly signifieth in the Hebrew *they set themselves*, that is, they set themselves as a wall, to withstand the Messias at his coming; and *convenerunt in unum* (met together) in the Hebrew properly signifieth *they consulted*, or

<!-- p. 15 (PDF 26) -->

*they came together to take counsel*: which in the Gospel we read that Herod and the princes of the Jews did against Christ. And so plain is this Prophecy, that not only did the Apostles, *Acts* iv, understand this place to the letter of Jesus Christ our Lord, but the ancient Rabbins also, as Rabbi Solomon witnesseth in his Commentary on this Psalm, expounded this same place of the true Messias, whom, blind and infatuated, they still await.

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. LET US BREAK THEIR BONDS ASUNDER, AND LET US CAST AWAY THEIR YOKE FROM US.**

The Prophet explaineth the cause why the kings and princes, with the Gentiles and peoples, raged and met together against Christ. For the cause is, lest they should be compelled to keep the law of Christ, which is contrary to the senses of the flesh and to human prudence. And so these words are spoken in the person of the wicked kings and princes, as if he said: "The kings of the earth stood up, and the princes met together against the Lord, and against his Christ," saying among themselves: "Let us break their bonds asunder," that is, let us not suffer ourselves to be made subject to the laws and the rule of the Messias. For *jugum ipsorum* (their yoke) St. Jerome translateth *laqueos eorum* (their snares); the Hebrew word עבות signifieth *ropes*; and the Prophet seemeth to have wished to repeat almost the same word, as he is ordinarily wont to say the selfsame thing twice. And so the Seventy Interpreters and St. Jerome looked not so much to the word as to the sense. Now all the words are metaphorical, whether thou call the law bonds, or ropes, or snares, or a yoke. And these metaphors explain the nature of the law, such as it is in the judgment of the wicked. For to the wicked the law of the Lord seemeth a most hard yoke, and a deadly snare, whereas to holy men the same law is sweeter than honey, and more to be desired than gold and precious stones, as we have it in *Ps.* xviii.

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. HE THAT DWELLETH IN HEAVEN SHALL LAUGH AT THEM, AND THE LORD SHALL DERIDE THEM.**

[Margin: The Religion of Christ is from God.]

Here now the Prophet showeth that in vain do Princes and peoples labour in assailing the Religion of Christ, which he had also said at the beginning. For the Religion of Christ is from God, and no man can resist God. And rightly he saith: "He that dwelleth in heaven," to show that God is above all, and beholdeth all things, and will therefore without any trouble bring to nought all their counsels, and scatter all their contrivings. And whereas he saith that the Lord shall laugh at them and deride them, he signifieth that by the divine judgment, through signs and wonders, through the patience of the Martyrs, through the conversion of nations and peoples, and through other ways known to God alone, they are to be so confounded as to lie open to the laughter and derision of all. This we see fulfilled. For the Jewish priesthood and the idolatrous are now laughed at by all. For the Jews and the Gentiles are without temples and sacrifices; the kings also of the Gentiles, who persecuted the Church, have all perished miserably.

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. THEN SHALL HE SPEAK TO THEM IN HIS ANGER, AND TROUBLE THEM IN HIS RAGE.**

[Margin: In God there is not properly Anger nor rage.]

He explaineth the manner in which God hath exposed the persecutors of Christ to be laughed at: namely, that he hath spoken to them, not with words, but with stripes, admonishing them by most grievous punishments, as is plain of Herod struck by an Angel, of Maximinus consumed by worms, and of others. There is not properly anger, nor rage, in God, but he always judgeth with tranquillity: but he is said by a metaphor to be angry and to rage, when he punisheth severely, and then chiefly when that punishment doth not profit them unto eternal salvation; for so are they wont to do who are angry and rage, who hurt not that they may heal, as physicians do, but hurt that they may hurt. And so David: "O Lord," saith he, "rebuke me not in thy indignation, nor chastise me in thy wrath." For he desired to be rebuked and chastised, but as by a father, not as by an enemy; unto salvation, not unto destruction.

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. BUT I AM APPOINTED KING BY HIM OVER SION HIS HOLY MOUNTAIN, PREACHING HIS COMMANDMENT.**

In this place something must be said of the words, before we come to the sense. For in the Hebrew copy it standeth thus: *Ego autem ordinavi Regem meum super Sion montem sanctum meum* (But I have ordained my King over Sion my holy mountain); and although it mattereth not much, as to the sense, whether God the Father say that he hath ordained Christ King, or the Son himself say that he was ordained by the Father: yet it is very probable that the Seventy Interpreters read somewhat otherwise than we now read. We have now נסכתי *nasachti, ordinavi* (I have ordained); but they seem to have read, the points only being changed, נסכתי *nissacti, ordinatus sum* (I was ordained), which word is found in *Prov.* viii, where we read: "*Ab æterno ordinata sum*" (I was set up from eternity); and where we read מלכי *malchi, Regem meum* (my King), they seem to have read מלכו *malcho, Rex ejus* (his King); and where we read קדשי *chodsci, sanctum meum* (my holy), they seem to have read קדשו *chodscio, sanctum ejus* (his holy). And since it is probable that the Seventy Interpreters had very correct Hebrew Copies, hence it becometh probable also that our Vulgate reading is truer than that which is now read in the Hebrew Copies. Let us now come to the sense. The Prophet had said that the adversaries of Christ would raise a tumult, and had set down their words: "Let us break their bonds asunder," etc. Therefore he now bringeth in Christ himself speaking, and as it were answering them, as if he said: The Gentiles and the peoples, and the kings and princes also, shall say: "Let us break their bonds asunder"; but God shall laugh at them, and Christ himself shall thus answer them: I am ordained King not by men, but by God, and therefore I fear not the threats of men; and I am ordained King over Sion his holy mountain, that is, over the Church, which is a city seated on a mountain, and whose type was Jerusalem, of which the chief part, and the more beloved of God, and

<!-- p. 16 (PDF 27) -->

sanctified, is Sion, according to that of *Ps.* lxxxvi: "The Lord loveth the gates of Sion above all the tabernacles of Jacob." And this my ordaining as king standeth by the decree of God, which I am about to preach. For that which we have in our Vulgate edition, *prædicans præceptum ejus* (preaching his commandment), and in the Hebrew text, *enarrabo decretum ejus* (I will declare his decree), is understood of the decree whereby God determined that the kingdom of Christ should be spread into the whole world. The Hebrew word חק *choc* signifieth *a commandment, a decree, a statute*, as is plain from many places, but chiefly from *Ps.* civ: "And he appointed the same to Jacob for a law, and to Israel for an everlasting testament: saying: To thee will I give the land of Chanaan." Where *præceptum* (there rendered "a law") is in Hebrew חק *choc*, and signifieth the decree of God concerning the giving of the land of promise to the children of Israel.

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. THE LORD HATH SAID TO ME: THOU ART MY SON, THIS DAY HAVE I BEGOTTEN THEE.**

[Margin: The eternal generation of Christ.]

This is the beginning and foundation of the divine decree: for all power in heaven and in earth is due to Christ, because he is the true and natural Son of God. Now there are three expositions of this place, and all of them literal, and intended by the Holy Ghost. The first is of the eternal generation, which is gathered from the Apostle, *Heb.*, where he saith thus: "Being made so much better than the angels, as he hath inherited a more excellent name than they. For to which of the angels hath he said at any time, Thou art my Son, to day have I begotten thee?" This place of the Apostle cannot be understood of any created generation, as of the nativity, or of the resurrection according to the flesh, because by a created generation the Angels also are sons of God, as is plain from chap. i, ii and iii of the book of *Job*;[^1] and yet the Apostle intendeth to prove that Christ is the son, but the angels ministers. It is necessary therefore that the words of the psalm cited by the Apostle be understood to the letter of the eternal generation, as St. Augustine rightly expoundeth; which exposition is favoured both by the word *hodie* (this day), because eternity is a present duration lacking beginning and end; and by the word *genui* (I have begotten), in Hebrew ילד *ialad*, which signifieth properly *to bring forth*. For God in the eternal generation needed not the fellowship of a mother, but himself begot, himself brought forth his only begotten son, according to that of *Ps.* cix: "From the womb before the day star I begot thee." The second exposition is of the resurrection of the Lord, which is gathered from the same Apostle, *Acts* xiii, where we read thus: "And we declare unto you, that the promise which was made to our fathers, this same God hath fulfilled to your children,[^2] raising up Jesus, as in the second psalm also is written: Thou art my Son, this day have I begotten thee." And these words fit the resurrection of the Lord very well, both because the resurrection is a kind of regeneration, as we have it in *Matt.* xix: "In the regeneration, when the Son of man shall sit";

[Margin: The resurrection of Christ is a regeneration.]

and also because the resurrection of Christ was from God alone, and he is called "the firstborn of the dead,"[^3] *Col.* i; and lastly because on the day of the resurrection began the glorious exaltation of Christ, and the manifestation of the name which is above all names, *Phil.* ii: "For which cause God also hath exalted him"; and the Lord himself said, *Matt.*, last chap.: "All power is given to me in heaven and in earth"; and this is the exposition of Hilary and Chrysostom. The third exposition is of the day of the Lord's Nativity according to the flesh, which is that of St. Cyprian, book ii *Against the Jews*, chap. viii, and is gathered from the introit of the Mass in the night of the same Nativity. For the Church applieth the words of this Psalm to the mystery of the nativity according to the flesh, because that generation was a singular work of God, so that the angel Gabriel also said, *Luke* i: "And therefore also the Holy which shall be born of thee shall be called the Son of God." And the holy Fathers understand, not only of the divine, but also of the human generation of Christ, those words of *Isa.* liii: "Who shall declare his generation?" And so, following those Theologians who teach that in the same sentence several literal senses can be found, we judge the sense of these words to be: this day, that is, in the day of eternity, I, God, have begotten thee, God; and this day, that is, in the day of thy nativity according to the flesh, I have begotten thee, that is, I have caused thee to be God and man, without the seed of man, without the corruption of thy mother, without the stain of sin; and likewise this day, that is, in the day of thy resurrection, I have begotten thee, that is, I have caused by my divine power that thou shouldst return to life, and that a life immortal and glorious.

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. ASK OF ME, AND I WILL GIVE THEE THE GENTILES FOR THY INHERITANCE, AND THE UTMOST PARTS OF THE EARTH FOR THY POSSESSION.**

The meaning is most plain, as if God the Father said: Because thou, O Christ, art my natural Son, and by my power made incarnate and raised from the dead, thou canst by right ask of me dominion over all the Gentiles, as thy inheritance, and all the earth unto its utmost bounds, as a possession due to thee. Now some things are here to be observed: *First*, that the word inheritance is taken in the Scriptures for any thing whatsoever that is possessed as one's own, even though it be not properly left by a father deceased: and therefore the people of God is called the inheritance of God, as much as God himself the inheritance of his people. And because an inheritance properly so called was wont to be divided among brethren, and was for the most part divided by lot, and the parts divided were measured with cords, therefore inheritance, portion, lot, cord, possession are taken for the same thing; often also two of these words are joined, as *Ps.* xv: "The Lord is the portion of my inheritance," that is, the Lord is the portion which hath fallen to me by inheritance; and elsewhere, *Deut.* xxxii: "Jacob the lot of his inheritance,"[^4] that is, the people of Israel is the inheritance of the Lord, which he chose for himself, and measured as it were with a cord, and divided from the inheritances of others. And so in this place all the Gentiles are called the inheritance of Christ, because he was made Lord of the whole world, which is expounded by the words that follow: "And the utmost parts of the earth for thy possession." *Secondly*, it is to be observed that these things are to be understood chiefly of the spiritual kingdom of Christ, that is, of

<!-- p. 17 (PDF 28) -->

the Church to be spread through the whole world, as St. Augustine very often teacheth. The sense therefore is, that Christ is appointed king over Sion, that is, over the people of the Lord, but that this kingdom is not to be shut within the bounds of Judea, or of Palestine, as the kingdom of David and of Solomon; but is to be stretched forth through all nations, and shall possess all the kingdoms of the world, according to the prophecy of Daniel, chap. ii. Nor, lastly, is it to be denied that it is understood also of the power of Christ over all nations, even the unbelieving: "For all power is given to him in heaven and in earth," *Matt.* xxviii; and he it is who was appointed Judge of the living and of the dead, *Acts* x.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. THOU SHALT RULE THEM WITH A ROD OF IRON, AND SHALT BREAK THEM IN PIECES LIKE A POTTER'S VESSEL.**

[Margin: How great is the power of Christ over all nations?]

Here is signified the power of Christ over the Church, and over all men, supreme and most just, so that he can as easily reward the good, and visit the wicked with punishments, as a potter can easily with a rod of iron break in pieces vessels newly made, and of clay. In the former part of the verse the supreme power of Christ is described on the part of the rod of iron, that is, of a judgment most right, inflexible, and which cannot be resisted; in the latter, on the part of the men subject to him, who shall be in respect of Christ as vessels of clay newly made in respect of the potter. And so *confringes* (thou shalt break in pieces) signifieth not the act, but the power; that is, it signifieth not that Christ will break all nations in pieces, but that all nations shall be subject to him, so that he can break them in pieces at his pleasure, whether mercifully, by breaking in them unbelief and sins, and forming of vessels of dishonour vessels of honour; or justly and severely, by breaking them with eternal punishments for their own demerits. For *confringes ut vasa figuli* (thou shalt break in pieces like a potter's vessels) must signify the same as *reges in virga ferrea* (thou shalt rule with a rod of iron): but *reges in virga ferrea* is referred not to the punishment of the wicked alone, but to the just and most mighty government of the whole Church. Which is manifest both from the version of the Seventy Interpreters, who rendered it ποιμανεῖς, that is, *pasces* (thou shalt feed), as St. John also rendered it in Greek in the *Apoc.* chap. ii and xix; and from *Mich.* chap. v, where, speaking most plainly of Christ, who cometh forth out of Bethlehem to be the ruler over the people of God, he saith: "Thou shalt rule them in strength,"[^5] and useth the same word which is found in this place, namely רעה *raha*.

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. AND NOW, O YE KINGS, UNDERSTAND: RECEIVE INSTRUCTION, YOU THAT JUDGE THE EARTH.**

The Prophet now exhorteth the kings of the earth, on whom the peoples depend, that, since they strive in vain to resist Christ, the true and supreme king of all kings, they submit themselves to him of their own accord; and because ordinarily from a perverse judgment there followeth a perverse affection, he first exhorteth them to correct their judgment, to understand the truth, and to receive instruction. Now it is the same thing: "O ye kings, understand," and: "Receive instruction, ye judges of the earth." For kings are the supreme judges of the peoples. Then he exhorteth them to correct their perverse affection, and, instead of hatred toward Christ, to begin to serve him, and to love and reverence him; therefore he subjoineth:

[Margin: Verse 11.]

**11. SERVE YE THE LORD WITH FEAR, AND REJOICE UNTO HIM WITH TREMBLING.**

Wonderfully hath he mingled love with fear, as if he said: Fear with love, and love with fear; for that word, *serve ye*, is proper to servants who fear, chiefly according to the Hebrew word עבדו *hibdu*. But that, *with fear*, signifieth properly filial reverence, or piety; for that very word יראה *iira*, which is found here, is found also in *Isa.* xi, where it is said of Christ: "The spirit of godliness shall rest upon him,"[^6] and in many places of the Scriptures it is taken for piety, or filial fear. And so "Serve ye the Lord with fear," according to the force of the Hebrew words, signifieth: Serve the Lord indeed, but with a free service proper to sons. And that which followeth: "And rejoice unto him with trembling," signifieth the same, but with the order and the turns changed; for in the former part of the verse he set in the verb servile fear, in the noun filial piety: in the latter part, on the contrary, he set in the verb the rejoicing of a loving son; but in the noun, the trembling of a fearing servant.

[Margin: Verse 12.]

**12. EMBRACE DISCIPLINE, LEST AT ANY TIME THE LORD BE ANGRY, AND YOU PERISH FROM THE JUST WAY.**

This place some render from the Hebrew, *osculamini filium* (kiss the son); others, *adorate filium* (adore the son); others, *adorate pure* (adore purely). It might also be rendered, *complectimini puritatem* (clasp purity), to which the translation of the Seventy Interpreters might easily be reduced; for to embrace discipline is the same as to clasp purity, namely, of doctrine. As also to kiss, or to adore, the Son of God is almost the same as to embrace his discipline. But since the Seventy Interpreters were most learned, and had the best texts, and in the translation of this place there is no variety among the Greeks, and (which is more to be wondered at) the Chaldee Paraphrast also rendered it, *accipite doctrinam* (receive doctrine), I altogether suspect, either that something hath been changed in the Hebrew by the fault of the times, or at least that the word בר *bar* signifieth not only son and purity, but also discipline, especially with some letter added thereto. For as among the Greeks from the word παῖς, that is, *boy*, is made the noun παιδεία, that is, *discipline*, which is in this place of the Psalm, so it may be that from the word *bar*, that is, son, some noun is made which signifieth discipline, which is chiefly owed to sons by their parents. Now the sense of these words is, that kings should not only correct their judgment and affection, be instructed and serve, as was said above, but should also do it with great fervour, that the instruction, on which the affection dependeth, may abide in them. For the words, both the Hebrew נשקו *nassecu* and the Greek δράξασθαι, signify not simply *to receive*, but to seize with great affection, and to clasp,

<!-- p. 18 (PDF 29) -->

and to hold fast, as one claspeth a person greatly beloved, in order to kiss him. Now he giveth the reason why it is necessary, saying: "Lest at any time the Lord be angry, and you perish from the just way." In the Hebrew there is not *Dominus* (the Lord), nor *justa* (just); but simply, *nequando irascatur, et pereatis de via* (lest at any time he be angry, and you perish from the way): but the Seventy added them for the sake of explaining. For the Lord was understood from the verse above: "Serve ye the Lord with fear, and rejoice unto him with trembling." The sense therefore is: Embrace discipline, lest at any time he be angry against the wicked and rebellious, whom I have said must be served with fear; and you perish, or be driven out, from the way of justice. This is the most grievous punishment of princes, when God for their sins delivereth them up to a reprobate sense, as is said in *Rom.* i, and suffereth them to be deceived by evil counsellors, and to do many evils, for which at length they perish in this life and in that to come, as is plain of Pharao, Roboam, Achab, and the like, in whom most grievous sins were the punishments of other sins, as St. Augustine argueth, book v, iii, *Against Julian*: for this is not to decline a little from the right way, but plainly to perish, and to be altogether driven out from the way of justice.

[Margin: Verse 13.]

**13. WHEN HIS WRATH SHALL BE KINDLED IN A SHORT TIME, BLESSED ARE ALL THEY THAT TRUST IN HIM.**

This is the conclusion of the whole Psalm, in which the holy Prophet declareth that from what hath been said it can be gathered how good and profitable it is to love the Lord, and to serve him with the whole heart: for they alone who shall be such will be able to have trust in the day of the last judgment, which cannot be far off. He saith: "When his wrath shall be kindled in a short time," to signify that that terrible day will come quickly: for a thousand years are as yesterday, which is past, nor can that be called long which hath an end. He saith: "His wrath shall be kindled," to signify that the day of judgment will be a day of vengeance and justice alone, and not of mercy. He saith: "Blessed are they that trust," not that trust alone sufficeth, but because true trust ariseth from true friendship.
