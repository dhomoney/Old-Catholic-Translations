BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XXXVIII.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*Unto the end, for Idithun himself, a canticle of David.*

The title sheweth that the Psalm was composed by David, but given to Idithun himself to be sung. For Idithun was one of the three chiefs of the Singers, as we have in book I of *Paralip.* chap. xvi, and book II, chap. v. And what *in finem* (unto the end) is, or *pro victoria* (for victory), as St. Jerome translateth from the Hebrew, we have said in the explanation of Psalm IV. But in this place both *unto the end* and *for victory* agree with the matter which is treated of. For although the Rabbins and some later writers teach that David, sick of a bodily disease, wished for the end of life and of miseries, and asketh these things of the Lord in the present Psalm, yet we, following the ancient Fathers, judge that a far higher doctrine is contained in this divine canticle of the Holy Ghost, put forth by the Prophet. To wit, we judge that here is described the shortness of the present life, that we may understand that our hope is to be placed not in this life, but in God, in whom is life everlasting; and that therefore we must give ourselves to patience, and to peace with all men, and that the tongue especially is to be kept from all strife, and brawling, and contention, that we be not hindered from the race to the prize of the supernal vocation. For surely he who runneth for a prize of the greatest price, if in the midst of the race he should see a suit moved against him for his cloak, would rather let the cloak go, than suffer himself to be hindered from the race. Nor do men go to law, or contend, on earth, save for temporal things. Therefore the present Psalm containeth evangelical and apostolical doctrine. For the Lord also taught, "that to him who striketh us on the cheek we should offer also the other, and to him who would take away our cloak, we should give our coat also,"[^1] *Matt.* v; and the Apostle saith: "There is a fault among you, that you have lawsuits one with another: why do you not rather suffer yourselves to be defrauded?" I *Cor.* vi. This Psalm therefore, by reason of a doctrine so sublime, ought to be sung *unto the end,* that is *always,* even to the end of the world; and not only in that time of David and Idithun. The same Psalm is also rightly inscribed, *for victory,* because there can be no nobler victory, than by patience to overcome evil by good, and for a man to command himself.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. I SAID: I WILL TAKE HEED TO MY WAYS: THAT I SIN NOT WITH MY TONGUE.**[^2]

David was solicitous not to lose the true good; therefore he deliberated, and firmly determined with himself, to use great circumspection in all his actions, that, if it might be, he should not offend even in word, as though he had heard from the Apostle: "See how you walk circumspectly," *Ephes.* v; and from blessed James: "He that offendeth not in word, the same is a perfect man," *James* iii. He saith therefore: "I said," namely I resolved with myself, I decreed, I set a law to myself, "that I will take heed to my ways," that I may walk most warily in the way of this life, and diligently mark where I set my step, lest perchance I fall into a hole, or stumble against a stone, or choose a miry way for a clean one, or at least stray from the right road, that is, I deliberated, and determined to consider all my actions. And because nothing is easier, nor more dangerous, than to offend in word, since from words unadvisedly uttered arise strifes, contentions, brawlings, and other evils so many, that St. James said, "the tongue is a world of iniquity," *James* iii; therefore the Prophet addeth in particular: "That I sin not with my tongue," that is, for this chiefly will I take heed to my ways, that I sin not with my tongue: for so shall I escape infinite evils.

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. I HAVE SET GUARD TO MY MOUTH, WHEN THE SINNER STOOD AGAINST ME.**

Never is there greater danger of sinning by the tongue, than when we are provoked by some detractor, or one who doth us reproach: therefore the Prophet saith, "I have set guard to my mouth, when the sinner stood against me," that is, when some man of iniquity perchance provoked me with reviling, or detraction, or other injurious word, then most of all I guarded my mouth, lest it should break forth into some word, of which I should afterwards have to do penance.

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. I WAS DUMB, AND WAS HUMBLED, AND KEPT SILENCE FROM GOOD THINGS: AND MY SORROW WAS RENEWED.**

He declareth what guard he set to his mouth, saying: "I was dumb," that is, I was altogether silent, as if I were dumb; "and was humbled," that is, I humbly had patience; and "kept silence from good things," that is, I abstained even from just defence, and from due reproof; and "my sorrow was renewed," for this very thing, that I kept silence from good things. For I had sorrowed first by reason of the injury received, but the reward of patience had assuaged the sorrow: again therefore the sorrow was renewed, because "I kept silence from good things," and defrauded my neighbour of due correction. So St. Augustine; but it might also be thus expounded: "My sorrow was renewed," because I had sorrowed from my own suffering, but "the sorrow was renewed," from compassion for my brother, whom I saw to be so grievously sick, that the medicine of correction seemed like to profit him nothing, for which cause also "I kept silence from good things."

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. MY HEART GREW HOT WITHIN ME: AND IN MY MEDITATION A FIRE SHALL FLAME OUT.**

He setteth forth what that sorrow renewed wrought, and

<!-- p. 250 (PDF 261) -->

saith: "My heart grew hot within me," that is, from that sorrow of compassion my heart was warmed with love, and thence I began to meditate on human misery, on the mercy of God, on the ingratitude of men, on the abundance of the charity of God towards the ungrateful and the evil, and many other things of that kind. "And in my meditation a fire shall flame out," that fire, I say, of which those two disciples said in Luke, chap. xxiv: "Was not our heart burning within us, whilst he spoke in the way, and opened to us the scriptures?" For diligent and attentive meditation on heavenly things is the ordinary way to kindle the fire of the actual charity of God. I know that others expound otherwise that fire flaming out from meditation, namely of the fire of anger and indignation: but we have followed St. Ambrose, and the reason of the connexion, and the edification of the reader.

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. I SPOKE WITH MY TONGUE: O LORD, MAKE ME KNOW MY END.**

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. AND WHAT IS THE NUMBER OF MY DAYS: THAT I MAY KNOW WHAT IS WANTING TO ME.**

From that inward heat "I spoke, saith he, with my tongue," that is, I spoke not in the tongue which all understand, but in a tongue known to me, as if he said: I know what I meant to say. "O Lord, make me know my end," etc.; the sense seemeth to be, that he desireth to know the end of his life, that is, "the number of the years, and of the days which he is to live upon the earth," that he may know how much time remaineth to him until death; but he meant not to say this: for that would have been a vicious curiosity, and a kind of rashness; therefore he said before: "I spoke with my tongue," in my own sense, in a speech known to me. He meant therefore to signify this, that human life is most short, and as it were a kind of nothing. But because few know well this truth, though otherwise most clear, and most known by experience, therefore he prayeth God not to suffer him to be deceived, as very many are deceived, so as to think that lasting which is most short. For why is the greatest part of men wholly intent on heaping up earthly wealth, and fighteth for it, and contendeth most sharply, and contemneth and neglecteth the goods to come, but because it thinketh not, or believeth not, that the present life fleeth as a shadow? He saith therefore: "O Lord, make me know my end," that is, grant, O Lord, by thy grace, that the eyes of my mind may be enlightened, and that I may plainly know that the end of my life is now near at hand. "And what is the number of my days: that I may know what is wanting to me," that is, grant that by attentive thought I may penetrate how short is the number of my days, and may know how little time remaineth to me. That this is the true exposition the following verses will teach. For being heard by the Lord he relateth not precisely the number of the years which remained to him, but striveth in divers ways to shew that the time of human life is most short, especially if it be compared with the eternity of God and of the world to come.

[Margin: Human life most short, and as it were a kind of nothing.]

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. BEHOLD THOU HAST MADE MY DAYS MEASURABLE: AND MY SUBSTANCE IS AS NOTHING BEFORE THEE.**

Being taught by the Lord that which he had asked, he saith that *his days are measurable,* that is, that the duration of his life is so short, that it can most easily be measured. But it is to be observed that St. Jerome from the Hebrew, הנה טפחות נתתה ימי, translated, *ecce breves posuisti dies meos* (behold thou hast made my days short); that St. Augustine with others read, *ecce palæstras posuisti dies meos* (behold thou hast made my days wrestling-places);[^3] lastly that Chrysostom and others read, *ecce palmares posuisti dies meos* (behold thou hast made my days of a palm's breadth). But all these come to the same; the Hebrew word טפחות signifieth *res palmares* (things of a palm's breadth), that is, measurable by one palm: for the word טפח signifieth *mensuram palmi quatuor digitorum* (the measure of a palm of four fingers), which the Greek word παλέστη also signifieth, which we have in the edition of the Seventy Interpreters. Therefore St. Jerome rightly translated, *breves dies* (short days); for those are short, which can be measured by a most short measure. St. Augustine, and others who read, *veteres dies* (old days), perhaps had in the Greek text παλαίας for παλαιστάς; but yet the days of our life are rightly called *old days,* because, as the Apostle saith, *Heb.* viii: "That which decayeth and groweth old, is near its end." And we know that old garments, and old houses, and the like old things last not long. St. Ambrose derived the Greek word not from the measure, but from the wrestling-place (*palæstra*): yet by a most fit similitude he explained the most swift and most short, and at the same time most laborious course of our life. For they who run or fight in the wrestling-place have a time most short, but most laborious, as is known. Our edition hath rightly put, *mensurabiles dies* (measurable days), that is, which are so short, that they can be measured with no trouble: which *dies palmares* (days of a palm's breadth) also signifieth, as Chrysostom readeth from the interpretation of Symmachus. But the Prophet, not content to have said that his days are short and measurable, added as it were correcting himself: "And my substance is as nothing before thee," as if he said: I have said too little, that the days are short; for *my substance,* that is, my subsistence, this very thing that I am and subsist, is nothing before thee. It is indeed something before the eyes of men, who see not but things present, but *before thee,* who seest things to come, and beholdest eternity, which is without all measure, it is nothing at all. For what are a few years, which pass most swiftly, to an infinite duration? That the name *substantia* (substance) in this place signifieth nothing else than *subsistentiam* (subsistence) and the space of life, may be understood from the Hebrew word חלדי, which signifieth properly *tempus* (time), or *sæculum* (age), so that the sense is: *Tempus meum* (My time), or, as St. Jerome rendered, *vita nihil est coram te* (life is nothing before thee).

[Margin: The days of our life are rightly called old days.]

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. AND INDEED ALL THINGS ARE VANITY: EVERY MAN LIVING.**

He declareth yet more, and striveth to persuade us of that which he himself plainly perceived. Not only,

<!-- p. 251 (PDF 262) -->

saith he, is the time of our life most short, but *every man,* whosoever he be, even a king and monarch, whom all admire, and whom all the rest envy, he, I say, even *while he liveth,* that is, while he is in vigour, and flourisheth, and is in the best estate, or, as it is word for word in the Hebrew נצב *nitsab, dum stat, dum firmiter consistit* (while he standeth, while he standeth firm), and seemeth rooted in the earth, is nothing else but all vanity, or the whole sum of vanity. For whatsoever is in him, whether health, or might, or beauty, or riches, or dignity, or power, is all frail and momentary.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. SURELY MAN PASSETH AS AN IMAGE: YEA, AND HE IS DISQUIETED IN VAIN.**

The Prophet saw that mankind was weighed down with so deep a sleep, that, not content with the thunders of words which he hath hitherto used to rouse men, he addeth another yet more vehemently striking human minds, if perchance, sleep being shaken off, they may begin to watch. Because therefore some one might say that it seemeth not true that the life of men is nothing, since we see the life of men to overflow with so many goods, wealth, honours, health, might, and the like; therefore the Prophet now saith that these are not true goods, but images and shadows of good things; and that therefore men are troubled in vain, when they either cannot get these, or are forced to lose them when gotten. As a king would be troubled in vain for the loss of a wooden or paper image of his kingdom, when the true kingdom remained to him. "Surely, saith he, man passeth as an image (*in imagine*, in an image)," that is, man passeth, and walketh through the way of this life in an image of things, not in the truth of things, having, namely, before his eyes in all this journey not true things, but the shadows and images of things. For the life which we have here is an image of the blessed life, which alone is true life; the health of this time is an image of immortality, which alone is true health; the beauty which we see here is scarce a kind of shadow of that beauty which we shall have, when "the just shall shine as the sun, in the kingdom of their Father," as is said *Matt.* xiii. Earthly riches are not riches, but images of those riches which we shall have, when we shall want nothing at all, but God shall be all in all. The same may be said of wisdom, of glory, of high estate and of the rest, which are here called goods. Moreover that phrase, *in imagine pertransit,* or *perambulat homo* (man passeth, or walketh, in an image), is like to those phrases frequent in holy Scripture, *to walk in the light, in darkness, in faith, in fear, in love,* and that which is twice or thrice in the second and third epistle of the apostle John, *to walk in truth;* to which may be opposed, *to walk in an image,* inasmuch as an image is opposed to truth. "Yea, and he is disquieted in vain," that is, without any cause is man disquieted in anxiously seeking or keeping temporal things; in vain he rejoiceth in the gain of them, and in vain he weepeth in the loss of them, as though they were things solid and true, and abiding: whereas they are imaginary, frail and perishable. St. John Chrysostom, whom Theodoret and Euthymius follow, expoundeth that, *in imagine pertransit homo,* as if it had been said, *instar imaginis pertransit homo* (man passeth after the manner of an image), so that not only human things are images of true things, but man himself also is not so much a man, as the image of a man painted on a tablet.

[Margin: This life and the rest are a shadow and image of things to come.]

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. HE STORETH UP: AND HE KNOWETH NOT FOR WHOM HE SHALL GATHER THESE THINGS.**

He proveth by a single example that men are disquieted in vain in seeking, increasing, keeping wealth: because they know not for whom they labour. They indeed think that they store up for children, or grandchildren, who will be grateful to the memory of their parents; but it happeneth very often that the children or grandchildren straightway die, and the inheritance cometh to strangers, or enemies; or at least that the children or grandchildren scatter in a most short time what the father or grandfather got with great labour, and in a very long time; or lastly that the heirs are ungrateful, and render evil for good, detracting from the industry and labours of their forefathers; which if those men could have foreseen, they would rather have laid up to themselves treasures in heaven, and would have passed this life without disquiet. See like sentences in *Eccles.* chap. ii, iv, v and vi.

[Margin: Verse 11.]

**11. AND NOW WHAT IS MY HOPE? IS IT NOT THE LORD? AND MY SUBSTANCE IS WITH THEE.**

From the shortness and vanity of the present life so clearly perceived, he concludeth that he for his part hath determined to place his hope in God alone. "And now," saith he, these things being so, "what is my hope (*exspectatio*, expectation)?" what do I hope? what do I seek? what do I wish? "is it not the Lord" who is my expectation and hope? Lastly, turning to God himself he saith: "My substance is with thee," that is, my life and my wealth are with thee, I count all created things as nothing, I desire to have thee alone for all things, because in thee are all things. In the Hebrew it is had word for word: *Et nunc quid exspecto, Domine? exspectatio mea tibi ipsa* (And now what do I expect, O Lord? my expectation itself is to thee). From which we may understand that only these words answer to the Hebrew reading: *Et nunc quæ est exspectatio mea? nonne Dominus?* (And now what is my expectation? is it not the Lord?) But these, *et substantia mea apud te est* (and my substance is with thee), seem to have been added for explanation's sake by the Seventy Elders, which were faithfully rendered by our Latin Interpreter. Therefore we need not ask, as many do, why the Latin Interpreter put *substantiam* for the Hebrew word תוחלתי *thochalthi,* which signifieth *spem* (hope) or *exspectationem* (expectation). For the Latin Interpreter put not *substantiam* for *exspectatione;* but added to the Hebrew words that which the Seventy Interpreters added. The like is in *Ps.* xxi, where we read: "O God my God, look upon me: why hast thou forsaken me?" for that, *respice in me* (look upon me), is not had in the Hebrew copy, but was added by the Seventy

<!-- p. 252 (PDF 263) -->

Interpreters, and thence was carried over by the Latin Interpreter into the Vulgate edition.

[Margin: Verse 12.]

**12. DELIVER THOU ME FROM ALL MY INIQUITIES: THOU HAST MADE ME A REPROACH TO THE FOOL.**

Because he hath said that he contemneth earthly things, and expecteth God alone, and hath placed all his hopes in him, he consequently addeth that he is solicitous about sins against God, but not about the reproaches of men. "Deliver thou me, saith he, from all my iniquities," that is, because sins alone are they which can hinder my way to thee, and which alone are true evils; therefore I pray that thou wouldst free me from them, and indeed from all, not past ones only, but also future ones, by blotting out the former, by hindering the latter. In which place it is to be noted that even perfect men, who despise earthly things, and pant after God with their whole heart, have always something for which they ask to be pardoned, and ought to be solicitous to offer daily prayers to God for daily sins. "Thou hast made me a reproach to the fool." This part of the verse is joined with what followeth in this manner: Thou indeed hast made me a reproach to the fool, but I was dumb, and opened not my mouth. Now he signifieth that, because he said that present things are vain and to be contemned, and that hope is to be placed in God alone, he was wont to be derided by fools, who perceive not the things that are of God. As the Gospel also saith of Christ the Lord, *Luke* xvi: "The Pharisees, who were covetous, heard all these things: and they derided him." In the Hebrew there is the contrary meaning, namely, *opprobrium insipienti non pones me* (thou shalt not make me a reproach to the fool). But the reading of the Seventy Interpreters is to be preferred, as St. Ambrose admonisheth, because the following words: "I was dumb, and I opened not my mouth," manifestly teach that David heard the reproaches of fools, and so also read and expound Augustine, Chrysostom, and other ancient Fathers. St. Jerome himself also, who in his version from the Hebrew put the negative particle, yet in his Commentary readeth and expoundeth this place without such a particle. Wherefore that particle seemeth to have crept into the Hebrew text after the times of the Seventy Interpreters.

[Margin: Sins alone are true evils.]

[Margin: Verse 13.]

**13. I WAS DUMB, AND I OPENED NOT MY MOUTH, BECAUSE THOU HAST DONE IT. REMOVE THY SCOURGES FROM ME.**

When I heard reproaches from fools, I answered not, nor defended myself, but "I was dumb," that is, I so bore myself, as if I had been dumb; nay also "I opened not my mouth," but so bore myself, as if I had been deaf, and had not heard those reproaches. For they who are dumb, and not deaf, open their mouth, and endeavour to answer, when they hear anything; but they who are deaf and dumb neither speak, nor open their mouth. Now he giveth the reason why he was dumb, and opened not his mouth. "Because, saith he, thou hast done it," namely, that reproaches should be cast upon me: for thou art he who hast made me a reproach to fools. And this same reason the same David gave, when he was dumb at the cursing of Semei, II *Kings* chap. xv:[^4] "Let him alone, saith he, and let him curse: for the Lord hath bid him curse David: and who is he that shall dare say, why hath he done so?" Where yet it is to be observed that God did not bid Semei curse David with a command of such a kind, that obedience to it would be praised: for we know that Semei sinned grievously in cursing David, and for this was afterwards punished by Solomon; but God is said to have bidden Semei to curse, because, seeing the evil will of Semei, he used the evil will, which he himself had not made, to humble and chastise David. Of which matter see St. Augustine, book *On Grace and free will,* chap. ii and following; or if thou wilt read something treated more at large, see in our Controversies, book II *On the loss of grace, and the state of sin,* chap. xiii and following. "Remove thy scourges (*plagas*, stripes) from me," as if he said: I gladly receive the reproaches and revilings of fools, which thou with fatherly severity hast ordained for my humiliation; but thy stripes I cannot bear: therefore I pray that thou wouldst wholly remove them from me. For he calleth *the stripes of God* the scourges and punishments which God in anger sendeth, not as a father or physician, but as a judge, of which he said in the Psalm before: "Rebuke me not, O Lord, in thy indignation." Such stripes are blinding, hardening, a reprobate sense, and condemnation itself to everlasting fire.

[Margin: How did God bid Semei curse David?]

[Margin: Verse 14.]

**14. THE STRENGTH OF THY HAND HATH MADE ME FAINT IN REBUKES: THOU HAST CORRECTED MAN FOR INIQUITY.**

[Margin: Verse 15.]

**15. AND THOU HAST MADE HIS SOUL TO WASTE AWAY LIKE A SPIDER: SURELY IN VAIN IS ANY MAN DISQUIETED.**

He explaineth why he so greatly desireth the stripes of God to be removed from him, and saith the cause is, because he hath experienced in himself, and in others, how intolerable such stripes are. Of himself first he saith thus: "The strength of thy hand hath made me faint in rebukes," that is, I myself, having experienced the strength and power, or the weight, of thy hand striking, fainted, and was plainly consumed, and perished "in rebukes," that is, in that cruel rebuke, when thou didst rebuke me in thy indignation. Now this befell David, when for the sin of adultery committed God, in punishment of it, permitted him to fall into the sin of murder, which is more grievous, and into so great blindness, that for many months he returned not to himself, nor knew his state, that is the death of his soul: for there is no punishment more grievous, than when the punishment of sin is another sin. For that sin is sometimes the punishment of sin, and indeed a punishment horrible, and to be feared above all punishments, the Apostle teacheth, *Rom.* i: "Who, when they knew God, have not glorified him as God: wherefore God gave

[Margin: There is no more grievous punishment of sin, than when the punishment of sin [?]]

<!-- p. 253 (PDF 264) -->

them up to the desires of their heart, unto uncleanness, to dishonour their own bodies;" and below: "For this cause God delivered them up to shameful affections;" and below: "And as they liked not to have God in their knowledge, God delivered them up to a reprobate sense, to do those things which are not convenient." See of this scourge St. Augustine, book V *against Julian,* chap. iii. Then the Prophet addeth concerning other sinners, punished by God with this cruel stripe: "Thou hast corrected man for iniquity. And thou hast made his soul to waste away like a spider," that is, for sins going before thou hast corrected in thy indignation the sinful man, and hast made his soul to waste away after the manner of a spider, which laboureth in weaving her web, that she may catch flies, and meanwhile is herself dried up and consumed. For so the souls of carnal men, by the just judgment of God, labour perpetually in getting temporal things, and in that labour consume wit and mind, and thence the soul is dried of all moisture of grace, so that it thinketh not even of its salvation, nor is held by any desire of true felicity. Against which dryness of soul the same Prophet in another Psalm, the LXII, desired spiritual marrow and fatness, saying: "Let my soul be filled as with marrow and fatness." The Prophet concludeth: "Surely in vain is any man disquieted," that is, every man whose soul wasteth away like a spider is disquieted in vain, laboureth to no purpose, is tossed with so many contentions without any cause. "*For* what doth it profit a man, if he gain the whole world, and suffer the loss of his own soul?" *Matt.* xvi. But in this place many things are to be observed: *first,* that those words, *in increpationibus* (in rebukes), in the Hebrew and Greek text, and in Ambrose and Jerome, are joined with the words following, namely thus: *In increpationibus propter iniquitatem corripuisti hominem* (In rebukes for iniquity thou hast corrected man); yet in Augustine, and in our common books, they are joined with those above, namely with these: *A fortitudine manus tuæ ego defeci in increpationibus* (The strength of thy hand hath made me faint in rebukes); but in either way the sense standeth. *Secondly,* note that St. Jerome translateth from the Hebrew not *araneam* (spider), but *tineam* (moth); but it is credible that the Hebrew word signifieth both. Surely the likeness is great between the spider and the moth; for as the spider by weaving her web drieth herself up, so also the moth by gnawing cloth destroyeth herself. *Thirdly,* note that where we have from the Greek text, *animam ejus* (his soul), in the Hebrew there is, חמודו *chamudo, desiderabile ejus* (that of his which is desirable); wherefore the Seventy Interpreters did not so much render the words, as expound them. For truly the soul endowed with reason is that which in man is most beautiful, precious and desirable; for it is the image of God, it is eternal, it is capable of eternal felicity, and by it we have fellowship with the angels, as by the flesh we have fellowship with the beasts; wherefore it is an incredible blindness, for the fulfilling of the appetites of the flesh, to lose that which in us is supremely precious and desirable. *Fourthly,* note that the verb *conturbatur* (is disquieted) is not had in the Hebrew or Greek text, but only *vane omnis homo* (in vain every man), or *vanitas omnis homo* (every man is vanity). But the Latin Interpreter added the verb for explanation's sake, and added that which is had above in verse 9. Note lastly that there are not wanting those who expound these two verses either of the scourge of temporal calamity, or of fatherly correction, which maketh men, who before were of a stiff neck, soft as spiders, that is, humble and contrite. But in those things which are not against faith, and are most obscure, it is lawful for every man to abound in his own sense. Us those words, *remove thy stripes from me,* have easily persuaded that here it is not treated of a temporal scourge, since in the Psalm before the same David said: "I am ready for scourges;" nor of fatherly correction, but of that terrible correction which the just most dread: for fatherly correction, which maketh men humble and contrite, the just love and desire, they do not dread or flee it. The similitude also of the spider wasting away hath seemed to us to signify nothing good. Yet we do not blame, but rather praise the explanations of others, which profit unto edification, though they seem not to us literal.

[Margin: [?] another sin.]

[Margin: The human soul [?]]

**16. HEAR MY PRAYER, O LORD, AND MY SUPPLICATION: GIVE EAR TO MY TEARS.**

With great affection he concludeth the Psalm, praying God; but what he prayeth for shall be explained a little after. Meanwhile this is to be known, that by *prayer* is understood a simple petition; but by *supplication* a vehement cry, as the Hebrew word ושועתי sheweth; and by *tears* the affection, whose voice soundeth more before God than any words. In the Hebrew and Greek, as they now stand, those words, *ne sileas* (be not silent), which we have in the following verse, are continued with this verse; and in that manner three verbs answer to the three nouns in this way: *Exaudi orationem meam, deprecationem meam auribus percipe, et lacrymas meas ne sileas* (Hear my prayer, give ear to my supplication, and be not silent to my tears). But the ancient Fathers, Ambrose, Jerome and Augustine, have our reading in their Commentaries on this place, and the Hebrew words, the stops which the Rabbins added being removed, very well admit the division of our books.

[Margin: Verse 17.]

**17. BE NOT SILENT: FOR I AM A STRANGER WITH THEE, AND A SOJOURNER AS ALL MY FATHERS WERE.**

He still asketh to be heard, and doth not yet explain his petition; but he speaketh to him who knoweth what the spirit desireth. "Be not silent," saith he, that is, answer him that asketh, despise not him that crieth; for he who is silent when a petition is heard doth the same as he who will not grant what is asked. Now he giveth the cause why he ought to hear, saying: "For I am a stranger with thee, and a sojourner as all my fathers were," that is, because thou knowest, and it is within thy knowledge, that I am not a citizen of this

<!-- p. 254 (PDF 265) -->

world, but a stranger and a sojourner, and by this a citizen of the city of the Jerusalem above, though I dwell here for the while; and it is fitting that thou hear a citizen of thy city set in exile, and crying to thee out of his pilgrimage. St. John Chrysostom noteth in this place that David was a great and spiritual man, who, though he abounded in the glory of a kingdom and in earthly wealth, yet truly acknowledged himself a stranger and a sojourner on the earth.

[Margin: Verse 18.]

**18. O FORGIVE ME, THAT I MAY BE REFRESHED, BEFORE I GO HENCE, AND BE NO MORE.**

Here he now setteth forth his petition, and it is that of which it is said in *Ps.* xxxi: "For this shall every one that is holy pray to thee in a seasonable time." He asketh therefore with crying and tears the pardon of sins, that with a quieted conscience he may come joyful from his pilgrimage to his country, and in sum he asketh grace and glory; which is that petition, which they alone, and all they, set before God with their whole heart, who acknowledge the vanity of the world. He saith therefore: "O forgive me (*remitte mihi*);" or, as St. Jerome translateth, *parce mihi* (spare me); or, as it might be translated word for word from the Hebrew, *desine a me* (cease from me), that is, be not a hard exactor, press not for the payment of debts, cease to demand again of me what I have ill wasted. "That I may be refreshed, before I go hence," that is, that before my departure out of this life I may be refreshed, understanding that my sins are forgiven me: for unless thou forgive here, I shall not go to rest, but to prison. Say therefore to my soul: I am thy salvation, before it be bidden to go forth from the body. "And be no more," namely a stranger and a sojourner, but a fellow citizen with the Saints, and a domestic of God. Or, before "I go hence, and be no more," that is, before I depart out of this life, and lose all life: for he who after this life hath not life everlasting, consequently hath everlasting death, and a kind of not being, since he is very far away from him who is. For they who are condemned to hell are and live; but they are said not to be, nor to live, because they have being and life only to this end, that they may always die, and desire not to be. St. Augustine hath the latter sense, but St. Ambrose toucheth both.
