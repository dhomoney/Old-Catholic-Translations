BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XXII.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*Unto the end, a Psalm for David.*[^1]

The title showeth that the author of the Psalm was David: but the argument of the Psalm is the singular benevolence of God toward the elect.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. THE LORD RULETH ME: AND I SHALL WANT NOTHING. HE HATH SET ME IN A PLACE OF PASTURE.**

In this Psalm is described the felicity of the elect of God by the likeness of sheep, which are ruled by some excellent shepherd. In their person David, who was one of them, saith: "The Lord ruleth me: and I shall want nothing," as if he said: I am a little sheep of God, and because God is a shepherd most mighty, most wise and most good, therefore I say with confidence: "I shall want nothing." This is the voice of a man most happy on the way, and in hope. For the blessed in their country and in possession do not say: "I shall want nothing," but: I want nothing; since labour and sorrow have fled from them, and they have now entered into the joy of their Lord. But the blessed on the way and in hope cannot say: I want nothing, seeing they are subject to many sufferings; but they rightly say: "I shall want nothing," because if they shall hunger, bread will not be wanting; if they shall be sick, the physician will not be wanting; and so of the rest. Now that word *regit* (ruleth), in the Hebrew and Greek text properly signifieth *to feed*, and therefore the sense is: The Lord ruleth me after the manner of a shepherd, as a shepherd ruleth his little sheep. And what he said in sum: "I shall want nothing," he declareth part by part in the words that follow. Now these seem to be the things which little sheep are wont to need: *first*, rich pastures; *secondly*, pure waters; *thirdly*, that they be brought back, if perchance they have strayed; *fourthly*, that they be led by convenient ways; *fifthly*, that they be defended from wolves and other wild beasts; *sixthly*, that being wearied they be upheld; *seventhly*, that if, going through thistles and stones, they be hurt, they be refreshed and restored; lastly, that when the day is spent, and the journey ended, they have a house, where they may rest. All these things God abundantly bestoweth on his elect, and for this cause they rightly say: "I shall want nothing." David beginneth from the first, when he addeth: "He hath set me in a place of pasture" (*In loco pascuæ, ibi me collocavit*, in a place of pasture, there he hath set me), that is, he hath not set me in a place dry, or desert, but in most plentiful pastures, where there is the greatest abundance of wholesome herbs. The particle[^2] *ibi* (there) is not in the Hebrew, nor is it necessary; it was added by the Greek interpreter, and thence carried over into the Latin edition, but it is superfluous. Now the fodder, or spiritual pastures of the reasonable sheep of God, with which the elect abound, are nothing else but the knowledge of God, the Eucharist, the bread of the word of God, Truth itself. For this it is which sustaineth and increaseth the spiritual life.

[Margin: The blessed on the way say: I shall want nothing. The blessed in their country: I want nothing.]

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. HE HATH BROUGHT ME UP, ON THE WATER OF REFRESHMENT: HE HATH CONVERTED MY SOUL.**

He toucheth the second and third good necessary to little sheep. "He hath brought me up, on the water of refreshment," that is, he hath not only set me in a place abounding with herbs, but also in a place near to wholesome waters, and so he hath brought me up, and nourished me with the best fodder lying upon the streams of waters, that I may be cooled from the heat and the burning of thirst. It mattereth little, that the Hebrew and Greek word signifieth not so much *refreshment* as *rest*. For the sense is the same. For he who quencheth the burning of thirst with wholesome waters both resteth and is refreshed. The spiritual water, which quencheth the burning of concupiscence in the reasonable sheep, is the grace of God, of which the Lord in the Gospel, *John* iv, 14:[^3] "He that shall drink of the water that I will give him, shall not thirst for ever." For nothing so represseth carnal desires as the taste of the love of God. For when the spirit hath been tasted, all flesh loseth its savour. "He hath converted my soul," this is the third gift, namely of bringing back. For neither can man by his own strength alone return to God, as he can by his own strength alone turn away. He saith therefore: The good shepherd sought me when I strayed, and brought me back, which is a great benefit, and proper to the elect, that they are not suffered to stray for ever.

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. HE HATH LED ME ON THE PATHS OF JUSTICE, FOR HIS OWN NAME'S SAKE.**

He recounteth the fourth good: "He hath led me," saith he, "on the paths of justice," that is, he hath made me to walk by the narrow way of his commandments, that I might live justly. And this he did by removing the occasions of sinning, by lessening the strength of the tempter, by increasing charity, by making justice to grow sweet, by enlightening, inflaming, alluring, terrifying, and in other innumerable ways. Which ways if we would mark, we should not cease at every moment to give thanks to so sweet a Shepherd, especially since he doth this not for our preceding

merits, but "for his own name's sake," that is, "that he may make known the riches of his mercy, unto the praise of the glory of his grace," as the Apostle saith, *Ephes.* i.[^4]

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. FOR THOUGH I SHOULD WALK IN THE MIDST OF THE SHADOW OF DEATH, I WILL FEAR NO EVILS, FOR THOU ART WITH ME.**

This is the fifth good, namely defence from wolves and other beasts of the forest. "For though I should walk in the midst," or, as the Hebrew text hath it, בגיא צלמות, *in the valley of the shadow of death*, that is, through places dark, and exposed to all dangers, of wild beasts, of robbers, of precipices, "I will fear no evils, for thou art with me." For "if God be for us, who is against us?" And truly there is an incredible security in the heart of him that loveth God, while he considereth that he is with him, whom nothing can resist. Most frequent in the books of the Prophets is the mention of the *shadow of death*, which properly signifieth thick darkness, such as death bringeth, which taketh away all light. For the blind are said to be in darkness, because they see nothing, and much more are the dead said to be in darkness, because they perceive nothing. Hence it is that the Poets say the places of the dead are shadowy, and overspread with darkness. Hence also the holy Scriptures everywhere join the shadow of death with darkness, that the one may be explained by the other. *Job* iii: "Let darkness, and the shadow of death cover it"; and chap. x: "To a land that is dark and covered with the mist of death"; *Isa.* ix: "The people that walked in darkness, have seen a great light: to them that dwelt in the region of the shadow of death, light is risen," where to dwell in darkness, and to dwell in the region of the shadow of death, are taken for the same thing. But because dark places are exposed to many dangers, and we are wont to journey through them not without exceeding fear; therefore David saith in this place: "Though I should walk in the midst of the shadow of death," that is, in the midst of exceeding darkness, where many dangers stand round on every side, "I will fear nothing, for thou art with me."

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. THY ROD AND THY STAFF, THEY HAVE COMFORTED ME.**

The sixth good is explained, which is the upholding of the wearied. For now the Prophet, leaving the likeness of sheep, speaketh properly of men; for weary sheep are not upheld by a staff, but are carried on the shoulders of the shepherds. And although these words might be referred to fatherly chastisement, as St. Augustine doth, yet it is more probable that to the letter they are to be referred to upholding, as Theodoret and Euthymius refer them. For the Hebrew word משענתך properly signifieth *a prop*, that is, *a staff that proppeth*: as also the Greek word βακτηρία is derived from *upholding*, whence βακτηριύειν[^5] is *to lean upon a staff*. Unless perhaps it please to refer the rod to chastisement, and the staff to upholding. For God, when he seeth his own grow weary on the way, and go forward more slowly than were meet, is wont to use both, the word of rebuke and the word of comfort, that by the one he may rouse, by the other uphold. But both at last bring comfort, since the soul rejoiceth, when it seeth that its progress is a care to God.

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. THOU HAST PREPARED A TABLE BEFORE ME AGAINST THEM THAT AFFLICT ME.**

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. THOU HAST ANOINTED MY HEAD WITH OIL; AND MY CHALICE WHICH INEBRIATETH ME, HOW GOODLY IS IT!**

This is the seventh good, namely that God wonderfully comforteth his elect in the tribulations, which in this life we cannot escape. Wherefore the sense of these verses is not, that God hath prepared for the elect a table, oil, and wine against the enemies, as if these were arms, with which the enemies are to be fought; but the sense is, that God setteth against great tribulations great consolations; and as bitternesses are heaped on by the enemies, so sweetnesses are heaped on by God, which are described after the manner of men by the likeness of a banquet, in which there is a table filled with the best meats, there is present also precious ointment, with which the head is sprinkled, nor is there wanting excellent wine, and most fit for inebriating, that is, for making glad. For the ancients were wont in great banquets to use precious ointments among the chief delights, which we see also observed in the Gospel, *Matt.* xxvi, by St. Mary Magdalen toward the Lord. Now those words, *adversus eos qui tribulant me* (against them that afflict me), are clearer in the Greek and Hebrew text. For the Greek ἐξ ἐναντίας, and the Hebrew נגד *neged*, signify *over against*, or *ex adverso* (on the opposite side), as St. Jerome rendereth. Therefore the sense is: over against the tribulations, which my spiritual enemies heap upon me, thou *hast prepared*, or *hast ordered*, as the Hebrew word soundeth, an exceeding consolation, namely a sumptuous table. "Thou hast anointed my head with oil," that is, thou hast poured precious ointment upon my head, and so hast made my face cheerful with oil, and there is not wanting by thy grace a chalice of wine, inebriating and goodly, to cheer my heart. There is a like sentence in *Psalm* xciii: "According to the multitude of my sorrows in my heart, thy comforts have given joy to my soul." And in the Apostle, II *Cor.* vii, 4: "I am filled with comfort: I exceedingly abound with joy in all our tribulation." That phrase, *calix meus* (my chalice), is in the Greek text *calix tuus* (thy chalice), and so Augustine and Euthymius read; but in the Hebrew there is כוסי *cosi*, *calix meus*, and so also St. Jerome rendereth. It is probable that the more correct Greek copies had τὸ ποτήριόν μου, *calix meus*, as St. Jerome readeth in the Commentary, where he expoundeth the version of the Seventy, and so Theodoret seemeth to have read. Nay, St. Jerome in the epistle *to Sunnia and Fretela*[^6] saith that in the edition of the Seventy there is *calix meus*, and that by an error there is in certain copies *calix tuus*. Therefore our Latin reading is altogether to be retained: but the sense

[Margin: God setteth against tribulations great consolations.]

is always the same. For that chalice is both mine, because given to me; and God's, because given by God.

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. AND THY MERCY WILL FOLLOW ME ALL THE DAYS OF MY LIFE.**

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. AND THAT I MAY DWELL IN THE HOUSE OF THE LORD UNTO LENGTH OF DAYS.**

This is the last good, which leadeth through to the highest good. "Thy mercy will follow me," not for a time, but perseveringly even to the end, which is the gift proper to the elect. "And that I may dwell," that is, and it will follow me, that "I may dwell in the house of the Lord unto length of days," that is, for ever. In the Hebrew and in the Greek text there is not *ut* (that), but only *et* (and): on the other hand some of the ancients, as Augustine, Jerome, Theodoret, in their Commentaries do not read *et*, but only *ut*, and certainly without the one or the other the reading would be clearer, namely thus: *Misericordia tua subsequetur me omnibus diebus vitæ meæ, ut inhabitem in domo Domini* (Thy mercy will follow me all the days of my life, that I may dwell in the house of the Lord), as the Fathers read; or thus: *Misericordia tua subsequetur me omnibus diebus vitæ meæ, et inhabitabo in domo Domini* (Thy mercy will follow me all the days of my life, and I shall dwell in the house of the Lord), as St. Jerome rendereth from the Hebrew. For in these readings nothing is to be understood. Yet our reading is not so obscure, that it cannot be understood easily enough. For that word *subsequetur* (will follow) is understood, which goeth a little before, namely: *Thy mercy will follow me, while I live*, that I may live rightly, *and will follow after this life*, that I may live for ever. Wherefore St. Augustine, although in the text he did not read *et*, yet in the Commentary addeth *etiam* (also), so that he seemeth to have understood it. Now for the word *inhabitem* (I may dwell), in the Hebrew there is שבתי, which signifieth *I shall return*. But the Seventy seem to have read ישבתי, one little letter being added, and so they rightly rendered, *ut inhabitem*. So also St. Jerome read, who did not render *I shall return*, but *I shall dwell*. Wherefore the Hebrew reading seemeth to have been corrupted after the times of St. Jerome.

[^1]: Translator's note: the Douay-Rheims title is "A psalm for David"; the words "Unto the end" are not in the Vulgate title, which is *Psalmus David*. Bellarmine does not number the title, and divides the Psalm into nine verses. The Douay-Rheims Bible has six: its verse 1 holds the title and the first half of his verse 1; its 2, the rest of his 1 and the first half of his 2; its 3, the rest of his 2 and his 3; its 4, his 4 and 5; its 5, his 6 and 7; its 6, his 8 and 9.

[^2]: Translator's note: the print has *Articula*, taken as a misprint for *Particula*.

[^3]: Translator's note: in the Douay-Rheims these words are in verse 13.

[^4]: Translator's note: the last words are at Ephes. i, 6. The first are not there in these words; compare Rom. ix, 23, "that he might shew the riches of his glory on the vessels of mercy".

[^5]: Translator's note: so the print. The usual form of the verb is βακτηρεύειν.

[^6]: Translator's note: the print has *ad Sunium et Fretellam*. The letter is St. Jerome's Epistle cvi, to the Goths Sunnia and Fretela, on readings of the Psalter.
