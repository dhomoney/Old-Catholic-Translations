BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XXX.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*Unto the end, a psalm for David, in an ecstasy.*

[Margin: What an ecstasy is.]

In this title there is nothing new, except that word *exstasis* (ecstasy), which is not in the Hebrew, and perhaps was not added by the Seventy Interpreters, but by some other interpreter: for Theodoret witnesseth that he found it in certain copies only. St. Jerome also hath it not, neither in his version from the Hebrew, nor in the Commentary, where he expoundeth the version of the Seventy Interpreters. Whoever added that particle, took it from verse 28 of this Psalm: *But I said in the excess of my mind.* Now *exstasis* signifieth an excess of mind, when the soul is so intent upon some thing, that it seemeth as it were to have forsaken its own body. And this excess ariseth either from some divine revelation, which carrieth away the mind to behold some secret thing, or from a vehement passion of fear or of love. But in this place there seemeth to be understood an ecstasy from the sudden and very great fear, with which David was seized, when he heard that Absalom his son and all the people had conspired against him. Which history is in II *Kings* xv. Now the Psalm seemeth to be understood to the letter of David fleeing from the persecution of Absalom, as Theodoret and Euthymius will have it. But it can also be applied to the Church groaning amid the straits of persecutions.[^1]

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. IN THEE, O LORD, HAVE I HOPED, LET ME NEVER BE CONFOUNDED: DELIVER ME IN THY JUSTICE.**[^2]

King David, fleeing from Absalom who pursued him, destitute of all human help, hath recourse to God, and saith: "In thee have I hoped," and therefore I trust that I shall never be disappointed of my hope, because thou art almighty and most faithful. Therefore according to this my hope, "deliver me in thy justice," that is, for the justice whereby thou art wont to punish the wicked, and to deliver the innocent.

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. BOW DOWN THY EAR TO ME: MAKE HASTE TO DELIVER ME.**

Because the persecution was pressing, and the friends of David, II *Kings* xvii, had signified to him by messengers, that he should not rest, but should go on fleeing, unless he would be overwhelmed, therefore he asketh to be heard speedily, and to be delivered from the danger that hung over him.

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. BE THOU UNTO ME A GOD, A PROTECTOR, AND A HOUSE OF REFUGE, TO SAVE ME.**

The words are clear, because the Seventy Interpreters have expounded the metaphorical words, which are in the Hebrew copy; for thus it is read word for word: *Be thou unto me a most strong rock, and a most fortified house, to save me.* Now in this place there is called a most strong rock either a stony cliff set on high, or a house or citadel built of the strongest stones; and then the words following will be an explanation of those going before, so that the sense is: Be thou unto me, O God, like a most hard rock, that is, like a most fortified house, or citadel, into which I may safely flee. Now all these things are plainly said most fitly to the matter in hand. For David was fleeing through the plains of the wilderness, where there was no citadel, into which he could flee.

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. FOR THOU ART MY STRENGTH AND MY REFUGE; AND FOR THY NAME'S SAKE THOU WILT LEAD ME, AND NOURISH ME.**

Here also the Seventy have expounded the metaphors of the Hebrew words: for thus we read in the Hebrew, *for thou art my rock, and my fortress,* that is, be thou to me in place of a citadel of stone and fortified, since I can have no other fortress.

<!-- p. 177 (PDF 188) -->

That which followeth: "And for thy name's sake thou wilt lead me, and nourish me," answereth very well to the history. For David by reason of his sudden flight knew neither whither he should go, nor whence he should procure food: whence it came to pass by divine providence, that Siba met him with two hundred loaves of bread, and a hundred bunches of raisins, and two vessels of wine. And so he saith: "For thy name's sake," that is, unto the glory of thy name thou wilt lead me to a safe place, and wilt provide meats for my nourishment. Some more recent writers render it, *duces me, et deduces me* (thou wilt lead me, and guide me): which is strange, since not only the Seventy, but also St. Jerome, both in this place, and in *Ps.* xxiii,[^3] will have the Hebrew word נהל *nahal* to signify *enutrire* (to nourish), or *sustentare* (to sustain).

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. THOU WILT BRING ME OUT OF THIS SNARE, WHICH THEY HAVE HIDDEN FOR ME: FOR THOU ART MY PROTECTOR.**

Not only, saith he, wilt thou lead me to a safe place, and there nourish me, but thou wilt also deliver me from the ambushes, which they have laid for me as a hidden snare: for he calleth a snare the secret conspiracy made with Absalom in Hebron against him, who feared no such thing, nor thought of it. To that, *for thou art my protector,* there is added, *Domine* (O Lord), in the Greek text, but St. Jerome affirmeth in his epistle *to Sunia and Fretella* that it is superfluous.

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. INTO THY HANDS I COMMEND MY SPIRIT: THOU HAST REDEEMED ME, O LORD, THE GOD OF TRUTH.**

Although he said with good hope: "Thou wilt bring me out of this snare," yet because he was not yet secure of his life, he addeth: "Into thy hands I commend," or, as it is in the Hebrew and the Greek text, *commendabo* (I will commend) "my spirit," that is, to thy care I will commend my life. For thou often at other times "hast redeemed me from death": thou, I say, O Lord, who art "the God of truth," that is, the most true God, or the most faithful God. Because of this place some suspect that the whole Psalm is to be understood of Christ: for he himself hanging on the cross said, *Luke* xxiii: "Father, into thy hands I commend my spirit." But even if the Psalm be not understood to the letter of Christ, yet the Lord could take these words from this Psalm, when he would commend his spirit to God the Father: as also St. Nicholas, when dying, recited this Psalm as far as the words, which we are now expounding; and we very often use the same words. Certainly those words of the same verse, "thou hast redeemed me, O Lord, the God of truth," are not rightly attributed to Christ, since he is himself the Redeemer, not the redeemed. St. Augustine attributeth to Christ the former words: "Into thy hands I commend my spirit"; the latter he attributeth to the Christian people: "Thou hast redeemed me, O Lord, the God of truth"; for St. Augustine will have it that in this Psalm the persons are often changed, and that now the Prophet speaketh, now Christ, and now the people. Which indeed is rightly and piously said, when the mystical explanation is sought: but

[Margin: Christ is the Redeemer, not the redeemed.]

when the sense of the letter is searched out, changes of persons seem not to be understood, where there is no distinction, or pointing, to mark that the sentence is ended; but in this place the words of the whole verse run on without such pointing in all the copies, Hebrew, Greek and Latin.

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. THOU HAST HATED THEM THAT REGARD VANITIES, TO NO PURPOSE.**

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. BUT I HAVE HOPED IN THE LORD: I WILL BE GLAD AND REJOICE IN THY MERCY.**

He giveth another reason, why he hath commended his life into the hands of God, namely because God is wont to hate them, that hope not in his help, but in vain things, which can bring no aid. "Thou hast hated," saith he, "them that regard vanities," that is, dreams, or auguries, or the answers of devils; which Saul did, who consulted the woman that had a divining spirit. By the name of vanity there can also be understood human industry, or craftiness, or any counsel and help of men whatsoever, which without God, or against God, hath no strength; therefore there is added, "to no purpose," because in vain do men lean upon vain things. "But I," saith he, have not regarded, nor looked upon vain things, to put my trust in them; but "I have hoped in the Lord," and therefore "I will be glad and rejoice in thy mercy," because indeed the divine mercy forsaketh not them that hope in the Lord. From the Hebrew the more recent writers read, *odivi observantes vanitates* (I have hated them that regard vanities); and truly the Hebrew word שנאתי signifieth *odivi* (I have hated); but the Seventy and St. Jerome read *sanetha*, which signifieth *odisti* (thou hast hated); therefore the change of the text was made after the times of St. Jerome. The sense is always true, but our reading cohereth better with what followeth; for *odivi vanitates* (I have hated vanities) is not rightly joined with that, *ego autem speravi* (but I have hoped).

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. FOR THOU HAST REGARDED MY HUMILITY, THOU HAST SAVED MY SOUL OUT OF DISTRESSES.**

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. AND THOU HAST NOT SHUT ME UP IN THE HANDS OF THE ENEMY: THOU HAST SET MY FEET IN A SPACIOUS PLACE.**

He allegeth past benefits, that, showing himself grateful, he may provoke God to grant the like. The sense is: "I will be glad and rejoice in thy mercy," because I have a pledge of mercy, the deliverance from the persecution of Saul; for then "thou hast regarded my humility," that is, my abjection and my affliction: for all these things the Hebrew word עני signifieth. Thou hast also then saved my soul, that is, out of the straits, in which I stuck fast of necessity, nor could I free myself from them. In the Hebrew it is word for word, *thou hast known the straits of my soul,* but the sense is the same; for the Seventy wished not to render word for word, but to explain the meaning in some manner. The very same is found, or is explained, in the verse following: "And thou hast not shut me up in the hands of the enemy," that is, thou hast not suffered Saul, who sought me to slay me, to obtain his desire; but "thou hast set my feet in a spacious place," that is, thou hast brought it about that, being delivered out of the straits in which I stuck fast,

<!-- p. 178 (PDF 189) -->

I could walk whithersoever I would, as one set in an open and free field.

[Margin: Verse 11.]

**11. HAVE MERCY ON ME, O LORD, FOR I AM AFFLICTED: MY EYE IS TROUBLED WITH WRATH, MY SOUL, AND MY BELLY.**

From the remembrance of past benefits he prayeth for future ones, and relateth his calamities. "Have mercy on me, O Lord, for I am afflicted," that is, as at other times when I was afflicted, thou hadst mercy on me, so now also have mercy on me, for tribulation hath come upon me again. Then he beginneth to number his present tribulations. "My eye is troubled with wrath." That phrase, *in ira* (with wrath), is ambiguous, for it can be understood of the wrath of God, and of the wrath of David. I would rather with St. Augustine understand it of the wrath of God; for it is certain from Books I and II of *Kings*, that David in the persecution of Absalom, and in all his other persecutions, never grew hot with anger, but was always most meek and most patient. And so the sense will be, *in thy wrath,* with which thou punishest my sin, *my eye is troubled,* that is, the eye of the body is grown dim with tears, or, the inward eye is darkened; *my soul* also is troubled, while it is shaken with great fear, and lastly *my belly* is troubled, the inward belly, I say, of my soul, that is the memory, which is the receptacle of thoughts. Thus briefly the holy Prophet maketh mention of all the three parts of the soul, since he would have the understanding to be meant by the eye, the will by the soul, the memory by the belly.

[Margin: The inward belly is the memory.]

[Margin: Verse 12.]

**12. FOR MY LIFE IS WASTED WITH GRIEF: AND MY YEARS IN SIGHS.**

David had now come to old age, and truly he saith that *his life is wasted with grief,* that is, that his age hath been spent in continual sorrow, *and his years in sighs.* For in his first thirty years he suffered almost continual persecutions; then, being brought to the helm of the kingdom, for the first seven years he waged war with the offspring of Saul; afterwards he waged many wars both with the neighbouring nations, and with his own son. Lastly the very care of governing, if a man will discharge his office not carelessly, but in earnest with the fear of God, maketh him groan under the burden.

[Margin: Verse 13.]

**13. MY STRENGTH IS WEAKENED THROUGH POVERTY AND MY BONES ARE DISTURBED.**

Besides the afflictions of his whole past life, there came this last tribulation, in which, being enfeebled by want, he lost his former powers. For *my strength is weakened,* and, *my bones are disturbed,* signify the same; for by the bones might is understood, as also by strength. That these things are most true to the letter, the history of Book II of *Kings*, chap. xv, xvi and xvii, plainly teacheth. For David fled without any provision, and went on to desert places, and made the journey not only on foot, but even barefoot, whence afterwards he was helped with provision by his friends. In the Hebrew for *paupertate* (poverty) there is read בעוני, *iniquitate* (iniquity), and so St. Jerome rendereth it, *infirmata est in iniquitate virtus mea* (my strength is weakened through iniquity); but the Seventy did not read עוני *havoni*, as St. Jerome read, but עני, in which word there are the same letters, one taken away, but different points, and that signifieth *iniquity*, this *poverty*. Both senses are true; for although the weakness had arisen from the want of food, yet that want without doubt proceeded from the iniquity of the persecutors, and the iniquity of the persecutors from the sin of David, which was in that manner punished by God.

[Margin: Verse 14.]

**14. I AM BECOME A REPROACH AMONG ALL MY ENEMIES, AND VERY MUCH TO MY NEIGHBOURS; AND A FEAR TO MY ACQUAINTANCE.**[^4]

He declareth another calamity of his which arose from that most notorious persecution, namely that he began to be despised by the neighbouring peoples, who heard of his so shameful flight; and that his acquaintance and friends began to fear, lest if they clave to him, they should be in danger of their life with Absalom, if haply he should come off conqueror. As to the words, that, *super omnes inimicos meos* (above all my enemies), is in the Hebrew מכל צרריו; but the Seventy and St. Jerome read it with iota, צררי, which can be expounded, *ab omnibus inimicis meis* (by all my enemies); likewise, *apud omnes inimicos meos* (among all my enemies): which last exposition pleased St. Jerome, who rendereth from the Hebrew, *apud omnes hostes meos* (among all my foes); and it seemeth also to have pleased the Seventy Interpreters, for they rendered, παρὰ πάντας τοῦς ἐχθρούς μου, which signifieth, *ad omnes* (unto all), or *apud omnes* (among all); but the reading of the Latin edition agreeth with the second understanding of the Hebrew word, which is very proper to it; for *præ omnibus* (before all) and *super omnes* (above all) are the same. The first understanding pleased certain more recent writers, who expound, *ab omnibus* (by all), that is because of all. All the understandings have a right sense; and so if thou read, *super omnes inimicos meos,* the sense will be: *I am become a reproach to my neighbours, and a fear to my acquaintance,* more than any of my enemies, though wicked and unjust, have ever suffered these miseries; and so St. Augustine expoundeth it. If thou read, *apud omnes inimicos meos,* the sense will be: My enemies knowing it and laughing, I am become a reproach to my neighbours, and a fear to my acquaintance. And this exposition Theodoret followeth. If thou read, *ab omnibus inimicis meis,* the sense will be: The multitude and cruelty of my enemies hath brought it about, that I should be a reproach to my neighbours, and a fear to my acquaintance. That conjunction *et* (and), when it is said *et vicinis* (and to my neighbours), hath been rightly restored to the Vulgate edition; for it is present in the Hebrew and the Greek, and is read by the ancient Fathers.

[Margin: Verse 15.]

**15. THEY THAT SAW ME WITHOUT FLED FROM ME. I AM FORGOTTEN AS ONE DEAD FROM THE HEART.**

He declareth that which he had said, *and a fear to my acquaintance,* as if he should say: Many of my acquaintance, who saw me thus fleeing and afflicted, *fled without,* that is, withdrew far from me, fearing for their life, if they should be joined to me. And so *I am forgotten from the heart,* that is, their heart remembereth me not, nor thinketh of

<!-- p. 179 (PDF 190) -->

me, no otherwise than if I were dead and buried; for they think that it is already all over with me. In the Hebrew there is a pointing after the particle *foras* (without), in this manner, *they that saw me without,* fled from me; and therefore some will have *foris* to be read, not *foras* (for the Hebrew word signifieth both), and they will have the sense to be, *they that saw me without,* that is, outside the palace, barefoot, with head uncovered, without any royal ensign, *fled from me.* But although this understanding also is right, yet we are not compelled to admit the pointing of the Hebrew copy, which was added by the Rabbins, since in the Greek there is no pointing, and the Fathers constantly read *foras,* not *foris.*

[Margin: Verse 16.]

**16. I AM BECOME AS A VESSEL THAT IS DESTROYED. FOR I HAVE HEARD THE BLAME OF MANY THAT DWELL ROUND ABOUT.**

Not only, saith he, have my friends and acquaintance forgotten me, and forsaken me; but also among the neighbours, who dwell round about, I have been held in contempt as *a vessel that is destroyed,* that is broken, useless for all things, which is plain even from this, that I myself have heard them that blamed me. Now the Prophet seemeth in this place to make mention of the blame of Semei, who dared to curse him to his face, and to cast stones at him; Semei indeed thought that David, cast out of his kingdom, would thenceforth be of no power, but was to be thrown as a useless vessel into the sewer. This cursing is described in Book II of *Kings*, chap. xvi. And although in that place of II *Kings* mention is made of Semei alone cursing, yet it is credible that many others at the same time spoke evil of David, so that it is true, what is here said of *the blame of many.* In the Hebrew, for *commorantium* (of them that dwell), there is מגור *mogor*, which word St. Jerome rendereth, *congregationem* (a gathering), others render, *pavorem* (dread); but it can fitly also be rendered, *commorationem* (a dwelling); for the verb גור *gur*, whence *magor* is made, signifieth *commorari, incolere, peregrinari* (to dwell, to inhabit, to sojourn), etc.; and so the sense will be: *I have heard the blame of many, a dwelling round about,* that is, who have their dwelling round about; but the Seventy wished to render the meaning more clearly, therefore they put, *of many that dwell round about.*

[Margin: Verse 17.]

**17. WHILE THEY ASSEMBLED TOGETHER AGAINST ME, THEY CONSULTED TO TAKE AWAY MY LIFE.**

The history of II *Kings*, chap. xvii, relateth that, after the cursing of Semei, counsel was taken before Absalom concerning the killing of David, and this is what is called to mind in this verse,[^5] as if the Prophet should say: While in my presence I am cursed, and hear them that blame me, in my absence counsel is taken in Jerusalem about slaying me.

[Margin: Verse 18.]

**18. BUT I HAVE PUT MY TRUST IN THEE, O LORD: I SAID: THOU ART MY GOD. MY LOTS ARE IN THY HANDS.**

The holy man showeth that amid so many straits he did not lose heart, for this reason that he had not placed his hope in the deceitful help of men, but in almighty God, whom no man can resist. "But I have put my trust in thee, O Lord"; wherefore? because "I said" in my heart: "Thou art my God," I have a great protector, without whose will no man can take my life from me. For "my lots are in thy hands," that is, the lots of my life or of my death hang not upon chance, but upon thy hand, that is, upon thy power and will. In the Hebrew there is, בידך עתתי, *in manibus tuis tempora mea* (my times are in thy hands); and perhaps that which we now read in the Greek, κλῆροι, which signifieth *sortes* (lots), ought to be read, καιροί, which signifieth *tempora* (times). For there are not wanting ancient versions from the Greek, which have *tempora mea* (my times). So certainly it is read more than once in the office of the Mass, and in the Psalter of the church of St. Peter at Rome. But the sense is the same; for *my times are in thy hands* is nothing else than: upon thy will hang the times of my life and of my death; I shall not live, save as long as thou wilt, nor shall I die, save when thou wilt.

[Margin: Verse 19.]

**19. DELIVER ME OUT OF THE HANDS OF MY ENEMIES; AND FROM THEM THAT PERSECUTE ME.**

In this verse there is no difficulty, and all the editions agree.

[Margin: Verse 20.]

**20. MAKE THY FACE TO SHINE UPON THY SERVANT; SAVE ME IN THY MERCY. LET ME NOT BE CONFOUNDED, O LORD, FOR I HAVE CALLED UPON THEE.**

He presseth the same petition, but with arguments added that are fitted to move God. "Make thy face to shine upon thy servant," that is, show thy face, or look upon me: for all these signify the same. For as God is angry with us because of sins, and is said to turn away his face, or to set a cloud before it, and not to look upon us: so on the contrary when he is reconciled, he is said to turn his face to us, and to look upon us, and to make his countenance to shine upon us, that he may make us full of light. He asketh therefore first reconciliation with God, if haply he be set against him; and he addeth for an argument, that he is his servant, ready for his service and obedience. Then he addeth: "Save me"; for this followeth after reconciliation, that he save and deliver him, to whom he is reconciled, and he joineth an argument, saying: "In thy mercy," that is, not for my merits, but for thy mercy. Lastly he addeth a third argument: "Let me not be confounded, for I have called upon thee." For it belongeth to a good and faithful Lord, who hath promised help to them that trust in him, not to confound him, who with so great confidence ceaseth not to call upon him.

[Margin: Verse 21.]

**21. LET THE WICKED BE ASHAMED, AND BE BROUGHT DOWN TO HELL. LET DECEITFUL LIPS BE MADE DUMB.**

It is a prophetical imprecation, and was straightway fulfilled. For Achitophel, who was the chief counsellor of Absalom, and had given most pernicious counsel against David, God bringing his counsel

<!-- p. 180 (PDF 191) -->

to nought, was confounded, and not bearing his confusion, hanged himself with a halter, and so the wicked man was brought down to hell, *and the deceitful lips were made dumb.* The history is in Book II of *Kings*, chap. xvii. Now Achitophel is called *wicked,* because he stirred up a son to the slaying of his father; and his lips are rightly called deceitful, because he had for a long time feigned himself a friend and faithful to David, and afterwards, when occasion offered, betrayed his perfidy. In the Hebrew, for *deducantur* (let them be brought down), there is ידמו *iddemu*, which St. Jerome rendereth *taceant* (let them be silent); but the Seventy seem not to have read ידמו *iddemu*, but *iddechu*, which signifieth *expellantur* (let them be driven out), from the verb נדח *nadach*.

[Margin: Verse 22.]

**22. WHICH SPEAK INIQUITY AGAINST THE JUST, WITH PRIDE AND ABUSE.**

These words are joined in the Hebrew with those above in the same verse; in our edition they make a new verse, but as to the sense they depend upon those above: for they declare that those deceitful lips of Achitophel ought justly to have been made dumb, because *they spoke against the just,* that is, against David, who had done no wrong, neither to Achitophel himself, nor to Absalom; and they spoke *iniquity,* that is, a counsel full of iniquity, and this *with pride and abuse,* that is, with the greatest arrogance and contempt. For *abusione* (abuse), both in the Hebrew בוז *vabuz*, and in the Greek ἐξουδενώσει, there is a word signifying *contempt*; and so St. Augustine and the Psalter of St. Peter's read. Therefore abuse in the Latin edition is not to be taken for simple abuse, but for that which is born of contempt, or for contempt itself: for Achitophel is said, in this place, *to have spoken with pride and abuse,* because he proudly contemned David, and so abused his name.

[Margin: Verse 23.]

**23. O HOW GREAT IS THE MULTITUDE OF THY SWEETNESS, O LORD, WHICH THOU HAST HIDDEN FOR THEM THAT FEAR THEE!**

[Margin: Verse 24.]

**24. WHICH THOU HAST WROUGHT FOR THEM THAT HOPE IN THEE, IN THE SIGHT OF THE SONS OF MEN.**

Now the holy Prophet, perceiving himself to be heard, and having received divine consolation, crieth out for wonder: "O how great is the multitude," etc. In the Hebrew there is only מה רב טובך, *how much is the goodness,* or *sweetness!* but the Seventy wished to express the wonder of the Prophet, therefore they said, *how great is the multitude of sweetness,* etc. But because there seemeth to be a certain contradiction in these words, since he saith first, *which thou hast hidden,* and then, *which thou hast wrought* (*perfecisti*, thou hast perfected) *in the sight of the sons of men,* therefore St. Augustine joineth that, *in the sight of the sons of men,* not with the word *thou hast wrought,* but with the word, *that hope,* so that the sense is: God hath hidden much sweetness for them that fear him, which he will give them in the other life, now in the mean time suffering them to be pressed by straits; but that sweetness *he hath perfected for them that hope in him in the sight of the sons of men,* that is, for those who freely show to men that they are the servants of God, and hope in God, and fear not to confess that which

they are. Theodoret and Euthymius will have that, *in the sight of the sons of men,* to be joined with the word *thou hast wrought,* and they deliver this sense: God in the time of tribulation hideth from them that fear him great sweetness, that is, wages and rewards, that he may exercise them: but a little after he openly showeth the same wages and the same rewards in the sight of the sons of men, that he may bid his servants be of good hope. So for a time he hid sweetness from David, namely when he was fleeing from the persecution of his son; but a little after he perfected his goodness to him, when he brought him back into his kingdom with the greatest glory. The very same will befall all the just, from whom rewards are now hidden, but in the day of judgment they will openly appear. Another sense also can be brought: "O how great is the multitude of thy sweetness, O Lord, which thou hast hidden for them that fear thee," that is, truly manifold is thy consolation, O Lord, which thou pourest within into the secret of the heart of them that fear thee, that is, of them who worship and love thee, and reverence thee with filial fear. For this is "the hidden manna, which no man knoweth, but he that receiveth it," as we read in chap. ii of the *Apocalypse*. This he experienced who said, *Ps.* xciii: "According to the multitude of my sorrows, thy comforts have given joy to my soul"; and he, II *Cor.* vii: "I am filled with comfort: I exceedingly abound with joy in all our tribulation." And if in the time of tribulation so great is the multitude of sweetness in the heart of him that fighteth, how great will it be in the time of glory in the heart of him that triumpheth? "Which thou hast wrought for them that hope in thee, in the sight of the sons of men," that is, that sweetness thou hast perfected, thou hast poured it perfect, heaped up, most plentiful into the heart of them that hope in thee, who namely refuse to be comforted save in thee: and all this thou hast done "in the sight of the sons of men," that is, over against, or opposite to the sons of men, that is, the more they multiply sorrows without, the more thou multipliest consolations within. For the Hebrew word נגד *neghed*, and the Greek ἐναντίον, properly signifieth *over against,* or *opposite,* as we also noted on *Ps.* xxiii;[^6] therefore, that the Hebrew word may agree with the Latin and the Greek, that phrase, *in the sight,* is to be referred not so much to seeing, as to opposition: for those things are said to be in sight of each other, which, being set diametrically opposite, look upon each other. But this moreover is also true, that this sweetness is poured into the hearts of them that fear God, *in the sight of the sons of men,* that is, while the sons of this world, who vex the sons of God with their persecutions, behold and are amazed: for although the sons of men, that is, carnal men, know not that sweetness, nay cannot so much as suspect of what kind it is, since they have the palate of the soul too much tainted; yet they see its effect, while they behold, and are astonished at, the patience, meekness, cheerfulness and peace of the Saints. So therefore in a wonderful manner a certain hidden sweetness is given to the Saints in the sight of the sons of men,

<!-- p. 181 (PDF 192) -->

while that sweetness lieth hidden in itself, and lieth open in its effects.

[Margin: Verse 25.]

**25. THOU SHALT HIDE THEM IN THE SECRET OF THY FACE, FROM THE DISTURBANCE OF MEN.**

[Margin: Verse 26.]

**26. THOU SHALT PROTECT THEM IN THY TABERNACLE FROM THE CONTRADICTION OF TONGUES.**

He declareth the manner in which holy men receive the sweetness of God in tribulation: for this cometh to pass, when the Saints are carried away into God by love and contemplation, and in God himself find a house of refuge, according to that which is said in this same Psalm: "Be thou unto me a God, a protector, and a house of refuge"; for they who know how to lie hid in God, make nothing of all tribulations, as if these in no wise concerned them. "Thou shalt hide them," saith he, namely them that fear thee, "in the secret of thy face," that is, in the hidden place, which is in thy face: for he who is carried away to contemplate the face of God, and who most attentively considereth that he is seen by God, and who watcheth the beck of God, and who, considering his beauty, is kindled unto the love of him, already dwelleth in a hiding place most safe from the disturbance of men, that is, from every evil, by which men are wont to be disturbed. "Thou shalt protect them," that is, the same that fear thee, "in thy tabernacle," that is, in thyself, in whom thou also dwellest. For God hath no other house, or other tabernacle in which he can be contained, but himself; and they who by love and contemplation dwell in God, have made the most High their refuge, and there shall no evil come to them, nor shall the scourge come near their dwelling, as is said in the ninetieth Psalm. Now they are protected in this tabernacle from every evil, not only of deeds, of which it was spoken in the verse above, but also of words, which is here said, "from the contradiction of tongues," because they care nothing for the speeches of men, who have God for witness. But if the face of the Lord is so notable a hiding place or tabernacle to men that fear God, while it is seen through a glass and in a dark manner, what will it be, when he shall be seen as he is? then truly our dwelling will be in Jerusalem, which is the vision of peace, of which it is said, *Ps.* cxlvii: "Who hath placed peace in thy borders."

[Margin: God hath no other house than himself.]

[Margin: Verse 27.]

**27. BLESSED BE THE LORD, FOR HE HATH SHEWN HIS WONDERFUL MERCY TO ME IN A FORTIFIED CITY.**

What he had said in general of the divine consolation, which just men receive in tribulation, he applieth to himself, who is one of them; and thence he giveth thanks to God. "Blessed," saith he, "be the Lord, for he hath shewn his wonderful" (*mirificavit*, he hath made wonderful), that is, he hath shown marvellous "mercy" toward me, "in a fortified city," that is, hiding me in the secret of his face, which is as it were a most fortified city, so that now nothing can hurt me. St. Augustine readeth, *in civitate circumstantiæ* (in a city of compassing about), in which I know not what Latin copies he followed. For the Hebrew word מצור *matsor* signifieth *a fortification*, the Greek word περιοχὴ denoteth *a compass*; and thence St. Augustine took *circumstantiam*: but Theodoret and Euthymius, Greek authors, understand by *a compass* the walls, or ramparts, with which cities are fortified, so that the sense is: *in a city of compass,* that is, in a city fortified with walls or ramparts. And St. Jerome not only in his version from the Hebrew, but also in the Commentary, where he expoundeth the version of the Seventy, readeth, *in civitate munita* (in a fortified city).

[Margin: Verse 28.]

**28. BUT I SAID IN THE EXCESS OF MY MIND: I AM CAST AWAY FROM BEFORE THY EYES.**

He accuseth himself of the distrust, which he had suffered at the beginning of the persecution. "But I said in the excess of my mind," that is, at that time, when through exceeding terror I had become as one bereft of mind, I said within myself: "I am cast away from before thy eyes," that is, it pleaseth thee not that I should reign any longer, or that I should live longer, therefore thou hast cast me off from thy kindly regard and favour, and hast left me destitute of thy wonted help. There is a trace of this doubting in Book II of *Kings*, chap. xv: "If I shall find grace," saith he, "in his sight, he will bring me again; if he shall say to me: Thou pleasest me not: I am ready, let him do to me that which is good before him."[^7] For the phrase, *in excessu mentis* (in the excess of mind), there is in the Hebrew בחפצי,[^8] which word for word signifieth, *in my hasting,* that is, *while I was hasting,* or *in my being amazed and afraid,* that is, while I was amazed and afraid: for the verb חפץ signifieth *to hasten,* and *to be amazed,* and *to be afraid,* and all are true; for David said that, when he was hasting to go out of Jerusalem, and was seized with dread and amazement. This amazement the Seventy rendered *exstasim* (an ecstasy), and the Latins *excessum mentis* (an excess of mind).

[Margin: Verse 29.]

**29. THEREFORE THOU HAST HEARD THE VOICE OF MY PRAYER, WHEN I CRIED TO THEE.**

The meaning is plain: therefore, saith he, lest I should distrust thy goodness, when thou sawest me in so great dismay, straightway "thou hast heard the voice of my prayer, when I cried to thee." That word, *ideo* (therefore), could also be rendered *vere* (truly), or *certe* (certainly): for it is an adverb signifying many things.

[Margin: Verse 30.]

**30. O LOVE THE LORD, ALL YE HIS SAINTS: FOR THE LORD WILL REQUIRE TRUTH, AND WILL REPAY THEM ABUNDANTLY THAT ACT PROUDLY.**

[Margin: Verse 31.]

**31. DO YE MANFULLY, AND LET YOUR HEART BE STRENGTHENED, ALL YE THAT HOPE IN THE LORD.**

He exhorteth at the end of the Psalm godly men, who suffer the like, not to cease to love the Lord, and to hope in him, because although for a time the wicked seem to rage unpunished, yet shortly they shall pay most bitter penalties. That, *veritatem requiret Dominus* (the Lord will require truth), St. Jerome rendereth, *fideles servat Dominus* (the Lord keepeth the faithful). The Hebrew word signifieth both, *truth,* and also *the faithful,* and the sense cometh to the same. That word, *abundanter* (abundantly), is ambiguous in the Hebrew and the Latin, whether it ought to be joined with the word *retribuet* (will repay), or with the word *facientibus* (them that act); but from the Greek it appeareth that it is to be joined with the word *facientibus*; and in the same

manner St. Jerome rendereth from the Hebrew: *Retribuet,* saith he, *his qui satis operantur superbiam* (He will repay them that abundantly work pride); and St. Augustine, *retribuet his qui multum superbiunt* (he will repay them that are very proud): in the same manner Theodoret, and other ancients.
