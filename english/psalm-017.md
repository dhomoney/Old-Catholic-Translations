BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XVII.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*Unto the end, for David the servant of the Lord, who spoke to the Lord the words of this canticle, in the day that the Lord delivered him from the hand of all his enemies, and from the hand of Saul, and he said.*[^1]

This title showeth that this Psalm was composed by David in thanksgiving, when he was delivered from all his enemies, and chiefly from Saul, who for that cause is expressed by name. This same Psalm is found in Book II of *Kings*, chap. xxii, with a few things changed. But it is uncertain whether that change was made by David himself, or by Esdras, who gathered the Psalms together. And because David bore the figure of Christ, this Psalm can rightly be expounded of Christ also.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. I WILL LOVE THEE, O LORD, MY STRENGTH: THE LORD IS MY FIRMAMENT, MY REFUGE, AND MY DELIVERER.**

I will love thee, O Lord, that is, with good reason I ought to love thee, since thou hast bound me to thee by so many benefits. He then setteth forth the benefits received from God: and first in a single word, then by several names, lastly by a recital of the things done. He setteth them forth in a single word, when he saith: "My strength," that is, the cause

<!-- p. 93 (PDF 104) -->

of all my might, by which I have triumphed over all enemies. And that which he said in one word: "My strength," he explaineth by parts, saying: *Firmament, refuge,* and *deliverer*, as if he said: With good reason did I say that God is my might, because he is my firmament, my refuge, and my deliverer. When I am in safety, he is a firmament to me; when I am in danger, he affordeth me a refuge; when perchance I fall into the hands of enemies, he delivereth me. And so in all ways he is to me strength and might. In the Hebrew, for the word *diligam* (I will love), there is not the word אהב *ahab*, which signifieth simply *to love*; but רחם *racham*, which signifieth *to love from the inmost bowels*. For the word *firmament* there is סלעי *salhhi*, *a rock*, that is, a stable and solid foundation. For *refuge* there is מצודתי that is, *a stronghold*, or a fortification, or a most strongly fortified stronghold, to which one may safely flee. But the Seventy Interpreters wished to expound the metaphors, and therefore they said not rock and stronghold, but firmament and refuge. In Book II of *Kings*, chap. xxii, that beginning is wanting: "I will love thee, O Lord, my strength." For the Psalm beginneth: "The Lord is my rock," etc. But that most beautiful beginning seemeth to have been added by none other than David himself.

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. MY GOD IS MY HELPER, AND IN HIM WILL I PUT MY TRUST.**

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. MY PROTECTOR AND THE HORN OF MY SALVATION, AND MY SUPPORT.**

In these two verses, which in the Hebrew are joined with the first verse, the Prophet, from the vehemence of his affection toward God, repeateth and presseth home the titles of his benefactor. Now these three: *Helper, protector,* and *horn of salvation*, answer to those three: *Firmament, refuge,* and *deliverer*. In the Hebrew, for the word *helper*, there is צורי *tsuri*, *a rock*, as for the word *firmament* there was also *a rock*, but another, and by another word. And so he calleth God his helper, because he affordeth a firm footing, that he fall not, according to that of *Ps.* xxxix: "He set my feet upon a rock." And with good reason he addeth: "In him will I put my trust," as in a most firm foundation. For the word *protector* there is in the Hebrew מגני *maghinni*, *a shield*, or *buckler*; so for the word *refuge* there was *a stronghold*: for both protect and defend. And so the Seventy Interpreters wished to explain the metaphors of the Hebrew names. *Horn of salvation* is as well in the Hebrew as in the Greek, and signifieth might to save. The metaphor taken from horned beasts is familiar to divine Scripture. *Ps.* cxxxi: "I will bring forth a horn to David,"[^2] that is, I will make David to be mighty and powerful, after the manner of a bull armed with horns, not feeble and weak, in the manner of a calf not yet putting forth horns. *Ezech.* xxix, 21: "In that day a horn shall bud forth to the house of Israel." *Mich.* iv, 13: "I will make thy horn iron." *Luke* i, 69: "He hath raised up an horn of salvation to us, in the house of David." God therefore is called a horn of salvation to David, and to all the just, because the just are most mighty against all enemies; but their might consisteth not in their own strength, but in the help and favour of God: "I can do all these things," said the Apostle, "in him who strengtheneth me."[^3] Now *the horn of my salvation* answereth to that clause, *and my deliverer*. For God delivereth by the horn of salvation, that is, by his saving power. Lastly that, *and my support* (*susceptor*), is a name embracing all the others, and answereth to that first name: *My strength*. For those whom God taketh up (*suscipit*), he delivereth, protecteth, strengtheneth.

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. PRAISING I WILL CALL UPON THE LORD: AND I SHALL BE SAVED FROM MY ENEMIES.**

This is as it were a certain conclusion of the words above, as if he said: I henceforth will continually praise God for so many benefits received, and with great confidence in my tribulations I will call upon the same God, and without doubt I shall be delivered from all enemies whatsoever. In the Hebrew there is מהלל *mehullul*, which St. Jerome rightly rendereth: *Laudatum invocabo Dominum* (I will call upon the Lord, who is praised). But if the points be changed, the same letters make *mehallel*, which signifieth *laudans invocabo* (praising I will call upon), as the Seventy rendered. The sense is always the same, namely, I will praise, and I will call upon, I will give thanks for things past,[^4] and I will call upon him for things to come.

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. THE SORROWS OF DEATH SURROUNDED ME: AND THE TORRENTS OF INIQUITY TROUBLED ME.**

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. THE SORROWS OF HELL ENCOMPASSED ME: AND THE SNARES OF DEATH PREVENTED ME.**

The Prophet now beginneth to recount the benefits received. And first he saith that he was once in most manifest danger of life, namely when Saul sought him, to kill him. But this danger of life he describeth poetically, employing various metaphors: "The sorrows of death surrounded me," that is, such dangers pressed upon me on every side, that I despaired of bodily safety; and therefore I was affected with such sorrow, that is, grief of mind, as they are wont to be affected with, who see death hanging over them. "And the torrents of iniquity troubled me," that is, the danger and the sorrow of which I spoke arose from this, that a multitude of the men of iniquity, like a torrent, had rushed upon me and troubled me, as they are wont to be troubled who are carried down and whirled about by a torrent of waters. "The sorrows of hell encompassed me"; he repeateth what he had said, "the sorrows of death surrounded me." For in this place the sorrow of death and the sorrow of hell are the same. For because before the passion of Christ all who died descended into hell, although not into the same part of hell, therefore to die and to descend into hell were taken for the same thing. "The sorrows of hell," saith he, "encompassed me," that is, grief of mind, such as they suffer, over whom there hangeth the departure out of this world to the place of the dead, which is hell. "The snares of death prevented me"; he repeateth what he had said: "The torrents

<!-- p. 94 (PDF 105) -->

of iniquity troubled me." For as the trouble from the multitude of the men of iniquity rising up against him was to David the cause of the sorrow of death: so the snares of death, laying hold on him beforehand, were to the same David the cause of the sorrow of hell. He calleth snares of death the ambushes of the men of iniquity, by which he was sought unto death. And so the sense of these two verses is, that David was seized with a mighty sorrow on account of the imminent danger of death, both from the open onset of his enemies, like a torrent violently snatching and rolling a man away, and from the ambushes of the same enemies, like snares taking the unwary. As regards the words, for *sorrows* there is in the Hebrew חבלי *cheble*, which word can signify *sorrows* and *cords*. Therefore St. Jerome rendereth: *Funes mortis* (the cords of death), and *funes inferni* (the cords of hell). But the Seventy Interpreters rendered: *Dolores mortis* (the sorrows of death), and *dolores inferni* (the sorrows of hell): which latter reading is the clearer. For *torrents of iniquity* there is in the Hebrew נחלי בליעל *nachale beliahhal*, which St. Jerome rendereth *torrentes Diaboli* (the torrents of the Devil), and the Seventy Interpreters *torrentes iniquitatis* (the torrents of iniquity). Both are right; for *Beliahhal* to the letter soundeth *without yoke*, and in the holy Scriptures this word is found in many places used to signify men of iniquity, who have shaken off the yoke of the law; or iniquity itself, which is contrary to the law: and because the head of the men of iniquity is the Devil, therefore the Hebrews are wont to signify the Devil also by this word.

[Margin: Before the passion of Christ all the dead descended into hell.]

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. IN MY AFFLICTION I CALLED UPON THE LORD, AND I CRIED TO MY GOD.**

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. AND HE HEARD MY VOICE FROM HIS HOLY TEMPLE: AND MY CRY BEFORE HIM CAME INTO HIS EARS.**

He had said that he was placed in the greatest danger; now he saith that he fled by prayer to God, and was heard. "In my affliction," that is, in those great straits, into which the persecution of Saul had cast me, and in many others like them. "I called upon the Lord," in whom my whole confidence is wont to be placed. "And I cried to my God." The repetition is familiar to David. "And he heard my voice from his holy temple," that is, my prayer reached even to the highest heaven, which is the temple of God not made with hands, and truly holy, which can in no way be violated and polluted. "And my cry before him came into his ears." This is a repetition, and in some manner a declaration of the verse above, as if he said: My crying prayer, that is, uttered with great affection, and poured out before him, that is, which I poured out having God before my eyes, "came into his ears," that is, was heard. In the Hebrew all the verbs are in the future, *invocabo, clamabo, exaudiet, introibit* (I will call upon, I will cry, he will hear, it will come in). But the Seventy Interpreters rightly rendered in the past: *invocavi, clamavi, exaudivit, introivit* (I called upon, I cried, he heard, it came in), since the law of Hebrew grammar permitteth the future to be put for the past.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. THE EARTH SHOOK AND TREMBLED: THE FOUNDATIONS OF THE MOUNTAINS WERE TROUBLED AND WERE MOVED, BECAUSE HE WAS ANGRY WITH THEM.**

He now beginneth to describe the effect of God's hearing, which was help from heaven against his enemies, by whose destruction he at length not only escaped dangers, but also obtained the kingdom. Now he describeth the anger of God against his enemies in the poetic manner: for as when some king is angry, and prepareth war, his whole kingdom is troubled; so when the King of the whole world is angry, the whole world is troubled, and chiefly the three visible elements, earth, air and water. David doth not signify in this place that the earth, and the air, and the water were in very deed so disturbed, as the words seem to sound; but he signifieth that the anger of God is such, that it can forthwith make the earth tremble even to its lowest foundations; and in the air forthwith raise winds and dark clouds, and thunders, and lightnings; lastly so dry up fountains, and rivers, and seas, that the caverns of the earth, and the sources of the fountains appear. He beginneth from the element of earth, and saith: "The earth shook, it trembled, the foundations of the mountains were moved, because he was angry with them," that is, when God is angry with the earth, forthwith the whole is moved, and trembleth, not only on the surface, but even to the lowest foundations of the mountains. Nor only when God is angry, but also when he manifesteth his presence on the earth, there followeth a shaking of the earth, as of one trembling and revering the majesty of the Creator. So at the resurrection of Christ there was a great earthquake; and when the same Christ, dying, was about to descend beneath the earth, the earth was moved. And David in another Psalm: "At the presence of the Lord the earth was moved," *Ps.* cxiii. In the Hebrew there is not *quoniam iratus est eis* (because he was angry with them), but *quoniam ira ei* (because anger was his), that is, because he was angry. And so according to the Hebrew text the earth and the mountains tremble, not because God is angry with the mountains themselves, but because he is simply angry. But the Seventy, rendering *quoniam iratus est eis* (because he was angry with them), wished to signify that the anger of God is against the earth and the mountains of the earth by reason of the men who dwell on the earth; otherwise there is no reason why God should be angry with the earth and the mountains.

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. THERE WENT UP A SMOKE IN HIS WRATH: AND A FIRE FLAMED FROM HIS FACE: COALS WERE KINDLED BY IT.**

He still goeth on to explain the working of God on the earth, when God willeth to show his presence on it; for he not only maketh the earth to tremble, but also to burn and to smoke, which is described to have happened, *Exod.* xx, *Heb.* xii, when God gave the law on mount Sina. "There went up," saith he, "a smoke in his wrath," that is, the wrath of God raised on the earth a vehement heat, whence a mighty smoke went up. "And a fire flamed from his face"; nor did it raise heat and smoke only, but also a great burning. "Coals were kindled by it," that is, the wrath of God, or the face of God looking upon the earth,

<!-- p. 95 (PDF 106) -->

so made it burn, as if he had turned the whole into burning coals. Elsewhere also he saith: "He looketh upon the earth, and maketh it tremble: he toucheth the mountains, and they smoke," *Ps.* ciii, 32.

[Margin: Verse 11.]

**11. HE BOWED THE HEAVENS, AND CAME DOWN: AND DARKNESS WAS UNDER HIS FEET.**

He passeth from the earth to the air: and describeth the things which are done in the air, when God willeth to show his anger, or his presence. God is said to bow the heavens, when he letteth down a cloud, in which he himself appeareth: for the cloud seemeth as it were a part of heaven; and ordinarily God hath been wont to show himself by a cloud, as is plain from the book of *Num.*, chap. ix; III *Kings* viii; *Matt.* xvii, and elsewhere. He saith therefore: "He bowed the heavens, and came down," that is, a cloud being let down, he manifested himself by it; "and darkness was under his feet," that is, God so dwelt in the cloud, as though he had darkness under his feet: all these are metaphorical, and from them we understand that God maketh himself invisibly present to us.

[Margin: Verse 12.]

**12. AND HE ASCENDED UPON THE CHERUBIM, AND HE FLEW; HE FLEW UPON THE WINGS OF THE WINDS.**

He goeth on to describe the working of God in the air, when he willeth to manifest his anger to men. Now he setteth God before our eyes as an armed man, seated on a chariot running most swiftly to and fro, and from it sending forth arrows against his enemies. The chariots of God are the clouds, according to that of *Ps.* ciii, 3: "Who makest the clouds thy chariot: who walkest upon the wings of the winds." The most swift horses are the winds, which bear the clouds hither and thither. The charioteers are the angels, whom he calleth Cherubim. The arrows are the lightnings and thunderbolts, which are hurled from the clouds. And truly wonderful is this description. For no chariots are more nimble than the clouds, no horses swifter than the winds, no charioteers more skilful than the angels, no darts which can be compared with the thunderbolts of heaven. And moreover these chariots fight from that higher place, so that they can hurt and cannot be hurt: "He ascended," saith he, "upon the Cherubim," that is, God not only useth the cloud as a house, or a tent, but he also useth the clouds as chariots; and the angels, the Cherubim, as charioteers, and the winds as horses. Now he is said to ascend upon the Cherubim, that we may understand that God is not ruled by the charioteer, but that he himself presideth over the charioteer, and is the principal mover and ruler both of the chariot itself and of the charioteer. And so God is said to ascend upon the Cherubim, and to fly upon the wings of the winds, because he useth the ministry of the angels in moving the clouds, which are as it were chariots of the air and most swift, seeing that they are drawn by the winds, as by winged horses, and thereby do not walk, but fly, and fly more swiftly than any bird.

[Margin: A wonderful description of God.]

[Margin: Verse 13.]

**13. AND HE MADE DARKNESS HIS COVERT, HIS PAVILION ROUND ABOUT HIM: DARK WATERS IN THE CLOUDS OF THE AIR.**

Lest God should be thought to stand forth visibly in the clouds, as in a chariot, the Prophet declareth that God is present invisibly, and for that cause useth dark clouds, that by that as by a symbol he may show himself to be invisible. There is also in these words a most elegant and poetic metaphor: "He made," saith he, "darkness his covert," that is, God lieth hid in the dark clouds, as in a secret place, or he brought it about that the dark clouds should serve him for a covert. "His pavilion round about him," that is, the same clouds are as it were a pavilion to God, by which he is covered on every side. "Dark waters in the clouds of the air," that is, the pavilion of which I spoke is nothing else but a dark cloud, such as it is when it is disposed to be presently turned into water. For then the cloud is very dense and watery, so that it can not improperly be called water existing in a cloud.

[Margin: Verse 14.]

**14. AT THE BRIGHTNESS THAT WAS BEFORE HIM THE CLOUDS PASSED, HAIL AND COALS OF FIRE.**

He declareth how God fighteth from the clouds, as from his armed chariots, and saith that, God commanding, the cloud is rent, and thence come forth hail and lightnings, like burning coals. And most elegantly he saith: "At the brightness that was before him the clouds passed," as though the rending of the clouds proceeded from the brightness of God lying hid within, as though the cloud could not bear the divine brightness, and therefore in the sight of God is burst and dissolved, and on that side vanisheth and passeth away. "At the brightness that was before him the clouds passed," that is, the clouds, shaken by the brightness of God present, in the sight of the same God passed away burst and dissolved. "Hail and coals of fire," supply, from that rending there came forth hails and lightnings. In very deed this happened in the time of Pharao, as we have in the book of *Exodus*, chap. ix, 24: "And the hail," it saith, "and fire mixed with it drove on together." The like happened in the war of Josue against the five kings, *Jos.* chap. x; and also in the war of the emperor M. Antoninus against the Germans, of which see Tertullian in the book *To Scapula*, and in the *Apology*, chap. v.

[Margin: Verse 15.]

**15. AND THE LORD THUNDERED FROM HEAVEN, AND THE HIGHEST GAVE HIS VOICE: HAIL AND COALS OF FIRE.**

He repeateth the same in other words: from the dissolving of the cloud there ariseth a mighty crash, which is called thunder, with which thunderbolts are often wont to be joined. That crash the Prophet elegantly calleth the voice of God, not only because God is the cause of that crash, but also because that sound is so mighty and so terrible, that it seemeth to be attributed to God alone, as his own proper voice. Hence is that of God himself to Job: "Hast thou an arm like God, and canst thou thunder with a voice like him?" *Job* xl, 4. But that, *hail and coals of fire*, is not found in some Greek texts; in the Hebrew it is found, and it might be read *grandinem et carbones ignis* (hail and coals of fire, in the accusative case), as some read; but our Interpreter rightly rendered *grando et carbones ignis*, because the words are altogether the same as in the verse above.

<!-- p. 96 (PDF 107) -->

And so here also something must be understood, that the sense may be: "The highest gave his voice," from which voice there came forth also hail and thunderbolts, as it were burning coals.

[Margin: Verse 16.]

**16. AND HE SENT FORTH HIS ARROWS, AND HE SCATTERED THEM: HE MULTIPLIED LIGHTNINGS, AND TROUBLED THEM.**

This is an explanation of what is above, and especially of that word *coals of fire*, as if he said: Coals of fire came forth from the rending of the clouds, because God *sent forth his arrows*, that is, lightnings, and "multiplied" them, and in that manner "scattered and troubled" his enemies.

[Margin: Verse 17.]

**17. THEN THE FOUNTAINS OF WATERS APPEARED, AND THE FOUNDATIONS OF THE WORLD WERE DISCOVERED.**

Here he now speaketh of the working of God in the waters, which working is a wonderful and sudden drying up of the waters; such as was done in the Red Sea and the river Jordan, as we have in *Exod.* xiv, and *Jos.* iv, by which drying up the deep of the sea and of the rivers lay open, which deep the Prophet calleth *the foundation of the world*, because it is the lowest part of the whole earth. For the word *fountains* there is in the Hebrew אפיכי *aphike*, which signifieth *courses*, or *torrents*. St. Jerome rendereth *effusiones aquarum* (the outpourings of waters); but since it is a general name, the Seventy Interpreters could rightly render *fontes* (fountains). The sense therefore is: "Then the fountains of waters appeared," that is, the waters being dried at the command of God, there appeared the bottom of the fountains, or of the rivers, or even of the sea, and so "were discovered," and uncovered, "the foundations," that is, the lowest parts "of the world."

[Margin: Verse 18.]

**18. AT THY REBUKE, O LORD, AT THE BLAST OF THE SPIRIT OF THY WRATH.**

He explaineth the cause of the drying up of the waters. The fountains, saith he, or courses of waters, appeared "at thy rebuke, O Lord," that is, because thou, O Lord, didst rebuke the waters, and they forthwith vanished. And how he rebuked them he explaineth, saying: "At the blast of the spirit of thy wrath." By a wonderful and plainly poetic metaphor the Prophet calleth the wind, by whose ministry the waters were dried, the spirit of the wrath of God, and the rebuke of God. For that the waters of the Red Sea were dried by God with a burning wind, Scripture testifieth, *Exod.* xiv. And so, what could properly be said: Thou, O Lord, sending a vehement wind, didst dry the waters of the sea, he saith by a most elegant metaphor: Thou, O Lord, didst rebuke the waters, because they hindered the passage of thy people, and with the spirit of thy wrath thou didst blow upon them, and they forthwith fled, as he saith elsewhere, *Ps.* cxiii, 5: "What ailed thee, O thou sea, that thou didst flee?" The Hebrew copies join this verse with the one above, because the sentence of the former hangeth on this latter; but the Seventy Interpreters rightly separated them, because they saw from the reckoning of the metre that it was so to be done; for neither is it absurd that one sentence should be contained in two verses.

[Margin: Verse 19.]

**19. HE SENT FROM ON HIGH, AND TOOK ME: AND RECEIVED ME OUT OF MANY WATERS.**

Now he returneth to recount the benefit of God, that is, to his deliverance from his enemies. For all that hath been said from the ninth verse unto this nineteenth pertaineth to the declaration of the power of God. And because he had said in the beginning: "The torrents of iniquity troubled me," and in the preceding verse had said that God is wont to dry the waters with his spirit, and to make the bottom of rivers and of the sea appear, therefore, continuing in the same metaphor, he saith: "He sent from on high, and took me," that is, he sent his hand from a high place even into the deep of the torrent, and thence took me, and so brought me out of many waters, that is, he wonderfully delivered me when oppressed with tribulations. In the Hebrew the verbs are of the future tense, *mittet de summo, et accipiet me* (he shall send from on high, and shall take me), ישלח יקחני *ishlach, ikkacheni*; but, as we have often said, the Seventy Interpreters were not ignorant that the tenses are confounded according to the rules of the Hebrew tongue, and that one is often put for another. Here certainly the past tense fitteth better.

[Margin: Verse 20.]

**20. HE DELIVERED ME FROM MY STRONGEST ENEMIES, AND FROM THEM THAT HATED ME: FOR THEY WERE TOO STRONG FOR ME.**

What he had said metaphorically in the verse above, he saith in proper words in this verse. That, *for they were too strong for me* (*quoniam confortati sunt super me*), ought to be expounded by the imperfect tense, that is, because they were stronger than I, and so St. Jerome rendereth; for the reason is given why the more powerful help of God was necessary, namely because the enemies of David were stronger than David himself. In the Hebrew and the Greek copy there is the future, *eripiet me* (he shall deliver me), יצילני *itsileni*: but our reading is found also in ancient books, and SS. Jerome and Augustine expound it. Wherefore it is likely that in the Greek ῥύσεταί με was formerly in the past. And the Hebrew can rightly be taken for the past.

[Margin: Verse 21.]

**21. THEY PREVENTED ME IN THE DAY OF MY AFFLICTION: AND THE LORD BECAME MY PROTECTOR.**

He presseth home in other words the benefit of God. "They prevented me (*prævenerunt*)[^5] in the day of my affliction," that is, provoked by no injury from me, they were themselves the first to do harm, or, before I could take heed, they set upon me, and came upon me unawares: but the Lord watched for me, and brought it about that their diligence could do me no harm.

[Margin: Verse 22.]

**22. AND HE BROUGHT ME FORTH INTO A LARGE PLACE: HE SAVED ME, BECAUSE HE WAS WELL PLEASED WITH ME.**

Again and again he repeateth his deliverance, to show that the benefit of God is fixed deep in his memory. "He brought me forth into a large place," that is, being delivered from the straits of tribulations he brought me forth as into a wide field, that I may now freely walk abroad

<!-- p. 97 (PDF 108) -->

without any fear of them that lie in wait. "He saved me, because he was well pleased with me (*quoniam voluit me*)," that is, he saved me from so many imminent dangers, chiefly for this, because it pleased him, that is, on account of his boundless mercy, because he loved me. For although a little after he will allege his merits, yet our merits themselves are gifts of God; of which the holy Prophet was not ignorant.

[Margin: Our merits are gifts of God.]

[Margin: Verse 23.]

**23. AND THE LORD WILL REWARD ME ACCORDING TO MY JUSTICE; AND WILL REPAY ME ACCORDING TO THE CLEANNESS OF MY HANDS.**

[Margin: Verse 24.]

**24. BECAUSE I HAVE KEPT THE WAYS OF THE LORD; AND HAVE NOT DONE WICKEDLY AGAINST MY GOD.**

[Margin: Verse 25.]

**25. FOR ALL HIS JUDGMENTS ARE IN MY SIGHT: AND HIS JUSTICES I HAVE NOT PUT AWAY FROM ME.**

[Margin: Verse 26.]

**26. AND I SHALL BE SPOTLESS WITH HIM: AND SHALL KEEP MYSELF FROM MY INIQUITY.**

[Margin: Verse 27.]

**27. AND THE LORD WILL REWARD ME ACCORDING TO MY JUSTICE; AND ACCORDING TO THE CLEANNESS OF MY HANDS BEFORE HIS EYES.**

He hath praised God for the benefit of deliverance from his enemies. Now he addeth that for the time to come also he shall be perpetually delivered, and that this shall be not from mercy alone, but also from justice, because he hath lived justly, and hath it in mind to live so henceforth. For God is just, and loveth the just, and helpeth, and protecteth them. "And the Lord," saith he, "will reward me according to my justice," that is, he will render me, as he hath done hitherto, many good things, according as my justice deserveth. "And will repay me according to the cleanness of my hands," that is, as I have justice not only in the heart, but also in the hands, that is, I am just inwardly and outwardly, I have a just heart, and just works: so God will render me good things before himself and before men, he will keep me inwardly and outwardly. But it seemeth presumption to proclaim one's own justice. It is not presumption, when it is done sincerely, and God is acknowledged the author of our good things. So also Nehemias declared his merits before God, II *Esdr.* v, Ezechias, *Isa.* xxxviii, Esther, chap. xiv. But that which David saith in this place seemeth false. For when he said these things, he had committed adultery and murder. And moreover he himself elsewhere, *Ps.* xviii, said: "Who can understand sins? from my secret ones cleanse me, O Lord." And elsewhere, *Ps.* cxlviii:[^6] "In thy sight no man living shall be justified." Some, on account of this objection, think that David speaketh not of his justice absolutely, but of the justice of the cause which he had with his adversaries. Others think that David speaketh only of the justice of religion, that is, that he always remained in the true religion, and never gave worship to idols. But those words are against these opinions: "Because I have kept the ways of the Lord"; also: "All his judgments are in my sight"; also: "I shall be spotless with him," etc. It must be said therefore that David calleth to mind his justice, which lay in this, that he worshipped God with a sincere heart, and had a steadfast purpose of never transgressing the law of the Lord, and if perchance he did otherwise, forthwith came to himself again, and returned to God from the heart. Now that, *who can understand sins?* can be understood of venial sins, which are not opposed to justice. But that, *in thy sight no man living shall be justified*, is understood of a justice which is believed to be had without the grace of God going before. For so no one shall be justified before God, because whosoever is just hath been justified by the grace of God.

[Margin: It is lawful to declare one's merits before God.]

[Margin: Whosoever is just hath been justified by the grace of God.]

[Margin: Verse 28.]

**28. WITH THE HOLY, THOU WILT BE HOLY; AND WITH THE INNOCENT MAN THOU WILT BE INNOCENT.**

[Margin: Verse 29.]

**29. AND WITH THE ELECT THOU WILT BE ELECT: AND WITH THE PERVERSE THOU WILT BE PERVERTED.**

He giveth the reason why he had said that he was to be rewarded by God according to his justice, namely because God rendereth to every one according to his works. Now he addresseth God himself, and saith: "With the holy thou wilt be holy," that is, with the merciful and kind man thou wilt deal kindly and mercifully, or thou wilt show thyself kind and merciful. For the Hebrew word חסיד *chasid*, for which we have *sanctum* (holy), properly signifieth *merciful*. And for *sanctus eris* (thou wilt be holy) there is in the Hebrew a single verb of the conjugation *hitpael*, תתחסד, which properly signifieth *to make*, or *show*, *oneself merciful*. And so the sense is not: if we shall be holy, God also will be holy; but: if we shall be holy, God will deal with us holily, that is, mercifully. In the same manner the rest are to be understood: "With the innocent thou wilt be innocent," that is, to him who hurteth no one God will show himself innocent, that is, he will not hurt him, nor suffer him to be hurt by others. "With the elect thou wilt be elect," that is, with the sincere and pure man, for that the Hebrew word נבר *nabar* properly signifieth, thou wilt show thyself pure, that is, thou wilt deal sincerely and candidly. "And with the perverse thou wilt be perverted," that is, to him who is not merciful thou wilt not show mercy; him who hurteth thou wilt hurt; him who dealeth not sincerely, but craftily supplanteth, thou wilt craftily, that is, wisely supplant. Now it is here to be observed that, where it was said: "With the holy thou wilt be holy, with the innocent thou wilt be innocent, with the elect thou wilt be elect," the same word was attributed to God; but where it was said, with the perverse thou wilt be perverted, in the Hebrew there is not the same word. Of man it was said עקש *hhikesh*, which properly signifieth *perverse*. But of God, תתפתל *thithpattel*, which signifieth *to wrestle*, and *to strive against*. And so the sense is: if a man be perverse, God will not be perverse in himself, or deal perversely; but he will wrestle against the perverse man with mighty effort, that he may utterly overthrow him, and make him fall from all his hope.

[Margin: Verse 30.]

**30. FOR THOU WILT SAVE THE HUMBLE PEOPLE; BUT WILT BRING DOWN THE EYES OF THE PROUD.**

The Prophet explaineth the two verses above,

<!-- p. 98 (PDF 109) -->

as if he said: With the holy thou wilt be holy, and with the innocent innocent, and with the elect elect, "for thou wilt save the humble people," that is, because humility, which is the guardian of all virtues, is most pleasing to thee, and to all the humble thou givest grace; but with the perverse thou wilt be perverted, because thou wilt bring down the eyes of the proud, that is, because pride, which is the queen of vices, displeaseth thee exceedingly: and therefore thou resistest all the proud, and castest them down and humblest them. Now he nameth the eyes, beyond the other members, because arrogance is seen in the eyes, and chiefly in the brows.

[Margin: Humility the guardian of all virtues.]

[Margin: Verse 31.]

**31. FOR THOU LIGHTEST MY LAMP, O LORD: O MY GOD ENLIGHTEN MY DARKNESS.**

Because the prophet David seemed to have spoken loftily enough of his own justice and cleanness, he showeth whence he had justice and the other gifts: and from this he most of all praiseth God, that from him is light, and strength, and all good things. "For thou," saith he, "lightest my lamp, O Lord," that is, from thee I have the beginning of all good, which is light to discern true goods from false, and true evils from false. For the first wound inflicted on nature from original sin is ignorance of the true good: and therefore the first healing beginneth from the divine light. "Thou," saith he, "lightest my lamp," that is, thou alone enlightenest the inward eye of my heart. "O my God, enlighten my darkness," that is, thou, Father of lights, thou, the true light, in whom there is no darkness, as thou hast hitherto begun to enlighten the inward eyes of my heart: so go on to enlighten my darkness, that is, to drive out all my darkness. For without the enlightening grace of God, as regards supernatural mysteries, there is mere darkness in our heart. For "we are not sufficient to think any thing of ourselves, as of ourselves." In the Greek there is φωτίσεις τὸ σκότος μου, *illuminabis tenebras meas* (thou wilt enlighten my darkness); in the Hebrew, *illuminabit tenebras meas* (he will enlighten my darkness). Our reading, *illumina tenebras meas* (enlighten my darkness), is found in the ancient books of SS. Jerome and Augustine; but the sense is always the same.

[Margin: Ignorance of the true good a wound inflicted by original sin.]

[Margin: Verse 32.]

**32. FOR BY THEE I SHALL BE DELIVERED FROM TEMPTATION; AND THROUGH MY GOD I SHALL GO OVER A WALL.**

The particle *quoniam* (for) is often redundant in Hebrew speech, as also the particle *et* (and), which is often put at the beginning of verses, or even of books. This had to be noted, lest the reason of these particles be sought too anxiously. The Prophet had said that he had from God light, which is the beginning of good works, and of true justice: now he addeth that he had strength and might to shun evils and to do good things, which from the divine light he knew were to be shunned, or to be done. "By thee," saith he, "I shall be delivered from temptation," that is, relying on thy help, by which I am made strong, I shall overcome all temptations, and shall shun all evils. "Through my God I shall go over a wall," that is, trusting in the help of the same God, and having received strength from the same, I shall be able to do all things, however hard, such as it would be to climb over a most high wall. In the Hebrew, for those words, *eripiar a tentatione* (I shall be delivered from temptation), there is ארץ גדוד *aruts gedud*, that is, *percurram exercitum* (I shall run through an army), or (as St. Jerome rendereth) *percurram accinctus* (I shall run girded). The Seventy Interpreters seem to have rendered the sense, not the words. For to run through the army of the adversaries, and to receive no wound, is the same as to be delivered from the darts of the enemies, which darts signify temptations, as the Apostle interpreteth, when he saith, *Ephes.* vi, 16: "Wherewith you may be able to extinguish all the fiery darts of the most wicked one." So also: I shall run girded through the midst of the enemies unhurt, signifieth the same as: I shall be delivered from temptation, when there shall be most need.

[Margin: Verse 33.]

**33. AS FOR MY GOD, HIS WAY IS UNDEFILED: THE WORDS OF THE LORD ARE FIRE TRIED: HE IS THE PROTECTOR OF ALL THAT TRUST IN HIM.**

He giveth the reason why he received from God light and strength, and why he so greatly trusteth in him, because God, good and truthful, is the protector of all that trust in him, and because he himself is the only true God and true Lord, from whom these good things can be looked for. "As for my God, his way is undefiled," that is, my God, whose way is undefiled, that is, God who is most holy, and walketh an undefiled way, that is, worketh most justly. "The words of the Lord are fire tried," that is, the promises of the Lord are most certain and most proved, as gold tried by fire: this God, I say, undefiled, and whose promise is most certain, "is the protector of all that trust in him." A clearer sense is had from the Hebrew, in which text *eloquium* (the word) is read in the singular number, and for the word *protector* there is *scutum* (a shield). Whence it appeareth that both God and his word are called the shield of them that trust in God. For so the Hebrew reading could be rendered to the letter: *Deus immaculatus in via sua, eloquium Domini igne examinatum, scutum est omnium sperantium in se* (God is spotless in his way, the word of the Lord is fire tried, he is a shield of all that trust in him). But because the Seventy Interpreters put *eloquia* (the words) in the plural number, and for the word *scutum* (shield) put *protector*, thence the sentence was darkened, which is to be made clear from the Hebrew.

[Margin: Verse 34.]

**34. FOR WHO IS GOD BUT THE LORD? OR WHO IS GOD BUT OUR GOD?**

This is the other reason, as we said a little before, why we must trust in God, and why light and might are to be looked for from him alone, because he alone is the true God. In the Hebrew there is: *Quis Deus præter Dominum? aut quis petra præter Deum nostrum?* (Who is God but the Lord? or who is a rock but our God?) from which we understand that our God alone is the true God, and, because he alone is the true God, that he alone is the true rock, firm and stable, in which we can safely trust and rest; and that all who trust in any other thing must needs be deceived and confounded.

[Margin: Our God alone is the true God.]

[Margin: Verse 35.]

**35. GOD WHO HATH GIRT ME WITH STRENGTH; AND MADE MY WAY BLAMELESS.**

He declareth now in particular the gifts received from God,

<!-- p. 99 (PDF 110) -->

by which, being delivered from all enemies, he came to the kingdom. And first he saith that he received from God might and innocence, which two are very rarely joined. For mighty men are prone to do injuries. But David was truly most strong and most innocent, so that he would not kill even his enemy Saul, when he most easily could. "God," saith he, "hath girt me with strength," that is, he granted me at the same time uprightness of life.[^7]

[Margin: Verse 36.]

**36. WHO HATH MADE MY FEET LIKE THE FEET OF HARTS: AND WHO SETTETH ME UPON HIGH PLACES.**

[Margin: Verse 37.]

**37. WHO TEACHETH MY HANDS TO WAR: AND THOU HAST MADE MY ARMS LIKE A BRAZEN BOW.**

He describeth in particular his strength, of which he had said: "Who hath girt me with strength," and saith that he received from God a wonderful swiftness in his feet, and a wonderful art of warring in his hands, and a wonderful might in his arms. He saith that his feet were made perfect for running, like those of harts, and that he was placed upon high places, that is, that with that swiftness of foot he easily climbed the highest mountains, which was verified when, fleeing from Saul, he dwelt on the highest and steepest tops of the mountains. He addeth that he had hands taught to war, and arms as it were of brass, in which might and the art of war are signified. Of which no one can doubt, who hath read the first and second book of Kings. For of the art of his hands the stone fixed in the forehead of Goliath, and the sling, can bear witness; of the strength of his arms the bears and lions are witnesses, which David slew by the sole force of his arms. In the Hebrew there is נחתה, *conteritur* (is broken), where we have *posuisti* (thou hast made). But the Seventy Interpreters did not read *nichathah*, but *natthatha*, which is *dedisti* (thou hast given), or *posuisti* (thou hast set). And so the modern Hebrew reading seemeth to have been corrupted by some chance: for it cannot be doubted that the Seventy Interpreters had corrected copies, and understood them most rightly.

[Margin: The wonderful strength of David is described.]

[Margin: Verse 38.]

**38. AND THOU HAST GIVEN ME THE PROTECTION OF THY SALVATION: AND THY RIGHT HAND HATH HELD ME UP.**

He declareth now the innocence, of which he had said: "Thou hast made my way blameless." For because by the gift of God David lived innocently, therefore God continually protected him, as he now saith: "Thou hast given me the protection of thy salvation." For swiftness of foot, and might of arm, and the art of fighting would have profited little against a king and a king's whole army, unless there had been present the protection of the salvation of God, that is, the divine protection saving him, and the right hand of God taking him up and sustaining him.

[Margin: Verse 39.]

**39. AND THY DISCIPLINE HATH CORRECTED ME UNTO THE END: AND THY DISCIPLINE, THE SAME SHALL TEACH ME.**

This also pertaineth to showing the innocence, or blameless way, of David. Not only, saith he, was thy protection present to me, but also thy discipline hath corrected me, that is, the instruction which is had from thy law, that directed me, lest I should err, and if at any time I was in danger of erring, that, being diligently looked into and considered, "hath corrected me," and that perseveringly even unto the end. The same "discipline" also of thy law henceforth "shall teach me." This discipline could also signify the fatherly chastisement, by which God through some temporal calamities corrected David, if at any time through human frailty he offended. In the Hebrew this verse is not found, but in place of this whole verse there are these few words: *Et mansuetudo tua multiplicavit me* (And thy mildness hath multiplied me). Perhaps the sense is the same; for the Hebrew reading can be thus expounded, *thy mildness*, that is, thy gentle and fatherly chastisement, *hath multiplied me*, that is, hath increased me in knowledge. For from the verb רבב *rabab*, which is found here, are named the *rabbim*, *masters* and *teachers*, because they are many in knowledge.

[Margin: Verse 40.]

**40. THOU HAST ENLARGED MY STEPS UNDER ME; AND MY FEET ARE NOT WEAKENED.**

He goeth on to relate his victories, which are all benefits of God. "Thou hast enlarged," saith he, "my steps under me," that is, thou hast made me go forward with great strides; "and my feet are not weakened," that is, nor did I therefore, being wearied, cease from my course. In the Hebrew these are in the future, *dilatabis* (thou wilt enlarge), etc.; but after the manner of the tongue the future is often put for the past.

[Margin: Verse 41.]

**41. I WILL PURSUE AFTER MY ENEMIES, AND OVERTAKE THEM: AND I WILL NOT TURN AGAIN TILL THEY ARE CONSUMED.**

[Margin: Verse 42.]

**42. I WILL BREAK THEM, AND THEY SHALL NOT BE ABLE TO STAND: THEY SHALL FALL UNDER MY FEET.**

These things do not seem properly to pertain to the future time, but to the past, as is gathered from what goeth before and what followeth. And as the Seventy Interpreters rendered *dilatabis gressus meos* (thou wilt enlarge my steps) as *dilatasti gressus meos* (thou hast enlarged my steps), so they could have rendered the rest, and made pasts out of futures. But the reading is to be kept as it lieth, and the verb *dixi* (I said) is to be understood, that the sense may be: "Thou hast enlarged my steps, and my feet are not weakened," therefore, having conceived great hope, I said within myself: "I will pursue after my enemies."

[Margin: Verse 43.]

**43. AND THOU HAST GIRDED ME WITH STRENGTH UNTO BATTLE; AND HAST SUBDUED UNDER ME THEM THAT ROSE UP AGAINST ME.**

Hence it appeareth that the Prophet speaketh of the past. As, saith he, I said: "I will pursue after my enemies," etc., so it was done, God helping. For thou, O God, "hast girded me with strength," that is, with might unto battle, and thou "hast subdued (*supplantasti*)," that is, hast made to fall, "under me them that rose up against me." That, *thou hast girded with strength*, is frequent in Scripture. "Being girded with power: who troublest the depth of the sea," *Ps.* lxiv. "Put on strength, O thou arm of the Lord," *Isa.* li. "The Lord is clothed with strength, and hath girded himself," *Ps.* xcii. "Stay you in the city till you be endued with power from on high," *Luke* xxiv. Now he signifieth that the chief arms consist not in sword and breastplate, but in strength and might; and therefore he is not the author of victory, who clotheth the soldier

<!-- p. 100 (PDF 111) -->

with sword and breastplate, but he who clotheth him with strength and might.

[Margin: Verse 44.]

**44. AND THOU HAST MADE MY ENEMIES TURN THEIR BACK UPON ME, AND HAST DESTROYED THEM THAT HATED ME.**

He repeateth the same in other and other words, that he may the more declare and praise the benefit of God. The sense of the verse is plain; for he signifieth that it was brought about by God that the enemies of David fled, and in that manner offered their back to be struck. But the words sound somewhat harsh.[^8] St. Jerome rendereth, *et inimicorum meorum dedisti mihi dorsum* (and thou hast given me the back of my enemies).

[Margin: Verse 45.]

**45. THEY CRIED, BUT THERE WAS NONE TO SAVE THEM, TO THE LORD: BUT HE HEARD THEM NOT.**

He giveth another cause of the victory: because not only did God hear his prayers, but he did not hear the prayers of his adversaries, although they also called upon the Lord.

[Margin: Verse 46.]

**46. AND I SHALL BEAT THEM AS SMALL AS THE DUST BEFORE THE WIND; I SHALL BRING THEM TO NOUGHT, LIKE THE DIRT IN THE STREETS.**

He seemeth to speak of the remnants of his enemies, as if he said: I have already won the victory over my enemies; but if perchance any remnants are left, I shall beat them into the smallest pieces, and cast them forth, as dust is wont to be cast forth by the wind, and I shall take them away from the earth, as the dirt of the streets is wont to be dried when a vehement wind bloweth.

[Margin: Verse 47.]

**47. THOU WILT DELIVER ME FROM THE CONTRADICTIONS OF THE PEOPLE: THOU WILT MAKE ME HEAD OF THE GENTILES.**

This had already been done; for before David composed this Psalm, he had been delivered from various contradictions and defections of the people, and had been made head of the Gentiles, that is, raised to the throne of the kingdom. And so either the words of the future tense are to be expounded for words of the past tense, which is usual in the Hebrew tongue; or certainly the continuance of the benefit is signified. As if the Prophet said: Hereafter, as thou hast done hitherto, "thou wilt deliver me from rebellions, and wilt establish me on my throne, if perchance it happen that I be cast down from it."

[Margin: Verse 48.]

**48. A PEOPLE, WHICH I KNEW NOT, HATH SERVED ME: AT THE HEARING OF THE EAR THEY HAVE OBEYED ME.**

[Margin: Verse 49.]

**49. THE CHILDREN THAT ARE STRANGERS HAVE LIED TO ME, STRANGE CHILDREN HAVE FADED AWAY, AND HAVE HALTED FROM THEIR PATHS.**

Not without cause doth he ask to be delivered from the contradictions of his own people, because he had found greater fidelity and obedience in certain Gentiles than in the sons of the people of Israel. Which is also a manifest prophecy of Christ, to whom the Gentiles cleaved, and from whom the Hebrews fell away. "A people, which I knew not," that is, the Gabaonites, the Gethites and others, whom I knew not for brethren, since they were Gentiles, serve me; "at the hearing of the ear they have obeyed me," that is, most promptly, as soon as the command was heard, they obeyed. But "the children that are strangers," that is, children bastard as to their manners, "have lied to me," that is, they deceived me, they served with a feigned heart. They "have faded away (*inveterati sunt*)," that is, they turned aside from me like withered leaves: for that the Hebrew word signifieth; "and have halted from their paths," that is, they did not walk rightly and faithfully with me. These things signify the rebellions under Absalom, under the son of Bochri, and others. In the Hebrew, for *claudicaverunt a semitis suis* (have halted from their paths), there is *contrahentur in angustiis suis* (they shall be drawn together in their straits), as St. Jerome rendereth; or *expavescent a carceribus suis* (they shall be afraid out of their prisons), as Pagninus rendereth. But it is to be known that the word יחרגו *iachregu* is not found save in this Psalm; therefore if it was lawful for Pagninus to render *expavescent* (they shall be afraid), and for St. Jerome *contrahentur* (they shall be drawn together), much more was it lawful for the Seventy Interpreters to render *claudicaverunt* (have halted). Nay, it is a wonder why later men have dared to depart from the version of the most ancient Interpreters in that word, which is not found elsewhere in the Scriptures, especially since the interpretation of the Seventy cohereth most of all with the other parts of this verse. Further, the other word מסגרות *misgheroth* properly signifieth *a narrow place*, or *a closed one*, from the verb סגר *sagar*, which is *to close*; and therefore St. Jerome rendereth *angustias* (straits), Pagninus *carceres* (prisons), and the Seventy *semitas* (paths): for paths are narrow ways. And so the sense is, that bastard children, who serve with a feigned heart, halt from the paths, that is, turn aside from the paths by which they should have walked with a straight foot.

[Margin: Verse 50.]

**50. THE LORD LIVETH, AND BLESSED BE MY GOD, AND LET THE GOD OF MY SALVATION BE EXALTED.**

He concludeth the Psalm praising God. "The Lord liveth," that is, now it hath appeared that the Lord liveth, and as he liveth, so may he live always, and "let the God," the author, "of my salvation be exalted."

[Margin: Verse 51.]

**51. O GOD, WHO AVENGEST ME, AND SUBDUEST THE PEOPLE UNDER ME, MY DELIVERER FROM MY ENEMIES.**[^9]

Let God, saith he, be exalted, who hath avenged the injuries done to me, and hath subjected to me the peoples who rebelled, and hath delivered me from the ambushes and the onset of raging enemies, such as were Saul and Absalom. For the word *iracundi* (wrathful) there is in the Hebrew אף *af*, which the moderns render *et* (and), or *etiam* (also). But since that word often signifieth *the nose*, and the nose in Scripture is put in many places for wrath, which is known from the change of the nose, the Seventy Interpreters rightly rendered *iracundi* (wrathful); for in the Hebrew phrase it is the same, *ab inimicis meis nasi* (from my enemies of the nose), that is, *of wrath*, and *ab inimicis iracundis* (from wrathful enemies).

[Margin: The nose for wrath, frequent in Scripture.]

[Margin: Verse 52.]

**52. AND THOU WILT LIFT ME UP ABOVE THEM THAT RISE UP AGAINST ME: FROM THE UNJUST MAN THOU WILT DELIVER ME.**

He prayeth for the preservation to himself of the divine benefits, namely to be lifted up above them that rise up against him, so that they strive in vain, since they cannot reach so high, and to be delivered from every unjust man. The particle *et* (and) in the beginning of the verse could have been

<!-- p. 101 (PDF 112) -->

omitted; for it is not found in the Hebrew and the Greek, nor hath St. Augustine it. It seemeth to have stuck in our text from the other interpretation of the word *af*, which signifieth *the nose*, and also the particle *et* (and); and perhaps it was in the margin: but it doth no harm to the sense.

[Margin: Verse 53.]

**53. THEREFORE WILL I GIVE GLORY TO THEE, O LORD, AMONG THE NATIONS, AND I WILL SING A PSALM TO THY NAME.**

For this cause, saith he, "I will give glory (*confitebor*)," that is, I will praise, confessing thy benefits, and that not secretly, but openly, and before the multitude of the people, that all may learn to trust in the Lord.

[Margin: Verse 54.]

**54. GIVING GREAT DELIVERANCE TO HIS KING, AND SHEWING MERCY TO DAVID HIS ANOINTED: AND TO HIS SEED FOR EVER.**

This conclusion standeth by itself, and the sense is: may the Lord be "giving great deliverance to his king (*magnificans salutes regis sui*)," that is, may he increase and multiply the salvation of body and of soul, and of all the things of the king, whom God himself chose for himself; and may he show perpetual mercy to David, whom he commanded to be anointed king, and to all his successors for ever. Which prayer was fulfilled in Christ Jesus our Lord, who reigneth and shall reign unto all eternity. Amen.
