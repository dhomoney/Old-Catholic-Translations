INTRODUCTION.

With no small reward assuredly shall they be gifted in heaven, and they shall work out for themselves their own certain eternal salvation, those Confessors who, discharging their office well, spend their labour upon the salvation of sinners. James the Apostle assureth us in these clear words: "He who causeth a sinner to be converted from the error of his way, shall save his soul" (namely his own, the soul of him that converteth, as the Greek text speaketh) "from death, and shall cover a multitude of sins." Epistle, chap. 5, verse 20.[^1] But Mother Church groaneth over the perdition of so many of her children through the fault of evil Confessors; for in their good or evil manner of acting the salvation or the ruin of the peoples chiefly lieth. Let fit Confessors be given (said St. Pius V), and behold the full reformation of all Christians. It is therefore to be held for certain, that if in all Confessors there were found that knowledge and that integrity of life, which so great a ministry demandeth, the world would not be so defiled with the filth of so many sins, nor would hell be so filled with souls from day to day. Now for goodness there is not here required that habitual goodness only, by which a man is simply set in the state of grace, but a positive goodness, namely that which is gotten by the exercise of the virtues, as becometh the minister of penance, who hath need of a twofold nourishment, of one, that he may sustain his own life; of the other, that he may feed his offspring. For the Confessor must direct the consciences of others, without erring either by too great indulgence, or by immoderate rigour;

<!-- p. [ii] -->

so many wounds must he handle, without defiling himself; with women and with young persons he will have so to deal, hearing in confessions their falls that are most shameful, as to take not even the least hurt to himself: he will have to use fortitude with men of high station, without being moved by respect of any man: lastly he ought to be wholly fragrant of charity, meekness and prudence. And so, that he may fulfil all these parts, there is need of no common uprightness, to which no man will attain, if, daily meditation helping him, he be not practised in prayer; otherwise he will lack the light and the graces necessary for rightly exercising this office, which (as is wont to be said) would be formidable even to the shoulders of angels.

Besides uprightness of life, the Confessor, since he is himself the judge of consciences, is bound also, as we have touched above, to be furnished with knowledge for judging rightly. Therefore I have thought fit to gather in this short work not only those things which regard mere practice, but also the more notable observations, and those more necessary to be known, upon the chief treatises of moral theology; noting the definitions, the principles, the texts both civil and canonical, likewise the decrees of the Pontiffs, and the declarations of the Sacred Congregations, and moreover the doctrines that are more necessary and more received; for as to the matters which need a more careful discussion, let the reader have recourse to the moral work which I have published divided into two tomes;[^2] where he will find them discussed at large with their reasons, and with citation of the places of the authors who treat of them. Here we shall only touch upon them briefly, as much as will suffice to stir up the diligence of the reader to weigh them. Some opinions which we have here followed will perhaps seem to some one either too rigid, or too indulgent; but I would beseech him that before

<!-- p. [iii] -->

he condemn them with this mark, he turn over the aforesaid work of larger size, distributed into three tomes (which hath been printed once at Naples and several times at Venice), the places of which are often cited here, because, reading there at large the reasons, and the authorities of the doctors who defend them, I hope for certain that they will seem to him by no means strange, nor rashly resting upon reason.

As often as citations are seen in the present work, with no author alleged, let the reader know that they are taken from our *Moral Theology*.

THE AUTHOR'S END

BY ALL MEANS TO BE READ FOR THE UNDERSTANDING OF THE WORK.

It is well to set down here beforehand some account of the end and the sum of this Work. I had already published at the Neapolitan press another larger Work of *Moral Theology*, which was afterwards often reprinted at Venice at the Remondini press in three Tomes in folio with some additions of mine, and lately also came forth again enriched with new accessions of mine, and which hath been received in many parts of Europe not without applause. In making that Work I endured the labours of fifteen years, running through and weighing the doctrines of very many authors whom I surveyed, of whom some I found more indulgent than is just; as men who, led by a false zeal of furthering the salvation of souls, indulged liberty too much, not without prejudice to the divine and ecclesiastical laws; but others I found who, abominating so great condescension, fell into the other extreme of a certain excessive rigour. This was my greatest labour, namely to choose out of so great a heap of opinions those opinions which on the one part should keep the obedience due to the divine Precepts and to those of the Church, and on the other should not add burdens which have not been laid on by God, binding every man to that perfection which according to human weakness is morally impossible to the generality of the Faithful. Hence I, taught partly by the reading of the Theologians, whom I studied to read stripped of all passion whatsoever; partly by the experience of thirty years of confessions and missions, composed the aforesaid Work for the instruction of the Young Men of our congregation. But because that work, as being more voluminous and more diffuse, could not so easily be read, or be procured by many; therefore I thought (a spur being added to me besides by many who desired it) to publish the present little Work. In this

<!-- p. [iv] -->

my chief end was, to make public a certain practical instruction for administering well the sacrament of Penance; but because in practice confessions can never be rightly heard, nor, as is meet, the direction of souls be held, unless there be had a knowledge of the principles, doctrines and more principal questions of moral matters; therefore I took pains to set forth here in a short and easy style all that which pertaineth to the sufficient no less than necessary instruction of any Confessor; noting in their proper places the definitions of the canons, of the pontifical bulls, and also of the Sacred Congregations, of which too little knowledge is had from other books. I have reduced the Work to a compendium, that it might be procured at less cost, and that it might be more easily read. As to the order, in the first part it will be treated of the Precepts of the Decalogue and of the Church. In the second of the Sacraments and Censures. In the third of Privileges, and among them of the faculties which Bishops and regular Prelates have as to absolution from reserved cases and censures. Then it will be treated how the Confessor ought to behave himself in practice with sinners who are in a proximate occasion, or with habitual sinners and relapsing sinners; and how with the divers kinds of persons; namely, the unlearned, children, the deaf, the dumb, those condemned to death, the possessed, maidens, and other women. Lastly four Appendices have been added. In the first there is an instruction for directing devout souls to perfection, with the exercise of meditation and of mortifications, of the frequenting of the Sacraments, and chiefly of Communion. There also it will be spoken of supernatural prayer, and particularly of the several degrees of contemplation; and of the manner of directing souls whom God raiseth to extraordinary graces of this kind, according to the various states in which they are found. In the second Appendix there is the Practice of assisting the dying, with seasonable advertisements to the Priests who assist, as to the remedies against temptations, concerning the last sacraments, and concerning the affections which are to be suggested to the dying, in the time of sickness and of the agony,

<!-- p. [v?] -->

with an account of the signs of approaching death. In the third Appendix there is the examination of those to be ordained, in which he that is to be ordained is instructed concerning the sacraments in general, and chiefly concerning the sacrament of Order in general, and in particular of every order. In the same place it is treated of the vow of chastity, of the canonical hours, and of censures in general and in particular, as much as is necessary for those to be ordained, and lastly of the sacrifice of the Mass and of the power and office of the Priest. Finally in the fourth appendix there are certain very useful admonitions for Confessors and Parish Priests, with a practical instruction on mental prayer. In this little Work there is not only (though briefly) all that which is contained in the aforesaid *Theology*, but moreover many other things have been added which are not found in the work published before. All to the glory of Jesus and Mary!
