BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XLIX.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*A psalm for Asaph.*

In the former book of *Paralipomenon,* chap. xvi, we read that Asaph was appointed by David chief of the Singers. But whether he himself composed the Psalms which are inscribed with his name, or only sang them, or presided over him that sang, is not altogether certain, as we said in the preface. The argument of the Psalm is the twofold coming of the Lord: one to preach, the other to judge; one hidden and in humility, the other manifest and in majesty. And since in both comings Christ gathereth men together, either to faith, or to judgment, the Psalm is rightly inscribed אסף *Asaph,* which is interpreted *congregans* (gathering). The Prophet addeth and declareth of what things chiefly we are to render an account in the day of judgment: accordingly the Psalm is most profitable.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. THE GOD OF GODS, THE LORD HATH SPOKEN: AND HE HATH CALLED THE EARTH.**[^1]

[Margin: Christ is true God and the Son of the true God.]

He beginneth from the first coming, and saith that the Lord hath spoken with his own mouth, who before spoke by the mouths of the Prophets; and that he hath called to his preaching not the Jews only, but all "the earth," that is, all that inhabit the earth; which he did by his Apostles, since "their sound hath gone forth into all the earth," as is said *Ps.* xviii. Now in this place the Lord is said to be "the God of gods," that we may understand that Christ is true God, the Son of the true God, who hath the same divinity with the Father: for the true divinity can be one and one only, but there are many gods, either by error and estimation, as the gods of the Gentiles, who "are devils," as is said in *Ps.* xcv; or by the grace of adoption, as the holy angels and holy men; or by likeness, as the princes and judges of the earth. But all these gods are subject to the one and only true God, who is therefore called "the God of gods." From the Hebrew St. Jerome rendereth *fortis Deus Dominus locutus est* (the strong God, the Lord hath spoken); but the Hebrew words admit both versions, and the sense of both readings is true, to the purpose of the things which are to be said. For if we regard the first coming, Christ the Lord showed himself the God

<!-- p. 329 (PDF 340) -->

of gods, since he was terrible to the devils, who are the gods of the Gentiles, and himself by the grace of adoption made many gods; and the very same was *the strong God,* since he overcame the enemy of the human race by the cross, and spoiled his goods. If we regard the second coming, he is most rightly called "the God of gods," since he will be the Judge of all judges; nor will the title of *the strong God* less properly befit him, when with a word he shall thrust down the devil and all his followers, whether devils or men, into hell fire, and shall shut the gates of hell with everlasting bars. He saith therefore: "The God of gods, the Lord hath spoken," that is, the Lord Christ, who is the God of gods, coming into this world, hath spoken the words of his Gospel. "And he hath called the earth," that is, and he hath called together the whole earth to hear his words, saying: "Come to me, all you that labour, and are burdened, and I will refresh you," *Matt.* xi.

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. FROM THE RISING OF THE SUN, TO THE GOING DOWN THEREOF: OUT OF SION THE LOVELINESS OF HIS BEAUTY.**

He declareth that by the earth he did not understand the land of Palestine, or any other particular land, but all the earth without exception; and at the same time he explaineth the place whence the Lord began to speak. For he began out of Sion, or out of Jerusalem, according to that of *Isa.* ii: "The law shall come forth from Sion, and the word of the Lord from Jerusalem;" and that of *Luke,* last chapter: "It behoved Christ to suffer, and to rise again from the dead, the third day: and that penance and remission of sins should be preached in his name, unto all nations, beginning at Jerusalem." "From the rising of the sun," saith he, "to the going down thereof," he hath called the earth, that is, all men who inhabit the earth from the beginning, where the sun riseth, even to the end, where the sun setteth. "Out of Sion the loveliness of his beauty," that is, the Lord hath spoken, and hath called the earth out of Sion, which is the loveliness of his beauty. Moreover the loveliness of beauty signifieth a most beautiful[^2] loveliness, or perfect beauty, as the Hebrew words rather sound. Now Jerusalem, according to the former sense, was *of perfect beauty,* as is said *Lam.* i,[^3] because it was a city most noble, most ancient, most populous, adorned with the royal and the pontifical seat, and also with the tabernacle, the ark of the covenant, and the other things which made it the head of religion and of the kingdom: whence also it was a type of the heavenly and divine city. But, according to the latter sense, the preaching of the Gospel was of perfect beauty, because it contained the mysteries of the redemption, the knowledge of the most holy Trinity, most efficacious sacraments, a perfect law, and came accompanied with innumerable signs and wonders; and it renewed and justified them that heard and believed, and exalted them from the lowest earth to the height of the heavens.

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. GOD SHALL COME MANIFESTLY: OUR GOD SHALL COME, AND SHALL NOT KEEP SILENCE.**

[Margin: The coming of Christ to judgment is terrible.]

Here he now foretelleth the second coming, as if he should say: The God of gods came and called the earth, but he came hiddenly, because in the form of a servant, in humanity, in meekness, that there might be place for redemption through his passion and death; but afterwards he "shall come manifestly" in great power and majesty, not in the manger of a cave, but in the clouds of heaven; not fastened to a cross between thieves, but on the seat of the Judge with all his angels. Neither shall he only come manifestly, but also he "shall not keep silence," as in the first coming, when as a meek lamb led to the slaughter he opened not his mouth, and as at this time he keepeth silence, while he seeth sins done and is silent; but he shall come with a trumpet and a dreadful cry: "At midnight there was a cry made: Behold the bridegroom cometh," *Matt.* xxv. And "he shall send his angels with a trumpet, and a great voice: and they shall gather together his elect from the four winds," *Matt.* xxiv. And: "The Lord himself shall come down from heaven with commandment, and with the voice of an archangel, and with the trumpet of God," I *Thess.* iv. And: "At the last trumpet: for the trumpet shall sound, and the dead shall rise again incorruptible," II *Cor.* xv.[^4] In the Hebrew those first words, *Deus manifeste* (God manifestly), are joined with the verse above in this manner: *Ex Sion species decoris ejus, Deus manifestabitur* (Out of Sion the loveliness of his beauty, God shall be manifested); and then a new verse is subjoined: *Veniet Deus noster, et non silebit* (Our God shall come, and shall not keep silence). But we are not bound to follow the Hebrew division, which was devised by the Rabbins: and that word, *manifeste,* in the Hebrew text is ambiguous, so that it may be the adverb *manifeste* (manifestly), and the verb *manifestabitur* (shall be manifested); accordingly the Seventy Interpreters did not err, who expressed it by the adverb. But if the Rabbins contend that the Hebrew word הופיע *hophiah* cannot be taken adverbially, we shall say that the Seventy Interpreters rendered not the words, but the sense; for it is the same: *Deus manifeste veniet, Deus noster et non silebit* (God shall come manifestly, our God, and shall not keep silence); and *Deus manifestabitur, veniet Deus noster, et non silebit* (God shall be manifested, our God shall come, and shall not keep silence).

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. A FIRE SHALL BURN BEFORE HIM: AND A MIGHTY TEMPEST SHALL BE ROUND ABOUT HIM.**

These things are commonly taken for the burning of the world, that is, of all those things which are on the earth, to wit, of cities, gardens, vineyards, palaces, living creatures, and the like corruptible things, of which St. Peter saith, II *Pet.* v:[^5] "But the day of the Lord shall come as a thief, in which the heavens shall pass away with great violence, and the elements shall be melted with heat, and the earth and the works which are in it, shall be burnt up;" of which burning St. Augustine disputeth, *Of the City of God,* Book XX, chap. 18, and the Scholastics on IV *Sentences,* dist. 47. The sense therefore is: "A fire shall burn before him," which shall burn up all things that it shall find upon the earth. "And a mighty tempest shall be round about him," that is, a very great commotion from every part of the world, both on the earth, and in the sea, and in the air, and in heaven, "men withering away for fear, and expectation of what shall come upon the whole world," as Blessed Luke saith, chap. xxi. Yet St. Augustine, expounding this place of the Psalm, by fire understandeth the divine judgment; and by the tempest the wind separating

<!-- p. 330 (PDF 341) -->

the grains from the chaff, that is, the distinction of the good from the wicked. Others not ill will have the wrath of the eternal Judge, with which he shall come at the last day, to be described by the fire and the tempest; or at least that all these things are signs of the majesty of almighty God; for when God came down also upon mount Sinai to give the law, the whole mountain seemed to burn, and thunders were heard, and lightnings were seen, and altogether a mighty tempest, and a very great terror, *Exod.* chap. xix.

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. HE SHALL CALL HEAVEN FROM ABOVE, AND THE EARTH, TO JUDGE HIS PEOPLE.**

[Margin: All angels and men shall be present at the divine judgment.]

He describeth the multitude of those who shall be present in that theatre, as it were, and shall come together to the greatest spectacle of all. "He shall call," saith he, "heaven from above," that is, from the upper part of the world the Judge shall call heaven itself, that is, all the angels, the inhabitants of heaven; for so the Lord saith, *Matt.* xxv: "When the Son of man shall come in his majesty, and all his angels with him."[^6] From the lower part he shall call "the earth," that is, all men who from the beginning of the world even to the end have dwelt upon the earth through the successions of generations. All the angels therefore and all men shall be present. Now he shall call this so great an assembly "to judge his people" (*discernere*), that is, to pass judgment upon his people, and to separate the elect from the reprobate; for, as is said *Matt.* xiii: "At the end of the world the angels shall go out, and shall separate the wicked from among the just." And *Matt.* xxiii:[^7] "He shall separate the sheep from the goats," that is, the heavenly Judge shall sever all the elect from all the reprobate, although they will be almost innumerable, with that ease with which a shepherd can separate a few sheep from a few goats. Others think that heaven and earth are called as witnesses of the divine sentence, as of a sentence that cannot be moved, as often elsewhere in the divine letters. The former exposition is St. Augustine's, the latter St. John Chrysostom's, whom Theodoret and Euthymius follow. Both are true, and the one is not repugnant to the other.

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. GATHER YE TOGETHER HIS SAINTS TO HIM: WHO SET HIS COVENANT BEFORE SACRIFICES.**[^8]

[Margin: The faithful alone are brought in by Blessed Matthew as to be judged.]

Although all men without exception are to be called to judgment, yet in a peculiar manner the judgment will pertain to the faithful: "*for* they that do not believe are already judged,"[^9] *John* iii; whence also *Matt.* xxv the faithful alone are brought in as to be judged, and are examined concerning works, not concerning faith. Therefore by the name of Saints are to be understood the faithful, who pertain to the people of God, who of old were consecrated to God by circumcision, and now are consecrated by baptism; so David in *Ps.* lxxxv saith: "Preserve my soul, for I am holy;" and the Apostle in his epistles calleth all Christians *saints.* He addresseth therefore the angels, and saith: "Gather ye together his saints to him," that is, bring to judgment the peculiar people of God, which he hath sanctified to himself by the sacraments; for that this is to be done by the ministry of the angels is manifest from chap. xiii of *Matt.*: "The angels shall go out, and shall separate the wicked from among the just;" and chap. xxiv: "And he shall send his angels with a trumpet, and they shall gather together his elect." That which followeth: "Who set his covenant before sacrifices," is a declaration of the word Saints; for they are called Saints "who set his covenant before sacrifices." Which sentence is clearer in the Hebrew text, where to the word it is read: *Qui feriunt pactum ejus super sacrificio* (Who strike his pact upon sacrifice), that is, who make a pact with God, that they may be his people, and he may be their God; which pact is upon sacrifice, that is, upon the divine worship, which consisteth chiefly in offering sacrifices. Our reading is a little more obscure, but it maketh the same sense; for by *testamentum* (covenant) is understood *pactum* (a pact), and by *ordinare* (to set in order) is signified *disponere* (to dispose). To dispose a covenant and to strike a pact are the same, as is manifest from divers places of Scripture, but chiefly from I *Mach.* chap. i: "Let us go, and make a covenant with the heathens that are round about us: for since we departed from them, many evils have befallen us." Therefore the sense of this verse is, that the saints of God are to be gathered together to judgment, that is, men dedicated to God, "who set his covenant before sacrifices," that is, who dispose a covenant, and strike a pact with God, that they may worship God with sacrifices, and God may be their God, and may always bestow benefits upon them. In the Hebrew copy there is the pronoun of the first person, in this manner: *Congregate mihi sanctos meos, qui feriunt pactum meum super sacrificio* (Gather ye together my saints to me, who strike my pact upon sacrifice). But the Seventy Interpreters in all these words read the letter *vau,* where now there is the letter *iod*; and it was easy for the letter *vau* to be changed into the letter *iod,* since they differ not, save that the one is a little longer than the other; and the letter *vau* being set in the text, the pronoun of the third person is made, and the Seventy rightly rendered: *Congregate illi, et sanctos ejus, et testamentum ejus* (Gather ye together to him, and his saints, and his covenant).

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. AND THE HEAVENS SHALL DECLARE HIS JUSTICE: FOR GOD IS JUDGE.**

All being gathered together to judgment, then at length "the heavens shall declare his justice," that is, from heaven the sentence shall be pronounced upon the good and the wicked; thence it will appear to all how great is the justice of God, which now often appeareth not, when, God permitting, the just are oppressed by men of iniquity: then therefore "the heavens shall declare his justice," because he shall come from heaven who shall judge most justly, and, sitting in the airy heaven, shall pronounce a most just sentence, and the whole heaven, that is, all the heavenly citizens, shall confirm the just sentence by their assent and praises, and shall say: "Thou art just, O Lord: and thy judgment is right." Neither can this declaration of heaven be false, "for God is judge," that is, because he who shall judge is God, upon whom injustice cannot fall.

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. HEAR, O MY PEOPLE, AND I WILL SPEAK: O ISRAEL, AND I WILL TESTIFY TO THEE: I AM GOD, THY GOD.**

The Prophet now passeth to the instruction of the people, and teacheth what the matter of the judgment will be, that is, of what things an account is to be rendered in the judgment, that every one may be able to prepare himself. And that the admonition may have greater credit, he bringeth in God himself speaking, and admonishing as a father. "Hear," saith God, "O my people, and I will speak;" for unless thou hear, I will not speak to thee, but to others who have ears to hear; for not without cause doth the Lord say in the Gospel, *Matt.* xi, and often elsewhere: "He that hath ears to hear, let him hear;" for as they who are deaf have ears for the ornament of the body, but not for hearing, so also they who have a mind, but attend not to the doctrine of God, have inward ears as though they had them not. It behoveth us therefore attentively to think and consider what God saith, if we will that he speak to us. He explaineth next more clearly, when he addeth: "O Israel, and I will testify to thee," that is, hear, O my people Israel, and I will speak by testifying, and by making known to thee that which most greatly concerneth thee. Now by Israel is not understood only the people which descended from Israel according to the flesh, but also the Christian people, which imitateth the faith of Israel; yea, this is here chiefly to be understood by Israel, since the Apostle saith, *Rom.* ix: "All are not Israelites that are of Israel: neither are all they that are the seed of Abraham, children; but they that are the children of the promise are accounted for the seed." And that which followeth: "I am God, thy God," is the reason why it behoveth us to hear, to wit, because he who speaketh is God, and peculiarly our God; and thereby we certainly know that he knoweth how, and is willing, to deliver to us most profitable precepts: for if he is God, he knoweth all things; if he is ours, he loveth us, and therefore is willing to teach us profitable things.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. I WILL NOT REPROVE THEE FOR THY SACRIFICES: AND THY BURNT OFFERINGS ARE ALWAYS IN MY SIGHT.**

[Margin: God is not delighted with the sacrifices of the ancients.]

The sum of the divine doctrine is, that God doth not require of us gifts, such as sacrifices are, as though he needed them, or as though these chiefly pleased him; but that he requireth inward virtues, faith, hope, love, obedience: for the sake of which virtues gifts please, and without which they please not. This is conformable to the words of Samuel, I *Kings* xv: "Doth God desire holocausts and victims, and not rather that the voice of the Lord should be obeyed?"[^10] And *Isa.* chap. i: "To what purpose do you offer me the multitude of your victims, saith the Lord? I am full," etc. And below: "Wash yourselves, be clean," etc. And of Christ himself, *Matt.* xxiii: "Woe to you scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites; because you tithe mint, and anise, and cummin, and have left the weightier things of the law; judgment, and mercy, and faith." And lastly of David himself in *Ps.* l: "If thou hadst desired sacrifice, I would indeed have given it: with burnt offerings thou wilt not be delighted. A sacrifice to God is an afflicted spirit: a contrite and humbled heart, O God, thou wilt not despise." He saith therefore: "I will not reprove thee for thy sacrifices," that is, I will not accuse or condemn thee in the judgment because thou hast offered me few sacrifices; then he giveth many reasons of this thing, of which the first is in the second part of this verse, to wit, because burnt offerings enough are daily offered to him; for when he saith, "and thy burnt offerings are always in my sight," that word, *autem* (and, but), is put for *enim* (for); for in the Hebrew there is the particle *et* (and), which is most often put for *enim* (for). The sense therefore is: I will not reprove thee for the want of sacrifices, for thy burnt offerings are always found upon my altar.

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. I WILL NOT TAKE CALVES OUT OF THY HOUSE: NOR HE GOATS OUT OF THY FLOCKS.**

[Margin: Verse 11.]

**11. FOR ALL THE BEASTS OF THE WOODS ARE MINE: THE CATTLE ON THE HILLS, AND THE OXEN.**

[Margin: Verse 12.]

**12. I KNOW ALL THE FOWLS OF THE AIR: AND WITH ME IS THE BEAUTY OF THE FIELD.**

This is the second reason why God doth not greatly require our sacrifices, because he himself is the Lord of all things; and therefore if he would have sheep, or oxen, or birds, or anything else, he can have them without any trouble, since he himself knoweth all things, and possesseth all things. "I will not take," saith he, "calves out of thy house: nor he goats out of thy flocks," that is, there is no need for me, if I would have calves, or he goats, to snatch them from thee, or to ask them of thee. "For all the beasts of the woods are mine," that is, mine are all the living creatures of the woods, as stags, wild goats, boars and the like. "The cattle on the hills, and the oxen," that is, mine are also all the animals which feed on the hills, as sheep, she goats, rams, he goats, oxen and calves. "I know all the fowls of the air," that is, not only are the four-footed animals mine, but also all the birds of the air, of which I know where they are, and for what they are of use. "And with me is the beauty of the field," that is, not only the animals, but also all the fruits of the earth, by which the fields are made beautiful and rich, are with me, pertain to my right, and I can use all of them as it pleaseth me; or it is with me, that is, the whole perfection of the field, with all its fruits, trees, living creatures, fountains and other things, is with me from eternity, subsisting eminently in my essence, so that I have no need to descend to those outward things. In the Hebrew, for that word *jumenta* (cattle), there is the word בהמות *behemoth,* which word signifieth *animalia* (animals) in general, and not only cattle properly so called; for in *Ps.* viii the same word is had, where our Interpreter rendereth *pecora campi* (the beasts of the fields); and in *Ps.* cxlviii, where we read *universa pecora* (all cattle), the same word is also found. And that, *in montibus et boves* (on the hills, and the oxen), might be rendered from the Hebrew *in montibus mille* (on a thousand hills); for the Hebrew word אלף *aleph,* which is had here, signifieth both *bovem* (an ox) and *mille* (a thousand). Therefore the Seventy Interpreters did not ill render *boves* (oxen), although St. Jerome

<!-- p. 332 (PDF 343) -->

[Margin: All animals are God's.]

rendereth *millium* (of thousands); but both readings have a true sense: for according to the reading of St. Jerome the sense is, *that the innumerable animals which are found on a thousand,* that is, on very many, *hills are God's;* according to the reading of the Seventy the sense is, *that the animals which wander freely on the hills, and also the oxen which are kept in the stalls, are God's;* and since in the Hebrew text, as it is now read, that, *in montibus* (on the hills), governeth a genitive after it, and ought to be read *in montibus bovis* (on the hills of the ox), which words do not seem to yield a fit sense, I suspect that the Seventy Interpreters did not read בהררי אלף *beharere heleph, in montibus bovis* (on the hills of the ox), but בהרר ואלף *beerer veheleph, in monte et bos* (on the hill, and the ox), one single letter being a very little changed, that is, *iod* changed into *vau,* which are letters most like, which could easily happen by the fault of the copyists; but for the sake of explaining the Seventy put the *plural* number for the *singular,* and for *in monte et bos* (on the hill, and the ox) they put *in montibus et boves* (on the hills, and the oxen). And that, *cognovi omnia volatilia cœli* (I know all the fowls of the air), St. Jerome rendereth from the Hebrew *cognovi omnes aves montium* (I know all the birds of the mountains); and truly the Hebrew word signifieth not *cœlum* (the air, heaven), but *montes* (mountains); neither is there a likeness of letters between the words, that we might suspect an error of the copyists. Yet the sense is the same, for *the fowls of the air* and *the birds of the mountains* are the same; for they are called *the fowls of the air* because they run to and fro through the airy heaven, and *the birds of the mountains* because for the most part they build their nests in the trees of the mountains. Lastly that, *et pulchritudo agri* (and the beauty of the field), is rendered by St. Jerome *et universitas agri* (and the whole of the field): but the Hebrew word זיז *ziz* is altogether ambiguous; for the Seventy in this place rendered it *pulchritudo* (beauty); in *Ps.* xcix[^11] they rendered the same word *singularis ferus* (a singular wild beast); in *Isa.* lxvi they translated the same word *introitus* (entrance). But St. Jerome in this place rendered it *universitas* (the whole); in *Ps.* lxxix, *omnes bestiæ* (all the beasts); in *Isa.* lxvi, *omnimoda* (of every kind). In this so great variety it is safest to follow the authority of the Seventy Interpreters, from whom as to the sense St. Jerome doth not dissent. For "the beauty of the field" ariseth from the whole of the field, that is, from all the fruits of the fields.

[Margin: Verse 13.]

**13. IF I SHOULD BE HUNGRY, I WOULD NOT TELL THEE: FOR THE WORLD IS MINE, AND THE FULNESS THEREOF.**

[Margin: Verse 14.]

**14. SHALL I EAT THE FLESH OF BULLOCKS? OR SHALL I DRINK THE BLOOD OF GOATS?**

This is the third reason why God doth not require our gifts for his own necessity, or profit, to wit, because he neither hungereth nor thirsteth; and consequently is neither hot nor cold, nor needeth anything; and if he did need, all things would be at hand for him, because he is the Lord of all things. "If I should be hungry," saith he, "I would not tell thee," to wit, that thou shouldst provide food for me, because "the world is mine, and the fulness thereof. Shall I eat the flesh of bullocks? or shall I drink the blood of goats?" That is, I do not feed upon things of that kind, who am a spiritual and immortal substance.

[Margin: Verse 15.]

**15. OFFER TO GOD THE SACRIFICE OF PRAISE: AND PAY THY VOWS TO THE MOST HIGH.**

[Margin: Verse 16.]

**16. AND CALL UPON ME IN THE DAY OF TROUBLE: I WILL DELIVER THEE, AND THOU SHALT GLORIFY ME.**

[Margin: The sacrifice of praise and the praise of God are different things.]

Hitherto he hath taught that hope is not to be placed in outward sacrifices, as though they sufficed without inward knowledge and love: now he teacheth that the sacrifice wherewith God is delighted, and which can make us secure in the last judgment, consisteth in the inward acts of the virtues. But it is to be observed that the praise of God is one thing, the sacrifice of praise another. For God can be praised by those who honour God with their lips alone, but praise cannot be sacrificed save by those who offer the incense of praise upon the altar of the heart, and with the fire of charity, that is, save by those who believe, and in some manner apprehend, that God is the supreme good, and who love with their whole heart God so believed and known, and admire and praise him as most beautiful, most good, most wise. Therefore the sacrifice of praise is the sign and effect of knowledge and love, and because the blessed in heaven always see and love God, therefore of them it is said, *Ps.* lxxxii:[^12] "Blessed are they that dwell in thy house, O Lord: they shall praise thee for ever and ever." He saith therefore: "Offer to God the sacrifice of praise," that is, praise God not with the lips alone, but from inmost knowledge and love. "And pay thy vows to the most High," that is, after thou hast praised God as he is in himself, regard him as he is the author and fountain of all thy goods, because of thyself thou art nothing, and render to him thanksgiving, and pay the tribute of obedience, which is the chief vow among all thy vows: for this thou didst promise when thou wast admitted to his people and household, and this he requireth more than outward sacrifices: "For obedience is better than sacrifices," as is said I *Kings* xv. Lastly, "call upon me in the day of trouble," that is, as in the day of prosperity thou oughtest to acknowledge that all thy goods descend from me, the fountain of goods, so also in the day of prosperity[^13] thou oughtest to flee to me, and to place thy whole hope in me: for I "will deliver thee" from all trouble, and thou in turn "shalt glorify me" with the sacrifice of praise and thanksgiving.

[Margin: Verse 17.]

**17. BUT TO THE SINNER GOD HATH SAID: WHY DOST THOU DECLARE MY JUSTICES, AND TAKE MY COVENANT IN THY MOUTH?**

After the instruction of the just, the Psalm goeth on to the teaching of the men of iniquity, but of the faithful. "To the sinner," saith he, "God hath said," that is, God hath willed the sinner to be admonished by me: "Why dost thou declare my justices, and take my covenant in thy mouth?" that is, why dost thou profess that thou knowest my law, so as to declare my precepts, in which all justice is contained, and profess also that my covenant, or pact, pertaineth to thee, while thou gloriest that thou art a son of Abraham, whereas thou neither

<!-- p. 333 (PDF 344) -->

keepest my law, nor observest the pact, nor imitatest the footsteps of Abraham?

[Margin: Verse 18.]

**18. SEEING THOU HAST HATED DISCIPLINE: AND HAST CAST MY WORDS BEHIND THEE.**

He describeth first the inward vices of the men of iniquity, then in the following verses he numbereth the sins of deed and of mouth. "Thou," saith he, "hast hated discipline," that is, thou art opposed in mind to the instruction of the divine law, "and hast cast my words behind thee," that is, and thou art become as though thou hadst forgotten my precepts, and hadst them behind thy back.

[Margin: Verse 19.]

**19. IF THOU DIDST SEE A THIEF THOU DIDST RUN WITH HIM: AND WITH ADULTERERS THOU HAST BEEN A PARTAKER.**

[Margin: The most frequent sins, luxury and avarice.]

From the forgetfulness and hatred of the divine law are born sins of deed, such as are thefts and adulteries, under which name fornications also are understood. Now these two are the most frequent sins, which arise from avarice and luxury; therefore the Prophet maketh mention of these two; and he saith: "If thou didst see a thief thou didst run with him: and with adulterers thou hast been a partaker," and he saith not *thou didst steal,* or *thou didst commit fornication,* because it is a less thing to steal, or to commit adultery, which is wont to be done secretly and under the goad of lust, than to run together with thieves, or to be a partaker with adulterers, because these things are done openly and readily, and from perversity of mind rather than from want or lust.

[Margin: Verse 20.]

**20. THY MOUTH HATH ABOUNDED WITH EVIL, AND THY TONGUE FRAMED DECEITS.**

He passeth to the sins of the mouth and saith: "Thy mouth hath abounded with evil," that is, evil speaking hath abundantly gushed out of thy mouth, as out of a fountain; for the Hebrew verb *shalach* signifieth *dimittere* (to let loose), as if he should say: Thou hast let loose thy tongue, as with broken reins, unto all evil speaking; but the Seventy Interpreters have declared the metaphor. "And thy tongue framed deceits," that is, thou didst speak fitly for deceiving, using the artifice of dissimulation, or of lies.

[Margin: Verse 21.]

**21. SITTING THOU DIDST SPEAK AGAINST THY BROTHER, AND DIDST LAY A SCANDAL AGAINST THY MOTHER'S SON: THESE THINGS HAST THOU DONE, AND I WAS SILENT.**

He addeth to the sin of the mouth, that he hath offended with the tongue not only against strangers, but also against his own brethren. "Sitting," saith he, "thou didst speak against thy brother," that is, not by the way and through anger, but sitting at leisure, and with a quiet mind, thou didst studiously and for a long time detract from thy brother. "And against thy mother's son," that is, against the same brother thou didst lay a scandal, by contriving calumnies, whereat he might stumble, and have an occasion of ruin. In the Hebrew text there is the word דפי *dophi,* which properly signifieth *opprobrium* (a reproach), or *probrum* (a disgrace), or *convicium* (a reviling). But the Seventy Interpreters rendered *scandalum* (a scandal), either because that word signifieth also *scandalum,* or because a *disgrace* cast upon another is a *scandal* to him. That which followeth: "These things hast thou done, and I was silent," among the Hebrews pertaineth to the following verse. But the pointing of the Hebrew text is not to be made much of, since it was devised by the Rabbins; and those words signify that God at this time seeth all things, but doth not straightway punish all things, that he may give place for penance. Therefore he seeth and is silent, as if he saw not; but the day of judgment will shortly come, in which, as was said above, verse 3: "God shall come manifestly, and shall not keep silence;" and this same thing he subjoineth, when he saith:

[Margin: Verse 22.]

**22. THOU THOUGHTEST UNJUSTLY THAT I SHOULD BE LIKE TO THEE: BUT I WILL REPROVE THEE, AND SET BEFORE THY FACE.**

While the impious sin without the fear of God, they seem to think that sins do not displease God; but in the judgment they shall understand that which is said in this Psalm. "Thou thoughtest," saith he, "that I should be like to thee," that is, that I was unjust and a friend of sinners, but it is not so; yea, "I will reprove thee," in the day of judgment, "and set before thy face" thy sins, that is, I will make thee see the grievousness and multitude of thy sins, and that thou canst not deny that the sentence passed against thee is most just. In the Hebrew there is: *Statuam te contra faciem tuam* (I will set thee before thy face); but the sense is the same. "I will set before thy face" is nothing else than: I will set thy life, thy actions, thy sins before thy eyes, that thou mayest not be able not to see them.

[Margin: Verse 23.]

**23. UNDERSTAND THESE THINGS, YOU THAT FORGET GOD; LEST HE SNATCH YOU AWAY, AND THERE BE NONE TO DELIVER YOU.**

This is an exhortation of the Prophet to the impious who forget that God is a just and almighty judge, that they may understand, thinking over with attentive consideration the things which have been said. "Lest" God "snatch" them "away,"[^14] while they think of nothing less, and bring them to judgment, and condemn them according to their deserts, and there be none who can deliver out of his hand. In the Hebrew it is had in the first person, *ne quando rapiam* (lest I snatch away), and according to this reading the words of God are continued; but the Seventy Interpreters seem to have read in the third person, and the sense is the same.

[Margin: Verse 24.]

**24. THE SACRIFICE OF PRAISE SHALL GLORIFY ME: AND THERE IS THE WAY BY WHICH I WILL SHEW HIM THE SALVATION OF GOD.**

God concludeth his words, teaching that the way of salvation is reduced to the one sacrifice of praise, so that they will be saved in the day of judgment who have continually offered this sacrifice; they reprobate and to be condemned, who have neglected to offer this sacrifice. "The sacrifice of praise," saith he, "shall glorify me: and there is the way by which I will shew him the salvation of God," that is, he who shall sacrifice praise to me, he shall be acceptable to me; for I will confess that I have been glorified by him, "and there," that is, in that sacrifice, will be "the way" to salvation; for by that way he shall come to the place in which "I will shew him the salvation of God," to wit, the divine, full and perfect salvation. In the Hebrew there is an ambiguous word, שם *sam,* which signifieth *illic* (there),

[Margin: Why is the sum of salvation placed in the offering of the sacrifice of praise?]

if it be read with the point on the right horn, as the Seventy Interpreters read; but if it be read with the point on the left, as St. Jerome read, it signifieth *posuit* (he hath set), or *disposuit* (he hath disposed); but in an ambiguous matter there is no reason why we should depart from the common and received reading. But whence is it that the sum of salvation is placed in the offering of the sacrifice of praise? St. Augustine answereth that it is from this, that no man truly praiseth God unless he be godly: for the impious, if they praise with words, praise not with their life; and thereby they do not truly praise, since their life contradicteth their words. It may also be said, as we admonished above, that "the sacrifice of praise" is not simple praise, but praise proceeding from the altar of the heart and the fire of charity: wherefore, since the sacrifice of praise includeth charity, and is the beginning of the sacrifice, or office, which is exercised for ever and ever in heaven by all the blessed, it is no wonder if the sum of salvation is included in it.
