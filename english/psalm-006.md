BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM VI.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*Unto the end, in hymns, for the octave. A psalm for David.*[^1]

That which is said, *pro octava* (for the octave), or, as it is in the Hebrew, על השמינית *hhal haschminith, super octavam* (upon the eighth), seemeth to be a kind of harp having eight strings, or something like. For in the first book of *Paralipomenon*, chap. xv, where the divers instruments are described with which the Psalms were to be sung, among others there is set down the harp upon the eighth. Yet that is not to be rejected which is commonly said, that by *the octave* in this place is signified the day of the last judgment, which shall be the eighth, because it shall follow after the six laborious days of this life, and after the seventh, of the rest of souls. For this Psalm is the first of those seven which pertain to penance. Nor is that likely which the Rabbins and their followers say, that this Psalm was composed by David for the recovering of the health of his body, which, to wit, he had lost on account of the adultery and murder which he had committed. For Scripture sufficiently declareth in the second book of *Kings* what punishment God exacted of David on account of the guilt of that sin: nor doth it anywhere make mention of a bodily sickness. But neither would the Church from the most ancient times have numbered this Psalm among the penitential ones, and that too in the first place, unless it pertained properly and to the letter to penance. And so the argument of this Psalm will be the prayer of a sinner fearing the divine judgment, and aspiring to reconciliation.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. O LORD, REBUKE ME NOT IN THY INDIGNATION, NOR CHASTISE ME IN THY WRATH.**

This is the petition of one truly penitent and contrite, who hateth sin above all things. For then doth God rebuke, that is, accuse, and chastise the sinner in wrath and in indignation, when he chastiseth not that he may save, out of fatherly love, but chastiseth that he may destroy, and that justice

<!-- p. 33 (PDF 44) -->

may be satisfied. Which he doth in this life, when he striketh with blindness and obstinacy, so that sin is the punishment of sin: but in the other, when he destroyeth into hell fire. This man therefore, shaken with horror, and fearing the abyss of the judgments of God, refuseth not the scourge of punishments which separate not from God, but drive to God; but he dreadeth that evil which is truly to be dreaded above all things, that is, to be given up to the desires of his heart, to shameful affections, to be hardened and blinded, and at length to be separated for ever from the face of God. Now wrath and indignation are taken in this place for the same thing, as also to be rebuked and to be chastised: for it is (as we have noted elsewhere) familiar to the Prophets to repeat the selfsame thing twice, for the sake of explaining, or of pressing it home.

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. HAVE MERCY ON ME, O LORD, FOR I AM WEAK: HEAL ME, O LORD, FOR MY BONES ARE TROUBLED.**

[Margin: Sins are truly miseries.]

To move God not to rebuke in indignation, the penitent David bringeth forward several arguments; and the first he taketh from his own weakness, as if he said: Look not, O Lord, upon my sins as offences against thee, but as my miseries and sicknesses; and so punish not as a judge, but cure as a physician; burn and cut, if need be, but out of mercy, to heal, not out of justice, to destroy. For sins are truly miseries, and the more they are committed out of malice, the greater miseries they are; and the less we know and fear them, the more miserable we are. Therefore, saith he, "have mercy on me, for I am weak," that is, look mercifully upon my sins, whatsoever they be, as sicknesses and diseases of my soul, which have made me weak and feeble. "Heal me, O Lord, for my bones are troubled." He repeateth the same in other words. For God, if he have mercy, taketh away misery, and so healeth; and thus it is the same thing, "have mercy on me" and "heal me." It is the same also, "for I am weak," and "for my bones are troubled": for by the bones is signified strength and fortitude; and the bones are then said to be troubled when fortitude wavereth, and strength is broken and weakened. Now the Greek and Hebrew words might also be rendered thus: *Tremuerunt* (have trembled), or *tremefacta sunt ossa mea* (my bones are made to tremble).

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. AND MY SOUL IS TROUBLED EXCEEDINGLY: BUT THOU, O LORD, HOW LONG?**

The second argument he draweth from the knowledge of his own sin, as in *Ps.* l: "For I know my iniquity." He saith therefore: Not only am I miserable, but I also acknowledge my misery, and for that cause my soul, beholding itself so deformed and miserable, and shuddering, is troubled exceedingly, and, being wholesomely terrified, becometh impatient, and crieth out: "But thou, O Lord, how long" hast thou not mercy, dost thou not heal? Now that word, *usquequo* (how long), without another word, hath a great emphasis. For it signifieth that it was uttered by a troubled soul, and one not able to frame a whole sentence.

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. TURN TO ME, O LORD, AND DELIVER MY SOUL: O SAVE ME FOR THY MERCY'S SAKE.**

This is the third argument, which is taken from mercy. "Turn to me," saith he, that is, look upon me. For the look of God is the cause of all our goods. "Turn thy face, and we shall be saved."[^2] And elsewhere, *Ps.* xxix: "Thou turnedst away thy face, and I became troubled." The Lord looked on Peter, and he wept bitterly. And James, chap. i, therefore calleth God *the Father of lights*, because as the sun by its look enlighteneth, warmeth and quickeneth bodies, so God, looking with the eye of his loving-kindness, enlighteneth, kindleth and quickeneth souls. "And deliver my soul," that is, bring it out of the pit into which it hath fallen; pluck it out of the snare of the hunters, wherein it is held taken and entangled; deliver it out of the hands of the enemies, to whom it was delivered up on account of sin. "O save me," that is, free me from the impending damnation to hell. For to save is properly to free from an impending danger of death. Now the order is to be noted. *First*, God turneth to us, and looketh upon us with his grace. *Secondly*, we turn to him, and so the soul is plucked out of fault. *Thirdly*, being freed from fault, we are saved from the danger of impending damnation. Now this whole thing, which is done in the act of justification, is not done through any preceding merits of ours (for what doth the wicked man merit, but punishment?); but through the mercy of God; therefore he addeth "for thy mercy's sake," as if he said: I dare to ask so mighty a gift, not because I am worthy, but because thou art merciful.

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. FOR THERE IS NO ONE IN DEATH THAT IS MINDFUL OF THEE: AND WHO SHALL CONFESS TO THEE IN HELL?**

This is the fourth argument, taken from the glory of God. I ask, saith he, not to be rebuked in indignation, because, if it were so done, I should without doubt be damned to eternal death, and so thy memory and thy praise would in part perish. For they that are damned to hell fire remember not God, to praise him, nor is there any in hell who confesseth to the Lord, that is, praiseth the Lord, confessing his benefits and wonderful works. They who understand this place of bodily death, and by hell understand the sepulchre, and will have the sense to be that the dead lying in sepulchres praise not God, nor are mindful of him, since they feel nothing, allege the words of Ezechias in *Isa.* chap. xxxviii: "For hell shall not confess to thee, neither shall death praise thee"; and it is plain enough that Ezechias asked to be freed from the danger of bodily death. But assuredly the place of Ezechias also ought to be understood of eternal death, and of the hell of the damned. For although Ezechias feared to die bodily, he feared also to die for ever, and he sang that canticle giving thanks to God, because he understood from the gift of bodily health, as from a sign of the divine benevolence, that his sins had been forgiven him, and that he had been freed from the danger

<!-- p. 34 (PDF 45) -->

of hell fire, whence he saith: "But thou hast delivered my soul that it should not perish, thou hast cast all my sins behind thy back. For hell shall not confess to thee, neither shall death praise thee: nor shall they that go down into the pit look for thy truth." And truly, if these things were understood of bodily death alone, the arguments would be very slender. For although those who are dead bodily, and lie in the sepulchre, praise not God, yet their souls live, and praise God; and even the bodies themselves in the sepulchre look for the truth of God, that is, the faithfulness of God concerning the promised raising up. They alone it is who go down into the pit of eternal damnation, who look not for the truth of God, nor remember God for good, nor praise him, nor ever shall praise him: and so St. Jerome, in his Commentary on Isaias, and other Fathers understand this place of Ezechias.

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. I HAVE LABOURED IN MY GROANINGS, EVERY NIGHT I WILL WASH MY BED: I WILL WATER MY COUCH WITH MY TEARS.**

The fifth argument the Prophet draweth from fruits worthy of penance. For, as the Apostle saith, I *Cor.* xi: "If we would judge ourselves, we should not be judged," that is, if we would condemn and punish ourselves, we should not be condemned, nor punished, by the Lord. For God spareth those who spare not themselves. He saith therefore that he not only understandeth and detesteth his sin, but also punisheth it according to his strength, and will punish it. "I have laboured," saith he, "in my groanings," that is, with so mighty a weeping have I bewailed my sins, that I am wearied, and yet I do not cease even now. For "every night I will wash my bed," that is, every night, instead of sleep and rest, I will abundantly bewail my sins, and will water my couch with tears. The abundance and long continuance of the tears is to be noted in this place. For in place of the word *lavabo* (I will wash), there is in the Hebrew אשחה *asche, natare faciam* (I will make to swim), as St. Jerome also translateth; and the Latin *lavabo* itself also signifieth not the shedding of a few drops, but so great a plenty that a bed can be washed with them. The word *rigabo* (I will water) also implieth a mighty plenty of tears running down after the manner of a little stream. Now that, *per singulas noctes* (every night), is ambiguous in the Hebrew. For it can signify, through the whole night, and so St. Jerome translateth; it can also signify, in every night, and so the Seventy took it: each is wonderful, and perhaps each is true together, that is, that for a long time, through every whole night, he shed most plentiful tears. In which mirror they ought to behold themselves who commit very many and most grievous sins, and are scarce able to send forth a few drops of a tear when they seek forgiveness from God.

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. MY EYE IS TROUBLED THROUGH INDIGNATION: I HAVE GROWN OLD AMONGST ALL MY ENEMIES.**

These things pertain to showing the effect of that very great pouring out of tears. For in the Hebrew, for *turbatus est* (is troubled), there is *caligavit* (is grown dim), or, my eye is eaten away, so that the sense is: my eye, on account of my rage and indignation against the foulness of sin, being eaten away by overmuch weeping, is grown dim, and is troubled. Now that which followeth, *inveteravi* (I have grown old), is in the Hebrew referred to the eye, and is of the third person, *inveteravit* (it hath grown old): yet because not only the Seventy, but St. Jerome also, translateth in the first person, *inveteravi*, or *consumptus sum* (I am consumed), it is credible that the Hebrew reading hath been somewhat changed since that time, so that in the word עתקה *hatecha* one *iod* is wanting at the end, and in place of ה there ought to be ת, and it is to be read עתקתי *hatachti*; which change could very easily happen. The sense therefore is: "My eye is troubled through indignation: I have grown old amongst all my enemies," that is, because I have grown old[^3] amongst all my enemies; and more clearly: my eye is grown dim for weeping, out of the indignation which I have conceived from this, that I have stuck so long in sins that I have grown old amongst all my enemies, as if he said: I cannot but be vehemently indignant with myself, that I have not yet perfectly overcome any vice, nor vanquished any enemy of my soul, but have waxed old amongst all my enemies. And he calleth enemies all who have driven him to sin, whether devils, or men, or the vices themselves and evil habits.

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. DEPART FROM ME, ALL YE WORKERS OF INIQUITY: FOR THE LORD HATH HEARD THE VOICE OF MY WEEPING.**

Because he took it so grievously that he had waxed old amongst his enemies, therefore he breaketh forth into these words: "Depart from me," that is, I will have your fellowship no more; I will not yield to your temptations, relying on the divine help. "For the Lord hath heard the voice of my weeping," that is, because the Lord, moved with mercy on account of my tears, hath not only forgiven my sins, but hath also increased my strength to resist you.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. GOD HATH HEARD MY SUPPLICATION: THE LORD HATH RECEIVED MY PRAYER.**[^4]

He explaineth what he had said, that he was heard, and repeateth the same twice or thrice, to signify the certainty of his being heard, and that he might in this manner give himself courage to resist temptations.

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. LET ALL MY ENEMIES BE ASHAMED, AND BE VERY MUCH TROUBLED: LET THEM BE TURNED BACK, AND BE ASHAMED VERY SPEEDILY.**

He concludeth, wishing for the entire end of spiritual wars. "Let them be ashamed, and be troubled," because they have profited nothing, but have laboured in vain; "let them be turned back" into their own place, whence they came; "and be ashamed," that is, let them depart confounded most speedily: because, to wit, I will not put off the change of life; but from this hour, and this moment, I set upon the right and perfect way of the Lord. This conclusion can

<!-- p. 35 (PDF 46) -->

also be a prayer for the conversion of those men who, whether by persecuting or by alluring, were to him a cause of sinning. For he wisheth and prayeth that they may be ashamed and troubled through a true knowledge and detestation of sin, and that so they also may be speedily turned to God. It can lastly be an imprecation to be fulfilled in the day of judgment. For then truly all the wicked, whether devils or men, who would have led the just into impatience, or other sins, shall be confounded and ashamed, and shall be turned to the knowledge of the truth, but unprofitably. For then they shall say that of *Wis.* v: "Therefore we have erred from the way of truth," etc. And this shall be very swiftly, because the day of the Lord delayeth not, although it now seem otherwise to us. But when it shall come, and shall come suddenly, then it will be felt how swiftly it hath come. In the Hebrew it is: *Erubescant, et conturbentur vehementer* (Let them be ashamed, and be very much troubled). Which word, *vehementer* (very much), is not found in this place in our Greek and Latin copies,[^5] but that it was in the edition of the Seventy Interpreters St. Jerome witnesseth in his epistle *to Sunia and Fretella*. But if one consider rightly, that particle is not wanting in our copies, but is set a little lower. For where we have, "let them be turned back, and be ashamed very speedily," in the Hebrew there is not *valde* (very), but only *velociter* (speedily). From which we understand that the sense is not: let them be ashamed very speedily, but: let them be ashamed very much, and let them be ashamed speedily. And in this manner all the copies will agree.

[^2]: Translator's note: so Bellarmine quotes it (*Converte faciem tuam*). The Vulgate at Ps. lxxix, 4 reads *Deus, converte nos, et ostende faciem tuam, et salvi erimus*, and the Douay-Rheims "Convert us, O God: and shew us thy face, and we shall be saved".

[^3]: Translator's note: the Latin is printed *inveterari* (to grow old), where the sense requires *inveteravi* (I have grown old).

[^4]: Translator's note: so the heading is printed (*Exaudivit Deus*). The Vulgate reads *Exaudivit Dominus*, and the Douay-Rheims "The Lord hath heard my supplication".

[^5]: Translator's note: the verse heading above, as printed, has *vehementer*, as the Clementine Vulgate has; Bellarmine here speaks of copies that lacked it.
