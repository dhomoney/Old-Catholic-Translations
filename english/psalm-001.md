R. BELLARMINE

EXPLANATION OF THE PSALMS.

PSALM I.

## ARGUMENT.

[Margin: Who is the author of the first Psalm?]

The first Psalm hath no title among the Hebrews, and there are not wanting those who think it to be of Esdras, who set the Psalms in order; but it is commonly ascribed to David by Ambrose, Hilary, Basil, Augustine and the rest. Even St. Jerome himself, who otherwise maketh many authors of the Psalms, saith in his *Epistle to Paulinus on the Study of the Scriptures* that this first Psalm is David's. Lastly, in the Greek text a title hath been added, whether by the Seventy or by others: *A song of David, Prophet and king.* Now this Psalm is most fitly set in the first place, because it treateth of beatitude; for beatitude is the foundation and beginning of moral doctrine. The argument of the Psalm, therefore, is to show that beatitude, whether in hope or in very deed, falleth to none but just men, and that we must therefore give ourselves to justice, that we may come unto beatitude.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1]

**1. BLESSED IS THE MAN WHO HATH NOT WALKED IN THE COUNSEL OF THE UNGODLY, NOR STOOD IN THE WAY OF SINNERS, NOR SAT IN THE CHAIR OF PESTILENCE.**[^1]

In the first and second verses the Prophet teacheth that beatitude, so far as it can be had in this life, consisteth in nothing else than true justice. The Apostle teacheth the very same, *Rom.* xiv: "The kingdom of God is not meat and drink; but justice, and peace, and joy in the Holy Ghost." For only truly just men are the friends of God, nay, his sons also, and thereby heirs of the kingdom; blessed in hope, as touching the most perfect felicity, and meanwhile here enjoying solid joy and the peace which surpasseth all understanding. Now in the first verse he describeth the just man by negation, in the second by affirmation, as if he had said briefly that he is just, and thereby blessed, who declineth from evil and doth good.

[Margin: Only truly just men are the friends of God.]

Here it must be carefully noted and committed to memory that it is the custom of David and of the other prophets to say the same thing twice, so that one part of a verse is the repetition or explanation of the other. For example, *Exod.* xv, in the canticle of Moses: "He is my God, and I will glorify him: the God of my father, and I will exalt him." *Deut.* xxxii: "Let my doctrine gather as the rain, let my speech distil as the dew." *Ps.* xxxiii: "I will bless the Lord at all times, his praise shall be always in my mouth." These occur very frequently, since this repetition is a kind of ornament proper to the holy Prophets. The first part of the first verse, then, containeth this meaning: that he is blessed who doth not transgress the law of God. But David, after the manner of poets, hath expressed this meaning by the metaphor of a way and of walking: "Blessed," saith he, "is the man who hath not walked in the counsel of the ungodly," that is, he is blessed who is just; and he is just who hath not walked in the counsel of the ungodly, that is, who in the way of life hath not followed the counsels, laws and opinion of the ungodly, which are wholly contrary to the way of God, that is, to the law of God.

[Margin: What is it to stand in the way?]

The other part of the same verse explaineth the very same thing in other words of the same meaning. For when he saith: "Nor stood in the way of sinners," he doth not mean, Nor halted in the way of sinners, but, Nor walked in the way of sinners. For to stand in the way, in this place, is not to rest and not to walk, but, in walking, not to depart from the way once begun. And so it is the same thing, Not to walk in the counsel of the ungodly, and, Not to stand in the way of sinners: for each signifieth that he is just who withdraweth from the way, that is, from the law and counsels, of sinners.

[Margin: What is it to sit in the chair of pestilence?]

And because the law of the Lord is broken not only by evil living but also by evil teaching, according to that of *Matt.* v: "He that shall break one of these least commandments, and shall so teach men, shall be called the least in the kingdom of heaven"; therefore the Prophet addeth: "Nor sat in the chair of pestilence"; that is, he is just, and thereby blessed, who transgresseth the law of the Lord neither by works nor by doctrine. For to sit in the chair of pestilence is to be in the assembly of pestilent men, or to sit beside the pestilent, and with them to despise the law of the Lord, as though it availed nothing toward living well, that is, with joy and gladness, but it were more profitable to fulfil the desires of the flesh and to indulge the senses in all things. The voice of them that sit in the chair of pestilence is described in *Mal.* iii, in these words: "He laboureth in vain that serveth God, and what profit is it that we have kept his ordinances?" As to the words: for the word *beatus* (blessed) the Hebrew hath אשרי

<!-- p. 10 (PDF 21) -->

*ascre*, which word hath the form of the plural number, and is therefore rendered by some more recent writers *beatitudines viri* (the beatitudes of the man), etc. But it is more probable that the word is indeclinable, and signifieth both *blessed* and *beatitude*. For not only did the Seventy render it μακάριος ἀνήρ, that is, *beatus vir* (blessed is the man); but St. Jerome also rendered it from the Hebrew: *Beatus vir*. For *cathedra pestilentiæ* (chair of pestilence) the Hebrew hath, to the letter, *cathedra* (chair) לצים *lezim*, that is, *derisorum* (of scoffers); but the sense is altogether the same. For there is no greater pestilence than to scoff at the law of God, and at them that strive to live justly and piously. Lastly, St. Basil noteth that by the words *Blessed is the man* women are not shut out from beatitude, but included, since the man is the head of the woman, and in the head the members also are understood.

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. BUT HIS WILL IS IN THE LAW OF THE LORD, AND ON HIS LAW HE SHALL MEDITATE DAY AND NIGHT.**

[Margin: The justice of this life doth not require that we offend not.]

In this second verse the just man is described by affirmation, and here also there are two parts, of which one is almost a repetition of the other. Now he is rightly said to be just, or blessed, whose will is in the law of the Lord; for it is not required for the justice of this life that we offend in nothing, since it is written, *James* iii: "In many things we all offend"; but it is enough if we be so affected toward the law of the Lord that we desire to fulfil it wholly, and that, if perchance we fall into some sin, as assuredly we often fall, it be beside our good will, that is, beside the charity which we have toward God and toward his law, and not against charity; and so it be a sin, not a crime, that is, venial, not mortal. The same is found in other words in another Psalm: "The law of his God is in his heart." For both the will, or heart, of the just man is in the law of God, and the law of God is in the will, or heart, of the just man. The law is in the heart as in its seat, and the heart is in the law as in a thing beloved, which is ever thought upon and desired. And this is what followeth: "And on his law he shall meditate day and night." For this is for the law to be in the will, or the will in the law: that the mind be perpetually exercised in thinking upon it and loving it, so that whatsoever is to be done may be done according to that rule. Therefore the words *day and night* do not signify that the just man cannot rest even for a moment of time from the contemplation of the divine law, but that he ought very frequently to recall his mind to the precepts of the law, and to be ever mindful of it whensoever anything occurreth to be thought, said or done wherein there is danger lest justice be violated. Moreover, not only the Latin word *meditabitur* (he shall meditate), but also the Greek μελετήσει and the Hebrew הגה *ieghe*, can be referred to the exercise whether of thought, or of voice, or of work. *Ps.* xxxiv: "My tongue shall meditate thy justice." And *Ps.* xxxvi: "The mouth of the just shall meditate justice."[^2]

[Margin: Virgil, Eclogue I.]

A profane poet also hath said: "*Meditabor arundine Musam*" (I will practise the Muse upon the reed).[^3] And the exercises of soldiers were called military meditations. And so the sense is: "And on the law of the Lord he shall meditate day and night," that is, he shall be assiduously exercised in thinking upon, fulfilling and teaching the law of the Lord.

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. AND HE SHALL BE LIKE A TREE WHICH IS PLANTED NEAR THE RUNNING WATERS, WHICH SHALL BRING FORTH ITS FRUIT, IN DUE SEASON.**

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. AND HIS LEAF SHALL NOT FALL OFF: AND ALL WHATSOEVER HE SHALL DO SHALL PROSPER.**

The Prophet hath declared who is to be called truly just: now he declareth that such a just man is blessed, in hope in this world, in very deed in the world to come. And the declaration is taken from the likeness of a tree which, planted beside streams of waters, hath all things that can be required in a most happy tree. For some trees bring forth leaves only, and keep them not long; others bring forth leaves and keep them always, but their fruits are either too early or too late; others, lastly, yield fruits in their season and are always clothed with leaves, but bring not all their fruits to ripeness: those trees alone, therefore, are in every part most perfect which bring forth leaves and fruits, if they keep their leaves always, and yield all their fruits whatsoever ripe in their season. Such trees are pines, palms and olives, to which Scripture is wont in many places to compare just men.

[Margin: To what trees the just are compared.]

To these most happy trees, then, the Prophet in this place saith that the just are like. For the just, "founded and rooted," as the Apostle saith, "in charity," are near by friendship to the living fountain, whence they ever draw the moisture of grace; and they do good works at the fitting time, and all things work together for them unto good, and they are ever green with glory and honour. For although they be sometimes despised by carnal men, yet they are honoured by the wise and, what is more, by the angels and by God himself. And these things indeed in this world; but moreover they bear fruit in their season, because they work out true salvation, to be received at the fit time, that is, after death; whereas the wicked seek felicity before the time, in this life, and so lose both the temporal and the everlasting. And they keep their leaves always, because they shall receive, according to the word of the Apostle Peter, I *Pet.* iii, "a never fading crown of glory";[^4] and: "The just shall be in everlasting remembrance," *Ps.* cxi. And lastly, "whatsoever they do shall prosper," because all their works, even to a cup of cold water given in the name of a disciple, shall receive a full and perfect reward. I know that the Hebrew text in modern Bibles maketh but one verse of these two, and endeth the former part of the verse at those words: "And his leaf shall not fall off." Led by which division, some expound those words, "And all whatsoever he shall do shall prosper," not of the real tree, but only of the just man. But we follow the Hebrew reading which is in the works of St. Jerome, with which the Greek and Latin copies agree. And certainly, according to

<!-- p. 11 (PDF 22) -->

our reading the similitude taken from the tree is far more beautiful and more full. It is also to be observed in this place that the word *prosperabuntur* (shall prosper) is in the Hebrew יצליח *iezliach*, which properly signifieth *he shall make to prosper*. Wherein is noted the active power of the tree, which concurreth to the ripening of all its fruits; and in like manner, in the application of the similitude, is noted the active power of free will, which concurreth with the grace of God unto meritorious works. Which is against the tenets of the Lutherans.

[Margin: Free will concurreth with grace unto meritorious works.]

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. NOT SO THE WICKED, NOT SO: BUT LIKE THE DUST, WHICH THE WIND DRIVETH FROM THE FACE OF THE EARTH.**

By comparison with the misery of the wicked the Prophet confirmeth the felicity of the just. For lest any man should think those good things to be common to all, and to come from natural causes only, and not chiefly from the providence of God, he addeth: "Not so the wicked," that is, they shall receive not the same lot, but one wholly contrary. And most elegantly doth the Prophet describe the misery of the wicked, and set it against the felicity of the just. The just, by reason of the abundance of divine moisture, are green and bear fruit, nor do they ever lose their greenness or the fruit of their labours. The wicked, on the contrary, destitute of that divine moisture, dry and barren after the manner of the lightest dust, scattered by the wind, leave not so much as a trace of themselves; nor do they lose only all glory, riches and pleasures, but they themselves also perish for ever. In the Hebrew text there is not that repetition, *non sic* (not so), nor the words *a facie terræ* (from the face of the earth). But these were added by the Seventy Interpreters for the sake of explaining or adorning. Likewise the word for *dust* is not עפר *haphar*, which properly signifieth dust in general; but it is מץ *mots*, which signifieth *gluma* (husk), that is, a kind of dust from the threshing of chaff, namely chaff most finely ground and as it were reduced to dust, which is exceedingly light, so that a slight breath can drive it far away.

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. THEREFORE THE WICKED SHALL NOT RISE AGAIN IN JUDGMENT: NOR SINNERS IN THE COUNCIL OF THE JUST.**

In the edition of Sixtus V and Clement VIII there is rightly put *non resurgent* (shall not rise again), whereas in most copies there was *non resurgunt* (do not rise again). For from the Hebrew, the Greek, the Chaldee, and the more correct manuscripts it is gathered plainly enough that we must read *non resurgent*. But the words *in concilio justorum* (in the council of the just) are put from the Hebrew; for in the Greek and in the Latin manuscripts there is constantly read *in consilio* (in the counsel). It may indeed have come to pass that, from the likeness of the words, *consilium* was put in the Latin copies for *concilium*; but it may also have come to pass that into the Hebrew copies there crept עדה *hada* for עצה *haza*, that is, *concilium* (council) for *consilium* (counsel).[^5] Certainly the Seventy Interpreters, who rendered in the same manner in the first verse and in the sixth, putting βουλή in both places, seem to have read the same Hebrew word in both. And so both readings seem worthy of approval: the one for the authority of the Vulgate edition, which agreeth with the Hebrew; the other for the authority of the Seventy Interpreters, whose word βουλή is everywhere rendered in the Vulgate Latin edition itself by *consilium*, not *concilium*. The Prophet, then, declareth in this place that the difference between the just and the wicked shall be clearly made manifest in the last judgment. For at this present time it often happeneth that all things are confounded. He saith therefore: "Therefore the wicked shall not rise again in the judgment of the just." For the words *of the just* belong to both parts of the verse, just as the words *shall not rise again* are referred not only to *in judgment* but also to *in the council*; and it is almost the same thing: "The wicked shall not rise again in the judgment of the just," and: Sinners shall not rise again in the council, or counsel, of the just. And so that question ceaseth which some treat at length in this place, why the Prophet said: "The wicked shall not rise again in judgment." Now David hath beautifully joined the last things to the first; for as he had said in the beginning that the just have not walked in the council[^6] of the ungodly, nor sat in their assembly, so now he saith that in the day of judgment the wicked shall not rise again in the judgment and council of the just. The sense, then, is this: "Therefore the wicked shall not rise again in the judgment of the just," that is, the judgment of the just shall be far other than that of the wicked. Which is explained by the words following: "Nor sinners in the council of the just," that is, when the wicked shall rise again, they shall not be in the assembly and fellowship of the just, but, being adjudged to hell, shall be in the company of the damned. But if we read *in consilio justorum* (in the counsel of the just), *consilium* can be taken for the place of counsel, and then it would come to the same as the word *concilio*. And since the words *non resurgent* might fitly be rendered from the Hebrew *shall not stand*, or *shall not stand fast*, there will be this sense also: in the judgment and company of the just, namely when the Saints shall sit with Christ as judges of the world, then sinners shall not stand, but shall fall. "*But* the just shall stand with great constancy against those that have afflicted them," *Wis.* v, 1.[^7]

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. FOR THE LORD KNOWETH THE WAY OF THE JUST: AND THE WAY OF THE WICKED SHALL PERISH.**

He giveth the reason why the wicked shall not receive in the judgment the glory of the just: namely, because the way of the just is approved by God, who cannot be deceived, and thereby is a right way, and one that leadeth to the city of God; the way of the wicked is not approved by God, and they that walk by it labour in vain, because they shall never arrive. The word *knoweth* is taken for the knowledge of approbation; and when it is said: "God knoweth the way of the just," we are made certain that they who walk by that way are secure of true felicity. But the words *the way of the wicked shall perish* signify that the walking of the wicked hath been undertaken in vain, because by that way one cometh not to the desired place. Therefore in this place *iter* (way, journey) is not taken for the material way on which one walketh, but for the walking itself, as we have said. And by walking

is understood the course of the whole life, its pursuits, labours and strivings, all which shall profit the wicked nothing. This Psalm can be applied to all the Saints, but chiefly, and by antonomasia, to Christ, who above all men and Angels did not transgress the law of God, but fulfilled it perfectly, becoming obedient unto death. And therefore, as a tree planted beside streams of waters, having a soul hypostatically united to the living fountain, which is the Word of God, the fountain of Wisdom on high, he was crowned with glory and honour, and brought forth wonderful fruits, and brought them to ripeness: to wit, he called, justified and glorified all the elect.
