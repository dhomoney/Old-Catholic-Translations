BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XXXI.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*To David himself, understanding.*

This title showeth that after the sin was committed, the eyes of David's mind were opened by the grace of God, that he might understand how grievously he had offended, which before, being blinded by carnal delight, he had not been able to understand. The same title showeth also that the Psalm is obscure, and that to enter into it there is need of the gift of understanding. Rightly is this same Psalm numbered by St. Gregory and others among the seven penitential Psalms, since it is wholly spent in bewailing his own sin, and in exhorting men to penance.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. BLESSED ARE THEY WHOSE INIQUITIES ARE FORGIVEN, AND WHOSE SINS ARE COVERED.**[^1]

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. BLESSED IS THE MAN TO WHOM THE LORD HATH NOT IMPUTED SIN, AND IN WHOSE SPIRIT THERE IS NO GUILE.**

No man knoweth better how sweet health is, than he who bewaileth the loss of it. The Prophet David then first truly acknowledged how sweet it is to be without sins, when he tasted the bitterness of sin. This is the cause why he beginneth the Psalm of penance from the praise of pardon and of innocence: for these make the soul sound, and are opposed to the sickness, which is contracted from sins. He beginneth from pardon, both that he may go forward from the lesser things to the greater, and because that was his most recent health. "Blessed are they whose iniquities are forgiven." It is an exclamation, and a proclaiming of the good, which is in pardon, as if he should say: O how happy are they, who after they have sinned, are not despised by God, but being stirred up by his grace, are converted to penance, and come to pardon! "And whose sins are covered"; the same is repeated in other words; for sins, when they are forgiven, are hidden and covered, so that they nowhere appear; but of this word we shall say more a little after. "Blessed is the man to whom the Lord hath not imputed sin." He passeth from pardon, which is of many, to innocence, which is of very few, and saith: O truly blessed and happy, whosoever he is, who hath done nothing, which could be reputed unto sin, to whom therefore the Lord, whose judgment is most right, hath imputed nothing at all unto sin. Nor only hath he not sinned in deed, but "neither hath guile been found in his spirit," that is, he hath sinned neither in word, nor in thought: for spirit can be referred to either spirit: to that, by which we are wise, and which is called the heart, or the mind, and to that by which we speak, and which is called the spirit of the mouth, or of the lips. Of the former the Apostle saith, I *Cor.* ii: "For what man knoweth the things of a man, but the spirit of a man that is in him?" Of the latter, I *Cor.* xiv: "I will pray with the spirit, I will pray also with the understanding; I will sing with the spirit, I will sing also with the understanding." Now by innocence in this place we understand, not a natural innocence, or one gotten by a man's own works without the gift of God, which is none; but that, which God hath imparted to some few by the gift of his singular grace, to whom he hath so forgiven[^2] the sin contracted by another's will, that is original sin, that he hath not suffered them to commit deadly sin by their own will; which is a more excellent kind of remission. Therefore all innocence hath some remission of sin annexed to it, and of all, Christ excepted, it can be said: "All have sinned, and do need the grace of God."[^3] And so the Apostle, *Rom.* iv, bringeth forward this place of the Psalm to prove, that no man is justified by works going before grace; and he saith: "To him that worketh not, yet believeth in him that justifieth the ungodly, his faith is reputed to justice, according to the purpose of the grace of God: as David also termeth the blessedness of a man, to whom God reputeth justice without works: Blessed are they whose iniquities are forgiven, and whose sins are covered: blessed is the man to whom the Lord hath not imputed sin." By which words the Apostle expoundeth the Prophet, and saith that David doth not call them blessed, who have obtained justice by their own works; but them, to whom God hath by grace forgiven sins, and given justice. Moreover the Prophet seemeth to me to have had regard to certain singular men, when he saith: "Blessed is the man to whom the Lord hath not imputed sin," etc., namely to holy Job, who saith of himself, chap. xxvii: "I will not depart from my innocence: my justification, which I have begun to hold, I will not forsake: for my heart doth not reprehend me in all my life." Likewise to Abel, Henoch, Noe, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, of whom no sins are read in the Scriptures. Perhaps also he foresaw in spirit Jeremias, and John the Baptist, sanctified in the womb, and the Virgin Mother of God herself, by a more excellent privilege preserved from original sin and from all actual sin. As to the words, in the Hebrew it is in the singular, אשרי נשוי פשע, *blessed is he, whose iniquity is forgiven, and whose sin is covered;* but with the points changed it can be read: *Blessed are they whose iniquities are forgiven;* which

reading is most true, as the edition of the Seventy, confirmed by the Apostle, is to us an argument great enough. Again in the Greek copy there is, ἐν στόματι αὐτοῦ, *in ore ejus* (in his mouth), for that which is in the Hebrew and in the Latin, *in spiritu ejus* (in his spirit): but St. Jerome in his epistle *to Sunia and Fretella* witnesseth that that, *in ore ejus,* is from the translation of Symmachus; but that the Seventy, and all the other Interpreters rendered, *in spiritu ejus,* as in the Hebrew. The heretics of this time endeavour to establish three false tenets from these verses: and perhaps the Holy Ghost therefore made the title of this Psalm, *Understanding,* because he foresaw that it would be wrongly understood by many. They say therefore, that justification consisteth in the remission of sins alone, not in the infusion of justice. For David saith absolutely, that they are *blessed,* that is just, *whose sins are forgiven.* They add moreover that this very remission of sins is not a true remission of sins, but an imputative one, by which namely God doth not take away sins, but covereth them, and imputeth them not. Lastly they gather from this place, that no satisfaction is necessary after the remission of the fault: because if God exacted a punishment, though temporal, from a man justified, he would not truly say that he doth not impute sin: for how doth he not impute, if he punisheth? But the holy Prophet, who put in the title, *The understanding of David,* understood altogether that the remission of sin is not wrought by God without the infusion of justice, and thereby he understood that by the grace of justification men become, from wicked, not only not wicked, but also truly just: for as the bodily sun doth not drive out darkness, unless it pour in light, so also the sun of justice, and Father of lights,[^4] doth not forgive sins but by the grace, or justice, which he poureth in. Wherefore St. Paul, *Rom.* iv, citing this place of the Psalm: "Blessed is the man to whom the Lord hath not imputed sin," speaketh thus: "As David also termeth the blessedness of a man, to whom God reputeth justice without works"; by which words the Apostle hath shown, that in the remission, or non-imputation of sin, justice is truly included. But as well the former error as the latter is yet more openly refuted, by the declaration of the words *to be covered,* and *not to be imputed.* For sins are said to be covered in this place, not because they are, and are not seen, but because they are abolished, and justice hath succeeded in their place: and therefore they are truly covered, and hidden even from the eyes of God, from whom nothing can be covered, or hidden, of the things that anywhere exist. Wherefore the same Prophet useth divers metaphors to signify the remission of sins, that what is wanting to one may be supplied by another; but that one is notable beyond the rest in *Ps.* l: "Thou shalt sprinkle me, O Lord, with hyssop, and I shall be cleansed; thou shalt wash me, and I shall be made whiter than snow."[^5] In which place the remission of sins is said not only to cover the stain, but truly to wash it away, and not only truly to wash it away, but to add brightness; nor only to take away blackness, but to add a whiteness so true, that it is said to surpass the whiteness of snow. Now what is it to take away the stain, and to add brightness, but to take away sin, and to pour in grace? what to remove blackness, and to put whiteness, but to remove injustice, and to give justice? Which same thing we read in chap. i of *Isaias*: "If your sins be as scarlet, they shall be made as white as snow: and if they be red as crimson, they shall be white as wool." Moreover the holy Fathers, Gregory Nazianzen, in the oration *On the holy laver;* Augustine, and Gregory the Pope in the commentary on this Psalm, to show that for sins to be covered is not for them to remain, and not to be seen, but to be wholly taken away, so that they are therefore not seen, because they are not; say that sins are covered by God in justification, as wounds are covered with a plaster by the physician, by which plaster they are so truly healed and taken away, that they nowhere appear. Origen also, Book IV *on the Epistle to the Romans,* and Theodoret on this place, say that by these words it is signified, that sin is so utterly rooted out, that not even a trace remaineth. But now as to the word of imputation, the adversaries are in error by the whole breadth of heaven. For that sins be not imputed is in the holy Scriptures, that they be not ascribed unto guilt, or not entered in the accounts, which in the vulgar tongue we say in Italian: *Non si mettere a conto* (Not to be put to account). *Wis.* xii: "Who shall accuse thee (*quis tibi imputabit*, who shall impute it to thee), if the nations perish, which thou hast made?" that is, who shall exact an account of thee, if all men perish? or who shall make thee guilty for this? And *Ezech.* xxxiii, of the penitent sinner God saith: "None of his sins, which he hath committed, shall be imputed to him," that is, he shall not render an account of them. II *Par.* xxx: "The Lord who is good will shew mercy to all them, who with their whole heart seek the Lord the God of their fathers: and will not impute it to them that they are not sanctified," that is, he will easily forgive, nor will he in truth exact an account of this, that they are less prepared. *Job* xlii: "That folly be not imputed to you." II *Tim.* iv: "All forsook me: may it not be laid to their charge" (*non illis imputetur*, may it not be imputed to them); and in the epistle *to Philemon*: "If he hath wronged thee in any thing, or is in thy debt, put that to my account (*hoc mihi imputa*), I will repay it," that is, ascribe it to my accounts, I will be thy debtor. But sin can be not imputed in two manners. First, if a man hath in truth not sinned, and therefore oweth nothing, and in this manner is understood that which we cited from the book of Wisdom: "Who shall accuse thee, if the nations perish, which thou hast made?" For even if all men should perish, they would not perish by the fault of God, therefore this could not be imputed to God. And in the same manner we expounded above the words of David: "Blessed is the man to whom the Lord hath not imputed sin," that is, who hath done no evil by his own will, for which he is guilty and a debtor before God. Secondly, if the sin hath been remitted and forgiven, so that there is now nothing to be imputed, and so are understood all the other sentences cited above; and so also many understand the place of the Psalm: "Blessed is the man to whom the Lord hath not imputed sin," that is, blessed is the man from whom God will not exact an account of sin, because he hath already remitted and forgiven it; and this exposition we do not reject, but we think the one above is to be preferred, because it is better continued with the words following: "There is no guile in his spirit." third manner of imputation, which the heretics have feigned, that for sin not to be imputed is that sin indeed cleaveth in the soul of him that sinneth, but is not reputed sin by God, can be proved by no example of Scripture, but rather is most openly disproved, since Scripture saith, both elsewhere, and in Psalm v: "Thou hatest all the workers of iniquity: thou wilt destroy all that speak a lie"; for if God hateth, and will destroy the unjust, certainly while iniquity remaineth in them, sin is imputed to them. And who would believe that God, a just judge, and who is no respecter of persons, doth not impute sin, but imputeth justice at that time, when a man is still wallowing in the filth of sin, and whatsoever he doth, according to the opinions of the Lutherans, is sin? Finely St. Justin Martyr in the dialogue *with Trypho,* refuting an error like the error of the Lutherans, saith: "Blessed is the man to whom the Lord shall not impute sin," that is, to whom, being penitent, God shall forgive sins, not as ye preach, deceiving your own selves, and as certain others like you affirm, that even if they be sinners, so that they know God, the Lord will not impute sins to them: thus he. This which we have said of the non-imputation of sin, can be shown from the contrary, of the imputation of justice. For that justice be imputed in the Scriptures, is not that a man be reputed just, when in truth he is not just; but it is that he be reputed just by the divine judgment, which cannot be deceived, who is most truly just. For that of *Gen.* xv: "Abraham believed God, and it was reputed to him unto justice," which is alleged by the apostle Paul, *Rom.* iv, and by the apostle James, chap. ii, signifieth nothing else, but that that act of believing was in Abraham a just work, and reputed for such by God. And that of *Ps.* cv: "Then Phinees stood up, and pacified him: and the slaughter ceased; and it was reputed to him unto justice, to generation and generation for evermore," what else doth it signify, but that that zeal of Phinees, with which he slew certain wicked men, was a most just work, and held for such by God, so that on that account he confirmed the priesthood to the sons and grandsons of Phinees for the longest times? To the same belongeth that which is in chap. iv *to the Romans*: "To him that worketh, the reward is not reckoned (*imputatur*) according to grace, but according to debt." For what is this, but that the reward is justly owed to him, who worketh a work worthy of reward? to be imputed therefore is not to be reputed as if it were, but to be reputed as it is. And that which the Apostle often repeateth in the same place, *that faith is reputed to justice,* doth not signify that faith is not justice, but is reputed so: but it signifieth that faith working by charity is most true justice, not gotten by works going before grace, but given and infused by God, and therefore is reputed and accepted by God for true justice. So therefore that sin be not imputed, doth not signify that sin remaineth, but is not punished: but it signifieth that there is nothing in the man justified, which can be reputed unto sin. From these things it is easily understood that what the Lutherans object concerning satisfaction can be solved in a single word: for if sin be not imputed, because a man hath lived innocently, it will be no wonder, if no satisfaction be required of him, to whom in this manner sin is not imputed: but if it is not imputed, because it hath been remitted by grace, then if it hath been remitted as to the fault and the eternal punishment, it will not be imputed unto fault and eternal punishment, but it will still be imputed unto temporal punishment: as we see was done in David, to whom it was said by the Prophet: "The Lord hath taken away thy sin from thee: thou shalt not die; but because thou hast made the enemies to blaspheme the name of the Lord, the child that is born to thee shall die."[^6] Where we see that the sin is not imputed as to his own death, and is imputed as to the death of his son; and that David was justified, and yet had to bear great grief for the death of his son, in punishment of the sin committed. But if at any time sin be remitted altogether as to all punishment, then also it will not be imputed unto any guilt or debt.

**3. BECAUSE I WAS SILENT MY BONES GREW OLD; WHILST I CRIED OUT ALL THE DAY LONG.**

Having set forth the beatitude of the justified, he bewaileth his own misery, as if he should say: They indeed are blessed; but I am wretched, who not only have not kept innocence, but for a long time put off seeking pardon by the confession of my sin; and therefore after my fault was acknowledged, I began so continually to cry out, and to waste myself with roaring, that my bones are enfeebled and worn, that is, all my strength is almost consumed. "Because I was silent"; in truth for a long time David was silent about the sin of adultery, nay he endeavoured by many arts to hide it; for first he tried in many ways to induce Urias to sleep with his own wife, that the son conceived of adultery might be thought to be Urias's own. And when Urias would not go to his own house, David added the crime of murder, that, Urias being taken away, and his widow straightway taken to wife, the child, which was looked for after nine months, might be believed to be David's, not as an adulterer, but as a husband. Nor yet, when that widow was taken to wife, did David straightway confess his sin, but waited till the son should be born. And not even then would he have begun to bewail his sin, unless he had been admonished by the prophet Nathan. And so for almost a whole year, and perhaps even longer, David lay in the mire of sin, and put off confession. Therefore he saith: "Because I was silent," and did not straightway confess my sin, but would hide and conceal it; therefore "my bones grew old; whilst I cried out all the day long," that is, after my sin was acknowledged I began to cry out all the day long for pardon, and so long, and with so great affection did I cry, that my bones are become weak and old. The Hebrew word בלו *balu* signifieth *inveteraverunt* (they grew old), and also *consumpta sunt* (they are consumed), or *attrita sunt* (they are worn), as St. Jerome rendereth it; therefore that word, *inveteraverunt,* is not to be referred to time, but to weakness, as that is taken of *Lam.* iii: "My skin

<!-- p. 187 (PDF 198) -->

he hath made old," that is worn, black, etc. That also, *whilst I cried out,* is in the Hebrew word for word, *whilst I roared.* From which we understand that that cry was a cry of grief and compunction, which can rightly be called a roaring; but not the cry of one exulting in sin, or proudly boasting of his sins, as some expound. Nor do we think that it was a roaring from bodily sickness, as others will have it: for Scripture doth not declare that David was sick at that time; and to affirm any thing without the testimony of Scripture, is to divine; but the roaring of penance, as with Theodoret we have expounded.

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. FOR DAY AND NIGHT THY HAND WAS HEAVY UPON ME: I AM TURNED IN MY ANGUISH, WHILST THE THORN IS FASTENED.**

Many evils befell David because of his sin: for the son born of adultery died an infant, and his daughter named Thamar was defiled by her brother Amon through incest, and the same Amon was slain by his brother Absalom, and Absalom, fighting against his father, was himself also slain: all which brought the greatest griefs to David, as the history of Book II of *Kings* relateth. Of these divine scourges therefore he saith: "For day and night thy hand was heavy upon me," that is, continually, without ceasing, thou ceasest not to redouble scourges upon me: for he is said to make his hand heavy upon another, who striketh him not lightly, but heavily. "I am turned in my anguish, whilst the thorn is fastened," that is, because of scourges of this kind I am turned to the thought of the grievousness of my sin in my anguish, whilst the thorn of tribulation is fastened in me, that is, tribulation and anguish have opened the eyes of the mind, which the allurement and delight of the flesh had shut. By the thorn there could also be understood with St. Jerome the sting of conscience, so that the sense is, *I am turned in my anguish,* whilst the sting of conscience pricketh. In the Hebrew and the Greek there is added *mihi* (in me), *whilst the thorn is fastened in me;* therefore in the Latin it ought to be understood. In the Hebrew there are other words and another sense, *conversus est viror meus in siccitates æstatis* (my greenness is turned into the droughts of summer), that is, all my good things have perished, as greenness and moisture in summer is dried up by the heat of the sun and perisheth. But the Seventy read otherwise than it now is in the Hebrew copies; for instead of נהפך לשדי, *conversus est viror meus* (my greenness is turned), they read נהפכתי לשדי, which is, *conversus sum in ærumna mea* (I am turned in my anguish), and so also read St. Jerome, who rendereth from the Hebrew, *versatus sum in miseria mea* (I have been tossed in my misery). But that which followeth, בחרבוני קיץ, *in siccitates æstatis* (into the droughts of summer), the Seventy read בחרבני קוץ, *dum adurit me spina* (whilst the thorn burneth me), which is the same with that which we have, *dum configitur spina* (whilst the thorn is fastened); for whilst the thorn is fastened, certainly it burneth and wasteth, which the verb חרב *charab* signifieth. And so by the least change of points, and of one or two like letters, we see that the reading can be found, which the Seventy Interpreters had; and that the reading, which was in the copies of the Seventy Interpreters, is truer than that which now is in our copies, to which the Rabbins have added points, we cannot doubt.

**5. I HAVE ACKNOWLEDGED MY SIN TO THEE, AND MY INJUSTICE I HAVE NOT CONCEALED.**

From his turning to know the greatness of his sin, of which he spoke in the verse above, it came to pass that he was no longer silent about his sin, nor hid it, but openly confessed it to God and to men. In the Hebrew it is in the future אדיעך, *cognitum faciam* (I will make known); but the future is put for the past, of which tense is the verb that followeth, כסיתי *kissithi*, *abscondi* (I have concealed). In the Greek there is not *tibi* (to thee), but it is in the Hebrew, and in the Greek it is understood. Now that, *cognitum tibi feci* (I have made known to thee), doth not signify that it was unknown to God, before David confessed it, but he speaketh after the manner of men. For a judge, even if he know the offence of the accused, because perhaps he himself saw it committed; yet is not said to know it judicially, unless the accused be convicted by the testimony of several, or the accused himself openly confess, being perhaps driven to confession by torments. So therefore God had indeed seen David sinning, but he willed that he should openly confess; therefore he applied scourges as it were the question. Then indeed David began to confess, nor did he only say, *I have sinned against the Lord,* which before the scourges he said privately to Nathan the prophet; but he publicly made his sin known to the Lord, that is, he confessed judicially, and by the writing of the Psalms he manifested his sin to the whole world; therefore he most truly addeth: "And my injustice I have not concealed."

**6. I SAID I WILL CONFESS AGAINST MYSELF MY INJUSTICE TO THE LORD: AND THOU HAST FORGIVEN THE WICKEDNESS OF MY SIN.**

For the comfort of penitents he declareth the unspeakable mercy of God, which he himself experienced in himself. For although God, "who is light, and in him there is no darkness," I *John* ii,[^7] pursueth with the utmost hatred the darkness of sins, and unless the sinner repent, is ready to cast him into the exterior darkness unto the utmost and everlasting torment: yet he is so inclined to forgive, if a man do penance from the heart, that he preventeth even the confession itself by having mercy and sparing. He seemeth to look back to that time, when Nathan the prophet in the name of God upbraided him with his sin; and he, being straightway pricked with compunction, said, II *Kings* xi:[^8] "I have sinned against the Lord," and Nathan added: "The Lord also hath taken away thy sin: thou shalt not die." For seeing so sudden a pardon, he thought what was true, that the remission of the sin was before the confession itself, but not before the inward contrition, in which there was together hatred of sin, love of God, the purpose of confessing and of making satisfaction: "I said, I will confess," that is, being pricked in heart I resolved straightway to confess "against myself my iniquity," and openly

<!-- p. 188 (PDF 199) -->

to acknowledge myself guilty and a sinner; and thou didst not wait for that word, by which I said: "I have sinned against the Lord," but of thy fatherly and unspeakable kindness, "thou hast forgiven the wickedness of my sin": which I learned from thy prophet Nathan, who saith: "The Lord hath taken away thy sin from thee." In the Hebrew there is עון חטאתי, *iniquitatem peccati mei* (the iniquity of my sin); in the Greek, *impietatem cordis mei* (the wickedness of my heart), and so the ancients read in their Commentaries, Augustine, Theodoret, Euthymius. The Latin Interpreter seemeth to have taken *impietatem* (the wickedness) from the Greek, τὴν ἀσέβειαν τῆς καρδίας μου, and *peccati mei* (of my sin) from the Hebrew. But all come to the same: for it is called wickedness of the heart, because it is conceived in the heart; it is called wickedness of sin, because it is committed by sinning. The same sin of David also was both iniquity in respect of Urias, and wickedness in respect of God.

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. FOR THIS SHALL EVERY ONE THAT IS HOLY PRAY TO THEE IN A SEASONABLE TIME.**

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. AND YET IN A FLOOD OF MANY WATERS, THEY SHALL NOT COME NIGH UNTO HIM.**

The Prophet saith that by his example there will be many, who shall learn to pray to God for the pardon of sins, that in this manner they may be delivered from the great evils in which sinners are one day to be wrapped. "For this" (*pro hac*): the Greek interpreters, Theodoret and Euthymius, and with them St. Augustine expound, *pro hac impietate* (for this wickedness), because *impietas* in Greek and in Latin is of the feminine gender; but the Hebrew word is of the masculine gender, and that, *pro hac,* doth not agree with it, which in the Hebrew also is of the feminine gender, על זאת; therefore we are compelled to say that "for this" signifieth, *pro hac re* (for this thing), or *pro hoc* (for this): for the Hebrews use the feminine gender for the neuter. And it could also be rightly rendered, *propter hoc* (because of this), or *super hoc* (upon this). And so the sense will be: For this cause, that is, because thou so mercifully forgivest the penitent, "shall pray to thee," that is, shall take confidence to pray to thee, "every one that is holy," that is every godly man (*pius*), every one truly penitent, who, beginning to hate his own wickedness (*impietatem*), beginneth to put on thy godliness (*pietatem*). And he shall pray "in a seasonable time," that is, before the time of mercy be ended, while this life lasteth, while God inviteth to penance. "Seek ye God," saith Isaias, "while he may be found: call upon him, while he is near," *Isa.* xxxv.[^9] That which followeth hath a twofold sense: one is: "Every one that is holy shall pray to thee in a seasonable time," that *in a flood of many waters,* those waters *may not come nigh unto him,* that is, that in the last day, when all kinds of punishments shall overflow upon the wicked as it were a certain flood, and the seasonable time of prayer and penance shall have passed, then they may be safe from punishments of that kind. This sense is most clear in the Hebrew; for thus St. Jerome rendereth word for word, *pro hac orabit ad te omnis misericors tempus inveniens, ut cum inundaverint aquæ multæ, ad illum non accedant* (for this shall every merciful man pray to thee, finding the time, that when many waters shall overflow, they may not come unto him). And this sense Theodoret followeth, although he expoundeth a little otherwise, namely thus: *For this shall every one that is holy pray to thee, in a seasonable time, that when the waters of many tribulations shall overflow, they may not come unto him,* that is, may not trouble him, but that he may be above them all, and may bear them undismayed. The other sense is: *Every one that is holy shall pray to thee in a seasonable time,* and he will do rightly and wisely; for *in a flood of many waters, when evils shall overflow upon all the wicked in the last day, as the waters of the flood overflowed in the times of Noe,* then the wicked *shall not come nigh unto him,* that is unto God: for there will be no longer a seasonable time; therefore Isaias saith: "Call upon him, while he is near," for one day he will be far off, so that ye cannot come nigh unto him. So St. Augustine and others expound. Both senses are true, but the former seemeth more literal; for that, *they shall not come nigh unto him,* seemeth not able according to the letter to be referred to God, since no mention of God hath been made in the words going before. And so that word, *verumtamen* (and yet), is to be taken for a copulative particle, as Euthymius rightly admonisheth; and the Hebrew word רק signifieth not only *verumtamen,* but also *certe* (certainly), *vere* (truly), etc.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. THOU ART MY REFUGE FROM THE TROUBLE WHICH HATH ENCOMPASSED ME: MY JOY, DELIVER ME FROM THEM THAT SURROUND ME.**

Having received pardon as to the fault, he asketh also pardon as to the punishment, namely deliverance from the tribulation, into which he fell because of his sin. Now he seemeth to speak of the persecution, which he suffered from Absalom his son, of which he spoke in the whole Psalm above. Perhaps also he speaketh of the temptations of the devils, which perpetually encompass and afflict us. "Thou art my refuge from the trouble which hath encompassed me." As if he should say: I have no safe refuge, but in thy mercy, since on every side friends fail, and on every side I am girt about by enemies: therefore thou who alone art "my joy,"[^10] that is, the cause of all my gladness, deliver me from the enemies[^11] that surround me. In the Hebrew there is: *Thou art my refuge from straitness, or from the enemy, thou wilt keep me: with the rejoicings of delivering thou wilt encompass me.* But it manifestly appeareth that the points of the Rabbins, and some change of letters, have disturbed this whole place. The Seventy Interpreters did not read תצרני, *custodies me* (thou wilt keep me), from צר, but יצרני, *obsidebit me* (shall besiege me), or *circumdabit me* (shall encompass me), from צור, and the future is put for the past; therefore they rendered, *quæ circumdedit me* (which hath encompassed me). Nor did they read רני פלט *ronne pallet*, *exultationibus liberandi* (with the rejoicings of delivering), but *ronni pallet*, *exultatio mea libera* (my joy, set free), or *erue* (deliver). St. Jerome also rendereth from the Hebrew, *laus mea* (my praise); whence he read *ronni*, not *ronne*, as it is to-day in the Hebrew Bibles. Lastly the Seventy did not read תסובבני *thesobebeni*, *circumdabis me* (thou wilt encompass me), but מסובבני *misobebeni*, *a circumdante me* (from him that encompasseth me).

<!-- p. 189 (PDF 200) -->

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. I WILL GIVE THEE UNDERSTANDING, AND I WILL INSTRUCT THEE IN THIS WAY, IN WHICH THOU SHALT GO: I WILL FIX MY EYES UPON THEE.**

The Lord answereth the prayer of his servant, and promiseth the help which was asked. He promiseth three things: *first,* inward prudence, that he may be able to beware of the ambushes of enemies, and to discern friends from enemies, when he saith: "I will give thee understanding," that is, I will make thee understanding and prudent. *Secondly,* the outward help of the singular providence of God, without which even the most prudent men are in many dangers, and this that signifieth, *I will instruct thee in this way, in which thou shalt go:* for in the Hebrew it is, *I will show thee the way by which thou mayest walk;* but in the Greek, *I will accompany thee in the way,* or *I will go together with thee.* From all which we understand that that, *I will instruct thee in the way,* signifieth direction and guidance in all actions. For God is said to show the way, and to accompany us, as the guide of the journey, when by the help of his grace he bringeth it about, that we err not in the way of conduct, and fall not into the dangers and ambushes of enemies, chiefly of the invisible ones. *Thirdly,* perseverance in grace, which is the greatest benefit and proper to the elect of God: "I will fix," saith he, "my eyes upon thee," I will not turn away my eyes from thee, but perseveringly and steadfastly I will look upon thee with the eye of kindness; therefore the inward help of prudence, and the outward help of providence, will always be with thee. In the Hebrew there is, *deliberabo super te oculo meo* (I will deliberate upon thee with my eye), or, as St. Jerome rendereth, *cogitabo de te oculo meo* (I will think of thee with my eye), but the change of one point can make of *oculo meo* (with my eye) *oculis meis* (with my eyes), or *oculos meos* (my eyes), and so in that word there is no difficulty. But I suspect that for the word איעצה, *cogitabo* (I will think), or *deliberabo* (I will deliberate), the Seventy read אמצה, *firmabo* (I will make firm), or *roborabo* (I will strengthen): for how can it be that most learned men by the verb יעץ, which signifieth *to deliberate, to take counsel, to think,* understood *to make firm,* and *to strengthen?* Yet if the Hebrew reading, as it now standeth, is to be reconciled with the Greek and the Latin, it will be needful to understand the word *semper* (always), so that the sense is, *I will think always of thee with the eye of my mind;* which is the same with that, *I will fix my eyes upon thee.*

[Margin: Verse 11.]

**11. DO NOT BECOME LIKE THE HORSE AND THE MULE, WHO HAVE NO UNDERSTANDING.**

Now at last the Prophet exhorteth all the faithful, as well the good as the evil, to learn from his example the evils, which follow sin, and the good things which follow penance and virtue; for he himself had experienced all. And first, turning to the evil, he saith: "Do not become like the horse and the mule, who have no understanding," that is, be not led by the senses alone, since ye are endowed with reason: do not like horses and mules neigh after the wives of your neighbours, as I did: do not like untamed and fierce horses or mules assail the poor and lowly with biting and with heels, as I did against Urias.

[Margin: Verse 12.]

**12. WITH BIT AND BRIDLE BIND FAST THEIR JAWS, WHO COME NOT NEAR UNTO THEE.**

He foretelleth the evils, which shall follow them, who will imitate horses and mules; for they shall be compelled by tribulations either to be converted to God, or at least they shall be hindered from hurting others as much as they would. But after the prophetical manner David expresseth this prediction by way of imprecation: "With bit," saith he, "and bridle bind fast their jaws, who come not near unto thee," that is, with halter and bridle thou wilt restrain as horses and mules them, who refuse, as men endowed with reason, to come to thee of their own accord by obedience. Ridiculously doth Calvin contend that it is not to be read, *in camo* (with the bit), but *in lupato* (with the curb), as if there were much difference between these words, and as if he were wiser than the Seventy Interpreters, who rendered ἐν κημῷ, whence is our *in camo;* and than St. Jerome, who rendereth from the Hebrew, *in camo;* and than St. Augustine, Theodoret and the other ancients, who all read *in camo.* Now *camus* signifieth a rope, or halter, with which horses are tied. But in this place *camus* (bit) and *frenum* (bridle) are metaphorical words, and are put to signify the calamities and miseries, which God useth to restrain men from sins, which is expounded in the verse following. In the Hebrew it is in the singular, *maxillam ejus* (his jaw); but not only the Seventy, but also St. Jerome rendereth in the plural number, *maxillas eorum* (their jaws). From which we understand that the points have not been everywhere rightly placed in our Bibles: for according to other points, the same word signifieth *maxillas eorum.*

[Margin: Calvin finding fault with our version.]

[Margin: Verse 13.]

**13. MANY ARE THE SCOURGES OF THE SINNER, BUT MERCY SHALL ENCOMPASS HIM THAT HOPETH IN THE LORD.**

This is the declaration of the bit and bridle, as if the Prophet should say: The impenitent sinner, and he who is well pleased with himself in sin, shall be worn with many scourges, both in this life, and in that to come. For although sinners sometimes seem to flourish, yet sin itself is a most grievous punishment, which bringeth forth other punishments without number, cares, anxieties, fears, dangers, remorse of conscience, and the like: nor content with these doth God the just judge add many other scourges, and unless the sinner repent, and pray to God in a seasonable time, without doubt he shall come to everlasting scourges. On the contrary, the just man, who hopeth in the Lord, and trusteth not in the vanity of mortals, shall be encompassed on every side by the divine mercy itself, so that from no part can the scourge come unto him. Now the divine mercy is the fountain of all good things; therefore when it is said: "Mercy shall encompass him that hopeth in the Lord," there is signified the heap of all good things which they most abundantly enjoy, who cleave to God alone. For, as we have often said, what can hurt him, to whom all things work together unto good, and all whose sufferings are turned into glory? Therefore the Psalm is rightly concluded with those words:

[Margin: The divine mercy is the fountain of all good things.]

[Margin: Verse 14.]

**14. BE GLAD IN THE LORD, AND REJOICE, YE JUST, AND GLORY, ALL YE RIGHT OF HEART.**

As in the beginning he called the just blessed, so now in the end he exhorteth the same to gladness, which is also as it were indirectly an exhortation to persevere in justice, that they may be able to persevere in gladness. "Be glad in the Lord, and rejoice, ye just," that is, ye have, O ye just, great matter of joy and rejoicing, but "in the Lord," who is to you the cause of so great good things, with which ye abound: there is no cause why ye should be sad for temporal losses, for which there shall be repaid to you in eternity good measure and pressed down and shaken together and running over, and here in the mean time spiritual consolation shall not forsake you. "And glory, all ye right of heart"; the same is repeated in other words. For to glory, in this place, is not to lift up oneself with praises, as the Latin word soundeth, but it is to shout for joy, as the Hebrew word והרנינו *vcharninu* soundeth, that is, to praise God with gladness, and more than once in the Scriptures *gloriari* (to glory) is taken for *lætari* (to be glad) and *exultare* (to rejoice), as when the Apostle saith, *Rom.* v: "But we glory also in tribulations." The verb which in the Hebrew properly signifieth *to glory,* is found in *Ps.* li, in the beginning: "Why dost thou glory in malice?" But in this place it is not the same verb, but another, which signifieth *to shout for joy.* By *the right of heart* we understand the just; for from rightness of heart ariseth rightness of words and of works: and they are just, whose hearts, voices and works are conformed to the most right rule, which is the divine law: from which rightness it cometh that God pleaseth man, and man pleaseth God; and that whatsoever at length befalleth a man, God willing or permitting it, is received with an even mind; and that so there is in the heart not only justice, but also "peace, and joy in the Holy Ghost," that is the kingdom of God, as the Apostle interpreteth, *Rom.* xiv; therefore most rightly David saith: "Blessed are they whose iniquities are forgiven," etc.; and: "Be glad in the Lord, and rejoice, ye just," etc., because the just alone are blessed, and enjoy true and solid joy.
