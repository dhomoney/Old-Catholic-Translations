BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XII.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*Unto the end, a psalm for David.*[^1]

Of this title nothing occurreth to be said. The argument of the Psalm is a prayer to God in the time of temptation.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. HOW LONG, O LORD, WILT THOU FORGET ME UNTO THE END? HOW LONG DOST THOU TURN AWAY THY FACE FROM ME?**

[Margin: He that is tempted is wont to suffer two evils.]

He that is tempted is wont to suffer two evils, depraved desires in the will, and darkness in the understanding. When depraved desires press hard, then God seemeth to forget, and to forsake the soul; when darkness obscureth the understanding, God seemeth to turn away his face from the soul. For he is light, and therefore he enlighteneth when he turneth his face toward us; he leaveth in darkness when he turneth it away. On account of the first, the man placed in temptation saith: "How long, O Lord, wilt thou forget me unto the end?" On account of the second he saith: "How long dost thou turn away thy face from me?"

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. HOW LONG SHALL I TAKE COUNSELS IN MY SOUL, SORROW IN MY HEART ALL THE DAY?**

The order being now inverted, he complaineth first of the darkness which he suffereth; secondly, of the depraved desires which he endureth against his will: "How long," saith he, on account of the obscuring of the understanding, "shall I take counsels in my soul?" that is, shall I devise various counsels, that I may be able to be delivered from the present evil? How long again, on account of the desires that infest my heart, "shall I take sorrow in my heart?" that is, shall I take up sorrow, and be sad, lest perchance I offend my God; and shall I do this all the day, that is, the whole day without any intermission. In the Greek there is added καὶ νυκτός, *et per noctem* (and by night). But this is not found in the Hebrew, nor is it read by St. Augustine; therefore it is rightly omitted in the Latin edition.

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. HOW LONG SHALL MY ENEMY BE EXALTED OVER ME? CONSIDER, AND HEAR ME, O LORD MY GOD.**

The first part of this verse embraceth both evils; for the enemy is then exalted over a man, when he oppresseth him, both by thrusting in depraved desires, which the man cannot shake off; and by pouring darkness over him, which he cannot scatter. Therefore, as one suffering violence, he crieth to God: "How long shall my enemy be exalted over me?" Then he calleth for help against both evils: "Consider" (*Respice*, look upon me), saith he, that is, turn thy face, "and hear me," that is, forget not, forsake not, help me, being entreated.

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. ENLIGHTEN MY EYES THAT I NEVER SLEEP IN DEATH: LEST AT ANY TIME MY ENEMY SAY: I HAVE PREVAILED AGAINST HIM.**

Again he repeateth the same prayer more clearly: "Enlighten," drive away the darkness of the mind by turning thy face to me, and looking upon me; "that I never sleep in death," that is, lest consenting to the desires I fall into the death of the soul. Scripture in many places calleth death itself, whether bodily or spiritual, a sleep: because it is as easy for God to recall a dead man to life, as it is easy for us to rouse a sleeping man from sleep. Moreover, that

<!-- p. 67 (PDF 78) -->

phrase, *that I never sleep*, signifieth that a man, by yielding to temptation, as it were falleth asleep, and no longer feeleth the torment from the temptation; but because that rest is not healthful, but deadly, therefore there is added, *in death*. And so, since a man is delivered from the trouble of temptation in two ways, either by the grace of God, the tempter being put to flight, or by consent to sin, the lust being satisfied: he asketh to be delivered in the first way, lest perchance to his own very great loss he be delivered in the second way, namely, by falling asleep through consent to sin. He addeth then the reason why he desireth to be delivered from temptation, saying: "Lest at any time my enemy say: I have prevailed against him," that is, lest the devil so glory that the servant of God hath been overcome by him, which would also turn to the injury of God.

[Margin: In two ways is a man delivered from temptation.]

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. THEY THAT TROUBLE ME WILL REJOICE WHEN I AM MOVED: BUT I HAVE TRUSTED IN THY MERCY.**

He explaineth the reason given above, as if he said: Not without cause did I say: "Lest at any time my enemy say," etc., for "they that trouble me will rejoice when I am moved," that is, not only if I shall have wholly yielded as one overcome, but also if I shall have begun to waver for a little: for as there is greater "joy in heaven upon one sinner that doth penance, than upon ninety-nine just who need not penance," *Luke* xv; so also the devils rejoice more, if they have led some perfect man to begin a sin, than if they have made many sinners wallow in the greatest sins. There is an example in St. Gregory, Book IV of the *Dialogues*, chap. vii. And hence it appeareth that this Psalm was written for the deliverance of the just man from the temptation of the devil, not, as some will have it, for the deliverance of David from the persecution of Saul. For David in that persecution changed his place daily. Therefore he would not have said: "If I am moved, my enemies will rejoice," as though he held it a great matter not to be moved. That which followeth: "But I have trusted in thy mercy," is another reason why it is fitting that he be helped by God, namely, because he resisteth the tempter relying on the mercy of God, and not on his own strength.

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. MY HEART SHALL REJOICE IN THY SALVATION: I WILL SING TO THE LORD, WHO GIVETH ME GOOD THINGS: YEA I WILL SING TO THE NAME OF THE LORD THE MOST HIGH.**

This is the last reason by which he endeavoureth to move God to succour him, namely, because, delivered from temptation, he will not be ungrateful to his deliverer, but will celebrate the benefit with heart, mouth and work. To the heart belong those first words: "My heart shall rejoice in thy salvation," that is, my heart shall leap for gladness when it hath obtained salvation, acknowledging it as from thee, and praising thee for it. To the mouth belong the words following: "I will sing to the Lord, who giveth me good things." In the Hebrew there is not *bona* (good things), but only the verb *tribuit* (giveth), or rather *retribuit* (rendereth). But the Seventy declared that *retribuit* by rendering *benefecit mihi* (hath done good to me); and the Latin Interpreter yet more clearly, *qui bona retribuit mihi* (who rendereth me good things); but the sense is the same: for God is wont to render protection to trust; therefore David said in the Hebrew word, *retribuit mihi* (hath rendered to me), namely protection to one trusting in him; and because deliverance from temptation is deliverance from the danger of the truest and greatest evil, therefore the Greek and the Latin rightly added the word *bona* (good things), namely of the true and greatest good. Lastly, to works belong the last words, "yea I will sing (*psallam*)[^2] to the name of the Lord the most high"; for *psallere* is properly to strike the harp with the hand. "David," saith the Scripture, "played with his hand," I *Kings* xviii, 10; and elsewhere: "On the psaltery and an instrument of ten strings I will sing praises to thee," *Ps.* cxliii, 9. And so he promiseth that he will rejoice with the heart, sing with the mouth, and strike an instrument of music with the hand, that the whole man may serve the praises of God. Now to play to the name of the Lord is to play unto the glory of the Lord, that is, to celebrate his praises. In the Hebrew these last words, "yea I will sing to the name of the Lord the most high," are not found; but perhaps they fell out through length of time. Certainly they cohere very well with what goeth before, and they are in the Greek copies, and are cited and explained by the Fathers, Jerome and Augustine, Theodoret and Euthymius. Mystically, to play to the name of the Lord is to do works in harmony with the heart and the words, "that men, seeing our good works, may glorify our Father who is in heaven," *Matt.* v, 16.

[^1]: Translator's note: Bellarmine does not number the title, and divides the Psalm into six verses. The Douay-Rheims Bible also has six, but divides otherwise: its verse 1 holds the title and his verse 1; its 2 is his 2; its 3 is the first half of his 3; its 4 runs from "Consider" to "in death"; its 5 from "Lest at any time" to "when I am moved"; and its 6 from "But I have trusted" to the end.

[^2]: Translator's note: the Vulgate has two verbs in this verse, *cantabo* and *psallam*, and the Douay-Rheims renders both "I will sing". Bellarmine's comment turns on *psallere*, to play upon a stringed instrument; it is rendered "to play" in his explanation. In the two texts he cites, the same verb is "played" and "I will sing praises" in the Douay-Rheims.
