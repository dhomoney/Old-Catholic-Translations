BELLARMINE'S EXPLANATION.

PSALM XLIII.

## TITLE AND ARGUMENT.

*Unto the end, for the sons of Core, to give understanding.*

[Margin: To whom doth this Psalm pertain?]

The title of this Psalm differeth nothing from the title of Psalm XL,[^1] except that in that the word *intellectus* (understanding) was put first, here it is put after. There it was *intellectus filiis Core* (understanding for the sons of Core), here it is *filiis Core intellectus* (for the sons of Core, understanding), as it is in the Hebrew, or *ad intellectum* (unto understanding), as it is in the Greek and Latin. And perhaps there is no mystery in this transposition. Perhaps also the word *intellectus* put first signified that in that Psalm calamities past or present were bewailed; but in this it is put after, because calamities are bewailed that were to come long after the times of David: for the holy Fathers agree that this Psalm containeth a prediction of things which happened after the times of David; and the Greeks indeed, as Chrysostom, Theodoret and Euthymius, teach that this Psalm is sung in the person of the Machabees, who suffered most grievous calamities from Antiochus and other kings of Syria or Egypt. But the Latins, as Ambrose, Jerome and Augustine, will have it that in this Psalm are expressed the voices of the holy Martyrs, who endured most cruel persecutions from the Roman Emperors. But these opinions of the Fathers can be reconciled, if we say that the present Psalm pertaineth to both, to the Machabees and to the Christians: for both suffered like persecutions, and for the same cause, namely for the confession and the law of the same true God. For as this Psalm pertaineth not only to the martyrs who suffered in the time of Nero, but also to those who suffered in the time of Diocletian, who was more than two hundred years later than Nero: so also it can pertain to the martyrs who strove under Antiochus Epiphanes, who went before Nero by about two hundred years.

## EXPLANATION OF THE PSALM.

[Margin: Verse 1.]

**1. WE HAVE HEARD, O GOD, WITH OUR EARS: OUR FATHERS HAVE DECLARED TO US,**[^2]

[Margin: Verse 2.]

**2. THE WORK, THOU HAST WROUGHT IN THEIR DAYS, AND IN THE DAYS OF OLD.**

<!-- p. 283 (PDF 294) -->

[Margin: God is not changed, but changeth the medicine according to the time.]

The people of God, placed in persecution, and groaning for affliction, beginneth to recount the great works which God once did for the defence of his faithful, and to wonder why he now seemeth to have forsaken the same his faithful, that by this recounting and wonder it may bend God to mercy. But David therefore premised in the title that there is need of understanding here, because God is not changed, but changeth the medicine according to the time, as he judgeth to be fitting for his sick people. O God, saith the Prophet in the person of the Church, or of the martyrs of both Testaments, "we have heard with our ears." Now he saith *with our ears,* when it might have seemed enough to say *we have heard,* or *we have heard with ears,* for the greater expression of certainty: as St. John writeth in the beginning of the first epistle: "Which we have seen with our eyes, and our hands have handled;" it seemed to have been enough to say, *which we have seen, and handled;* but that, *with our eyes, with our hands,* hath great force to signify assured certainty. So therefore in this place they say: "We have heard with our ears," that is, we ourselves have heard. They add straightway whence they heard; and say: "Our fathers have declared to us," that is, we received it not by uncertain rumour from unknown men, but our fathers themselves, men most worthy of credit, who would never have deceived us their sons, they, I say, "have declared to us." And what they heard from the fathers they explain, when they add: "The work, thou hast wrought in their days, and in the days of old," that is, our fathers declaring it, we have heard the wonderful work which thou didst in their days, they themselves, that is, seeing it; and also "the work which thou didst in the days of old," which our fathers themselves heard from their fathers relating it. It mattereth not much that in the Hebrew there is no *et* (and), whence some expound: In their days, which are the days of old.

[Margin: Verse 3.]

**3. THY HAND DESTROYED THE GENTILES, AND THOU PLANTEDST THEM: THOU DIDST AFFLICT THE PEOPLE AND CAST THEM OUT.**

He explaineth in particular one of the wonderful works which God did for his faithful in the days of the fathers, and saith: "Thy hand destroyed the Gentiles," that is, thy power destroyed out of the land of promise the nations of the Chanaanites and Jebusites, which dwelt in it; "and thou plantedst them," that is, and those nations being cast out, thou plantedst our fathers in that very land. "Thou didst afflict the people and cast them out," that is, thy hand destroyed them, because thou didst afflict the peoples of those nations with most grievous battles, and at length, when they were overcome, didst cast them out, that they might give place to thy people. From this place we understand that what was said above: "Our fathers have declared to us the work, thou hast wrought in their days," is not to be taken of an immediate relation, but of a mediate, that is, by means of the succession of sons: for what is related in this place was done in the time of Moses and Josue; now Moses, or Josue, could not relate it by themselves to the Machabees, or to the Apostles, nor to David, who wrote the Psalm, but they related it by their successors. Now that, *thou plantedst them,* containeth a notable metaphor; for it signifieth that the Hebrews were so settled and established in the land of promise, that they could not be driven out but with difficulty, as trees are set in the ground, when they are planted, and firmly rooted, so that they cannot easily be plucked up. Moreover the things that are planted not only stand firm, but also grow, and are multiplied; which metaphor Psalm LXXIX explaineth more clearly: "Thou plantedst the roots thereof, and it filled the land. The shadow of it covered the hills: and the branches thereof the cedars of God."

[Margin: Verse 4.]

**4. FOR THEY GOT NOT THE POSSESSION OF THE LAND BY THEIR OWN SWORD: NEITHER DID THEIR OWN ARM SAVE THEM.**

[Margin: Verse 5.]

**5. BUT THY RIGHT HAND AND THY ARM, AND THE LIGHT OF THY COUNTENANCE: BECAUSE THOU WAST PLEASED WITH THEM.**

[Margin: How is God said to cause the light of his countenance to shine upon any man?]

He proveth what he said: "Thy hand destroyed them," etc. For, saith he, "they got not the possession of the land by their own sword," that is, our fathers, in the time of Moses and Josue, fought indeed with the Chanaanites; but unless thou hadst been their helper and protector, they would neither have possessed the land, nor have been able to escape out of the hands of their enemies: for they were fewer, less exercised in war, and were to fight against most strongly fortified cities. Not therefore by their own sword, that is, by the power of their arms, did they possess the land of promise. "Neither did their own arm save them," that is, neither was their might so great that in battle they could escape captivity, or death; "but thy right hand and thy arm, and the light of thy countenance," gave them the land to be possessed, and saved them from death, or captivity, when they fought with the Chanaanites: and this for that reason, "because thou wast pleased with them," that is, not for their virtues, or merits, but because it pleased thee to choose them for thy people. That, *the light of thy countenance,* signifieth properly *a kindly look,* or grace and favour, as we said in the foregoing Psalm, verse 3. For God is said *to cause the light of his countenance to shine upon* a man, when he vouchsafeth to look upon him with a serene countenance, that is, when he hath mercy on him, and imparteth his grace to him. Now that the things which are said in this place are most true is manifest from the book of Josue, chap. vi, where we read that the walls of the city of Jericho fell utterly down at the mere shout of the children of Israel, and from chapter x, where we read that, when Josue was fighting, God sent hailstones from heaven upon the enemies, and Scripture saith: "And many more were killed with the hailstones than were slain by the swords of the children of Israel."

[Margin: Verse 6.]

**6. THOU ART THYSELF MY KING AND MY GOD, WHO COMMANDEST THE SAVING OF JACOB.**

Now the people

<!-- p. 284 (PDF 295) -->

[Margin: God is not only the king, but also the judge of his people.]

of God expoundeth its wonder, saying: "Thou art thyself my King and my God, who commandest the saving of Jacob," that is, thou thyself, who didst help and protect our fathers, because thou wast their King and God, thou, I say, "art thyself also my King and my God;" and that same, "who commandest the saving of Jacob," that is, who art wont to save thy people, which from its parent is named Jacob. In this place some will have that *et* (and) to be superfluous, when it is said, *et Deus meus* (and my God), and they expound, *tu es Rex meus, Deus meus* (thou art my King, O my God).[^3] Their reason is, that that *et* is not found in the Hebrew text. But the Seventy Interpreters have *et,* and so read and expound all the ancient Greeks and Latins, Ambrose, Jerome, Augustine, Chrysostom, Theodoret, Euthymius, and the sense is more sublime: for Scripture signifieth that God is not only the king of his people, but also God, that is, the judge of the whole world, eternal and almighty, who can not only protect his people, whom he ruleth, but also punish all its adversaries. Therefore that *et* is to be retained; and it is to be believed either that it hath fallen out of the Hebrew text, or that the Seventy elders added it, that they might declare that the word *Deus* (God) is not of the vocative case, but of the nominative, and that something more is signified by that name than by the name of king. It is also to be observed in this place that *qui mandas salutes Jacob* doth not signify *qui mittis salutes* (who sendest salvations), but *qui præcipis,* or *jubes* (who commandest, or biddest): for the Hebrew and Greek word signify this, and from them the ambiguity of the Latin word is to be cleared. And so the sense is, "who commandest," that is, who biddest and commandest thy people to be saved, that is, who by command and nod alone canst, and art wont to, give salvation to thy people, as if he should say: "Thou art my King," but not as other kings, who protect by warring; but thou art also God, who by word alone, by command alone, by nod alone art able to save Jacob.

[Margin: Verse 7.]

**7. THROUGH THEE WE WILL PUSH DOWN OUR ENEMIES WITH THE HORN: AND THROUGH THY NAME WE WILL DESPISE THEM THAT RISE UP AGAINST US.**

He now showeth that not only is God the same God who was in the time of the fathers, but also that the people is the same, and hath the same hope in God which the fathers had: for hence ariseth the wonder, why the same God dealeth with the same people in so contrary a manner, that whom before he made victor without any trouble, he now suffereth to be overcome and vanquished. "Through thee, saith he, we will push down our enemies with the horn," that is, we also, if thou be our helper and protector, shall overcome our enemies through thee, that is, trusting in thy help. "And through thy name we will despise them that rise up against us," that is, and thy name being called upon, we shall not fear, nor make much of the assault of the enemies rising up against us. That, *ventilabimus cornu* (we will toss with the horn), is metaphorical, the similitude being drawn from bulls, which with their horns cast into the wind and the air whatsoever is set before them. We, saith he, relying on thy power, will drive away and rout our enemies with that ease wherewith a most fierce bull is wont to toss chaff with his horns.

[Margin: Verse 8.]

**8. FOR I WILL NOT TRUST IN MY BOW: NEITHER SHALL MY SWORD SAVE ME.**

He goeth on to show the likeness between the present people of God and the ancient. For as the ancient people did not possess the land by its own sword, so, saith he, "I also, thy present people, will not trust in my bow," that is, I will not trust in my strength and arms. "Neither shall my sword save me," that is, I know and confess that if I obtain salvation, not my sword will give it me, but thy help.

[Margin: Verse 9.]

**9. BUT THOU HAST SAVED US FROM THEM THAT AFFLICT US: AND HAST PUT THEM TO SHAME THAT HATE US.**

He declareth what he said: "Neither shall my sword save me," saying: "But thou hast saved us from them that afflict us," that is, I confess that my salvation dependeth not on my strength, because as often as I have been saved from any tribulation whatsoever, thou "hast saved us from them that afflict us," and thou art he who "hast put to shame" our enemies, "who hated us," and desired to destroy us, bringing it about by thy protection that their attempt was frustrated, and that they themselves, being put to shame, withdrew from assaulting us.

[Margin: Verse 10.]

**10. IN GOD SHALL WE GLORY ALL THE DAY LONG: AND IN THY NAME WE WILL GIVE PRAISE FOR EVER.**

He concludeth from the things which he hath said that the people of God, as often as it shall be delivered from tribulation, will refer that benefit to God as received from him, and will give all the glory to God himself. "In God, saith he, shall we glory all the day long," that is, not in ourselves, but in God shall we glory at every time in which we shall be delivered from the hands of enemies: and the name of the Lord being called upon, to him we will confess praise and glory even for ever. By which words he confirmeth what he wished to show, that the people is the same that it was before, that is, trusting in the help of God, not in its own strength, as the ancient people: not in its own strength, but in the help of the Lord did it trust. That, *laudabimur* (we shall be praised), is a Hebrew phrase signifying to glory, not properly to be praised. Like to it is the Italian phrase, when we say: *Mi lodo del tale* (I praise myself of such a one, that is, I am well pleased with him), and this phrase is found in many other places, as in *Ps.* xxxiii: "In the Lord shall my soul be praised;" *Ps.* civ: "Glory ye (*Laudamini*) in his holy name," and often elsewhere. But that, *et in nomine tuo confitebimur* (and in thy name we will give praise), is clearer in the Hebrew, *et nomini tuo confitebimur* (and to thy name we will give praise). St. Augustine will have these verses, from the seventh to the tenth, to contain a prophecy of the future victory of Christ and of Christians, when the Church shall triumph over all enemies. But the former exposition, which is Chrysostom's, seemeth more literal: for here not the Prophet speaketh, but the people of God, which wondereth that it is not helped by God, whereas it is the same people with that which God so manifestly protected by his help. It followeth therefore:

<!-- p. 285 (PDF 296) -->

[Margin: Verse 11.]

**11. BUT NOW THOU HAST CAST US OFF, AND PUT US TO SHAME: AND THOU, O GOD, WILT NOT GO OUT WITH OUR ARMIES.**

[Margin: The weakness of the Christian people in the time of the persecutions.]

Here now beginneth the wonder and complaint of the pitiable people grievously afflicted by enemies. "But now," that is, of old thou didst follow us with so great favour, "but now thou hast cast us off, and put us to shame;" for both in the time of Antiochus it was a reproach to be called a Jew, and in the time of the tyrants the name of Christians was infamous, and the faithful seemed forsaken by God. "And thou, O God, wilt not go out with our armies" (*in virtutibus nostris*), that is, if we would resist the enemies who afflict us, thou wilt not go out with us, as of old thou wentest out, to fight for thy people. By *virtutes* (powers) armies are understood, as is clearly had in the Hebrew, בצבאותינו *betsibothenu:* for armed hosts are the strength and might of a people; and *virtus* in this place signifieth not moral virtue, but power and might. Moreover in the time of the persecutions, as well of Antiochus as of the Gentile emperors, so great was the weakness in the people of God, that they durst not in any wise resist; and therefore they fought only by suffering, and enduring. But although they were put to flight and slain by the visible enemy, yet over the invisible enemy they triumphed most gloriously: and although in the sight of men they seemed forsaken by God, yet in secret they were helped by the greatest grace of God. But in this place that only is treated of which was done outwardly, and it is left to the sons of Core to understand what was done in secret.

[Margin: Verse 12.]

**12. THOU HAST MADE US TURN OUR BACK TO OUR ENEMIES: AND THEY THAT HATED US PLUNDERED FOR THEMSELVES.**[^4]

He goeth on to explain the persecution. "Thou hast made us turn our back to our enemies" (*Avertisti nos retrorsum post inimicos nostros,* thou hast turned us backward behind our enemies), that is, thou hast caused that we who were in the first place should now be in the last, and should follow our enemies as vanquished and captive; and the same our enemies, "who hated us," at their pleasure "plundered for themselves" both us and ours, that is, turned them to their own use. St. Jerome rendered from the Hebrew, *vertisti terga nostra hosti* (thou hast turned our backs to the enemy): which sense the Hebrew words easily admit, which to the word sound thus, *avertisti nos retrorsum ab inimicis nostris* (thou hast turned us backward from our enemies); but the Greek and Latin version, which hath *post inimicos nostros* (behind our enemies), cannot easily bear that sense: for they who turn their backs and flee, do not turn their backs behind the enemies, but before the enemies. Therefore we have set down above another sense from Chrysostom and Augustine, nor is our exposition repugnant to the Hebrew words; for instead of that which we said is had, *ab inimicis nostris* (from our enemies), it can also be read, *præ inimicis nostris* (before our enemies), as St. Augustine readeth, and according to that reading the explanation which we have brought standeth very well: for whether thou read, *avertisti nos retrorsum post inimicos nostros* (thou hast turned us backward behind our enemies), or read, *avertisti nos retrorsum præ inimicis nostris* (thou hast turned us backward before our enemies), it is always signified that we have been reduced to a lower and hinder place, and have miserably come into the power of the enemies.

[Margin: Verse 13.]

**13. THOU HAST GIVEN US UP LIKE SHEEP TO BE EATEN: THOU HAST SCATTERED US AMONG THE NATIONS.**

He wonderfully describeth the martyrdoms of the Saints. "Thou hast given us up, saith he, like sheep to be eaten," that is, thou hast permitted us to be slaughtered so easily, and in so great a multitude, as if we were sheep appointed for the shambles, which are killed in great number, and without any resistance: "Thou hast scattered us among the nations," that is, and those of us whom the enemies would not kill, thou hast permitted to be scattered among the nations. Where are noted the exiles into most remote places, to which the Saints were frequently banished.

[Margin: Verse 14.]

**14. THOU HAST SOLD THY PEOPLE FOR NO PRICE: AND THERE WAS NO RECKONING IN THE EXCHANGE OF THEM.**

He describeth another punishment, whereby the holy martyrs, as the vilest slaves, were appointed to cut marbles, or to tend beasts, or to be torn in the theatres by wild beasts, that the eyes of the beholders might be delighted with that spectacle. "Thou hast sold, saith he, thy people for no price," that is, thou hast delivered thy people to the power of enemies, and hast received nothing of price. "And there was no reckoning in the exchange of them" (*non fuit multitudo in commutationibus eorum,* there was no multitude in their exchanges), that is, and when the enemies, to whom thou hadst delivered them, would sell them for slaves, and exchange them for money, there was no multitude of money in their exchanges, that is, as the vilest slaves they were sold almost for nothing, and were appointed to the most abject works. St. Augustine readeth: *Non fuit multitudo in jubilationibus eorum* (There was no multitude in their shouts of joy), and expoundeth: there remained not many in the Churches to shout for joy to the Lord in celebrating the divine offices; but the Greek copies vary: and the more correct have as we read in the Vulgate, which reading agreeth with the Hebrew text, and so also Ambrose, Jerome and Chrysostom read and expound.

[Margin: Verse 15.]

**15. THOU HAST MADE US A REPROACH TO OUR NEIGHBOURS, A SCOFF AND DERISION TO THEM THAT ARE ROUND ABOUT US.**

To bodily punishments he joineth mockeries and reproaches. "Thou hast made us, saith he, a reproach to our neighbours," that is, from this, that by thy permission we have come into the calamities which we described a little before, it hath come to pass that we are now a reproach to our neighbours, and are scoffed at and derided by the same neighbours who are round about us.

[Margin: Verse 16.]

**16. THOU HAST MADE US A BYWORD AMONG THE GENTILES: A SHAKING OF THE HEAD AMONG THE PEOPLE.**

By *similitudinem* (a similitude) is understood a proverb, or parable, as is said more clearly in *Ps.* lxviii: "I became a byword (*in parabolam*) to them." He saith therefore: "Thou hast made us a byword among the Gentiles," that is, thou hast caused the Gentiles to make to themselves a parable, or proverb, of our calamity, and when in speaking they would propose an example of a thing greatly to be detested, to bring forward our misery.

<!-- p. 286 (PDF 297) -->

Thou hast also made us "a shaking of the head among the people," that is, thou hast caused the peoples of the nations to detest, or deride us not only with mockery of words, but also with wagging of the head.

[Margin: Verse 17.]

**17. ALL THE DAY LONG MY SHAME IS BEFORE ME: AND THE CONFUSION OF MY FACE HATH COVERED ME,**

[Margin: Verse 18.]

**18. AT THE VOICE OF HIM THAT REPROACHETH AND DETRACTETH ME: AT THE FACE OF THE ENEMY AND PERSECUTOR.**

He explaineth the effect of the mockeries, because without doubt the confusion and shame was vast. "All the day long, saith he, my shame is before me: and the confusion of my face hath covered me," that is, at every time of this persecution my shame is in my sight, I always see my confusion, which confusion of my face hath covered me wholly as with a certain veil. Now all this confusion ariseth "at the voice of him that reproacheth and detracteth me," that is, from them that reproach me with folly, because I worship the Crucified; reproach me with impiety, because I venerate not the Gods; reproach me with false crimes, infanticides, incests, and the like, and detract concerning these things among themselves. And he explaineth who these are that reproach and detract, when he addeth: "At the face of the enemy and persecutor." But this shame and confusion seemeth not to befit the holy martyrs, since it is written: "He that shall be ashamed of me, of him the Son of man shall be ashamed, when he shall come in his majesty, and that of his Father," *Luke* ix. And: "He that shall confess me, the Son of man also will confess him, when he shall come in the glory of his Father with the holy angels," *Mark* viii.[^5] But the Lord doth not forbid every shame and confusion whatsoever, but that which hindereth confession. For they are blamed who, overcome by shame of this kind, durst not confess Christ, or were even driven to deny him. But that those of whom this Psalm treateth were not overcome by shame, the words following teach.

[Margin: Verse 19.]

**19. ALL THESE THINGS HAVE COME UPON US, YET WE HAVE NOT FORGOTTEN THEE: AND WE HAVE NOT DONE WICKEDLY IN THY COVENANT.**

After explaining the benefits of God toward the ancient fathers, and the destitution and dereliction of the younger people, the Prophet subjoineth in the person of the same people that the cause of this desertion cannot be attributed to their sins: whence is gathered the reason of the wonder and complaint. Thus therefore he speaketh: "All these things have come upon us," that is, we have suffered these straits and tribulations; and "yet we have not forgotten thee," that is, we have not left thee, to worship strange gods: for Scripture is wont to join the forgetting of God with idolatry. *Ps.* cv: "They changed their glory into the likeness of a calf that eateth grass. They forgot God, who saved them." And *Deut.* xxxii: "They sacrificed to devils and not to God: thou hast forsaken the God that beget thee, and hast forgotten the Lord that created thee." And in this very Psalm: "If we have forgotten the name of our God, and if we have spread forth our hands to a strange god." These therefore affirm that they have not worshipped strange gods; therefore they say: "We have not forgotten thee, and we have not done wickedly in thy covenant," that is, not only have we not cleaved to strange gods, forgetting thee the true God; but neither have we done iniquity against the covenant which we made with thee in Sina, that we would keep thy law; for *testamentum* (testament) in this place, as is clearer in the Hebrew ברית *berith,* signifieth *pactum* (a covenant). And so they protest that they have not left God, nor sinned against his law.

[Margin: Verse 20.]

**20. AND OUR HEART HATH NOT TURNED BACK: NEITHER HAST THOU TURNED ASIDE OUR STEPS FROM THY WAY.**[^6]

They repeat the same in other words, that they may the more declare their innocence. *And our heart hath not turned back* is the same as *we have not forgotten thee,* that is, when we walked with thee, cleaving to thee from the heart, we turned not back, nor forsook thee. But that, *et declinasti semitas nostras a via tua* (and thou hast turned aside our steps from thy way), requireth a repetition of the preceding negation, that the sense may be: "Our heart hath not turned back, and thou hast *not* turned aside our steps from thy way," that is, thou hast not suffered our steps to turn aside from thy way, that is, from thy law, which is the same with that, "and we have not done wickedly in thy covenant." St. Jerome most clearly rendereth from the Hebrew, *et non declinaverunt semitæ nostræ a via tua* (and our steps have not turned aside from thy way); for although in the Hebrew there is no negation, and the verb *declinasti* (thou hast turned aside) is in the second person singular, yet St. Jerome chose to render the meaning, not the words. Other Interpreters also, as St. John Chrysostom noteth, added the negation. The Seventy Interpreters, whom the Vulgate edition followeth, did not put the negation in their text, because they found it not in the original, but yet it is to be understood, as in many other places. But if any man will altogether expound affirmatively that, *et declinasti semitas nostras a via tua,* he may by *the way of the Lord* understand the way of mercy and grace; for "all the ways of the Lord are mercy and truth," *Ps.* xxiv, saith the same Prophet. And so the sense will be: thou hast turned aside our steps from the way of thy mercy, from thy favour, from thy grace, from thy help in our tribulations; with which exposition the things that follow agree well enough.

[Margin: Verse 21.]

**21. FOR THOU HAST HUMBLED US IN THE PLACE OF AFFLICTION: AND THE SHADOW OF DEATH HATH COVERED US.**

If we follow the latter exposition of the foregoing verse, the present verse will declare how God turned aside the steps of his people from his way, namely because "he humbled it in the place of affliction;" but if we follow the former exposition, the particle *quoniam* (for) will either be superfluous, as it is more than once superfluous in sacred Scripture, or will give the cause of the following sentence, not of the preceding, in this manner: "For thou hast humbled us

<!-- p. 287 (PDF 298) -->

[Margin: What doth the shadow of death signify?]

in the place of affliction, *therefore* also the shadow of death hath covered us." Moreover to humble a man in the place of affliction is to cast him down into the depth of miseries; for *in loco* (in the place) is put for *in locum* (into the place). St. Jerome rendereth from the Hebrew, *in locum draconum* (into the place of dragons). And truly the word תנים, which is in the Hebrew text, signifieth *dragons.* But the Seventy seem to have rendered the sense, not the word. For the places of dragons are places full of caves, desert, dreadful, and thereby places of affliction; and because places of affliction are dark and gloomy, as caves, pits, foul prisons, therefore he addeth: "And the shadow of death hath covered us," that is, darkness hath covered us, and a vast obscurity. All which are metaphors, whereby the greatness of the tribulation and persecution is signified; the term *shadow of death* is most frequent in the Scriptures of both Testaments, and for the most part is joined with the term *darkness,* as may be seen *Job* iii, *Ps.* lxxxvii and cvi, *Isa.* ix, *Matt.* iv, *Luke* i, and signifieth nothing else but great obscurity. Now obscurity is called the shadow of death, because in death life is extinguished, which is as it were a certain light, whence, life being extinguished, darkness alone is left. St. Augustine saith that mortality is the shadow of death, that is, the image of death; but that the true death is damnation with the devil, which is called eternal death.

[Margin: Verse 22.]

**22. IF WE HAVE FORGOTTEN THE NAME OF OUR GOD, AND IF WE HAVE SPREAD FORTH OUR HANDS TO A STRANGE GOD:**

[Margin: Verse 23.]

**23. SHALL NOT GOD SEARCH OUT THESE THINGS: FOR HE KNOWETH THE SECRETS OF THE HEART.**

They who speak in this Psalm prove by the testimony of God that they have not in truth forgotten the Lord, as they said above. For they say: "If we have forgotten the name of our God, and if we have spread forth our hands to a strange god" to entreat him, "shall not God search out these things," as if they should say: Surely he will search them out, and will find whether it be so. "For he knoweth the secrets of the heart." But assuredly he will find that we have not forgotten his name.

[Margin: Verse 24.]

**24. BECAUSE FOR THY SAKE WE ARE KILLED ALL THE DAY LONG: WE ARE COUNTED AS SHEEP FOR THE SLAUGHTER.**

[Margin: Verse 25.]

**25. ARISE, WHY SLEEPEST THOU, O LORD? ARISE, AND CAST US NOT OFF TO THE END.**

The people of God concludeth, asking of God an end of the persecution. "Because, saith he, for thy sake we are killed all the day long," that is, because for the confession of thy name we are daily delivered to death, many of us are daily slain, "we are counted as sheep for the slaughter," that is, we are made like to sheep appointed for the slaughter, which are butchered without resistance, or mercy. "Arise, why sleepest thou, O Lord?" that is, since thy cause is here in hand, and thy servants suffer so great things, why art thou silent, as if sleeping thou didst not mark? "Arise" from sleep, that is, do what they do who rise from sleep, and begin to see what before they saw not. "Arise to our aid, and cast us not off" from thy help "to the end," that is, even to the consummation; let the persecution at length be ended, thou bringing succour. This place the Apostle brought forward, *Rom.* viii: "Who, saith he, shall separate us from the love of Christ? Shall tribulation? or distress? or famine? or nakedness? or danger? or persecution? or the sword? As it is written: For thy sake we are put to death all the day long. We are accounted as sheep for the slaughter."

[Margin: Verse 26.]

**26. WHY TURNEST THOU THY FACE AWAY? AND FORGETTEST OUR WANT AND OUR TROUBLE?**

[Margin: How God is said to turn away his face.]

He goeth on in the same prayer, and useth two other metaphors: for in the verse above he took a similitude from sleep, now he taketh one from the turning away of the face, and from forgetting, neither of which hath place in God. Yet God is said to turn away his face, when he helpeth not, as if he saw not our misery; and he is also said to forget, when he succoureth not them that need help, and the afflicted, as if he had forgotten them.

[Margin: Verse 27.]

**27. FOR OUR SOUL IS HUMBLED DOWN TO THE DUST: OUR BELLY CLEAVETH TO THE EARTH.**

[Margin: A man can be humbled in heart and in body.]

Persisting in the same prayer, and knowing that the prayer of him that humbleth himself is most pleasing to God, he saith that he is humbled to the utmost, so that he cannot be humbled more. For he who prayeth standing can be humbled by bending the knees; and he who prayeth on bended knees can yet be humbled by prostrating himself wholly on the ground; but he who prayeth prostrate on the ground hath not whither to descend, that he may be more humbled. Moreover a man can be humbled in heart and in body even to the earth: in heart, if he truly think, and understand, and confess that he is dust, as Abraham prayed, when he said: "I will speak to my Lord, whereas I am dust and ashes," *Gen.* xviii; and in body, if he pray prostrate on the earth, which Matthew, chap. xxvi, and Mark, chap. xiv, relate of Christ the Lord: "He fell, say they, upon his face on the ground, saying," etc. Nor is it against this that Luke, chap. xxii, saith: "Kneeling down, he prayed:" for he began to pray kneeling down, then he fell upon the ground. Both humiliations they show forth, who say in this Psalm: "For our soul is humbled down to the dust: our belly cleaveth to the earth," that is, we know that we are dust, and truly our belly hath cleaved to the earth, while we pray to thee prostrate in body. All these things can also be referred to the greatness of the persecution, by which they had been reduced to extreme and utmost ignominy, and contempt.

[Margin: Verse 28.]

**28. ARISE, O LORD, HELP US AND REDEEM US FOR THY NAME'S SAKE.**

They add the last and most efficacious reason why God ought to be moved to mercy toward his people, namely lest any longer

<!-- p. 288 (PDF 299) -->

his name be blasphemed. "Arise, say they, help us" in this tribulation, "and redeem," that is, deliver us, "for thy name's sake," that is, that thy name be not blasphemed, but glorified, and as thou hast sold us for no price, so redeem us for no price, not for our merits, but for thy name's sake, for thy mercy and kindness. For the fuller understanding of this Psalm, certain small questions seem needful to be resolved, which might easily occur to the reader.

[Margin: Why do they who speak in this Psalm complain that they are punished without cause?]

The first question is: Why they who speak in this Psalm complain that they are punished without fault of their own, whereas other Saints are wont to impute persecutions to their own sins; for Daniel, chap. ix, speaking of the Babylonian captivity, saith: "We have sinned, we have committed iniquity, we have done wickedly, and have revolted: and we have gone aside from thy commandments, and thy judgments." And the three holy children, cast into the furnace of fire, and wonderfully delivered by God, confess in this manner, *Dan.* iii: "Thou hast executed true judgments in all the things that thou hast brought upon us, and upon the holy city Jerusalem: for we have sinned, and committed iniquity, departing from thee: and we have trespassed in all things." The same is the voice of the holy Machabees, Book II, chap. vii: "We, say they, suffer these things for ourselves, having sinned against our God." The same saith St. Cyprian of the persecutions of the Christians in the fifth sermon, which is *Of the Lapsed.* I answer that it cannot be denied but that God permitteth persecutions to be stirred up for the sins of the people. But holy men speak in divers manners: for sometimes they take upon them the person of the weak members (for we are one body, every one members one of another), and attribute to themselves the sins of their brethren: in which manner the tongue often confesseth as it were sins, which yet were committed by other members; but sometimes they put on the person of the perfect and holy, who in truth are punished in the common persecution without fault of their own. Thus therefore the Scriptures alleged do not fight with one another: for Daniel, the three children, and the holy Machabees spoke in the person of the weak members; but these, who speak in this Psalm, put on the person of the innocent and of the Saints.

[Margin: Why doth God suffer the innocent to be punished?]

The second question: Why doth God suffer the innocent to be punished, when he permitteth persecution to be stirred up because of the men of iniquity? I answer that the innocent in persecution are not properly punished, but proved: for God willeth by the fierceness of persecution, as by the fire of the fining-pot, or by the fan of the floor, to purge his Church, and to bring it about that it may appear who are true, who feigned faithful, who are gold, who brass, who wheat, who chaff. "Tribulation, saith the Apostle, *Rom.* v, worketh trial;"[^7] and in the book of *Wis.,* chap. iii: "God hath tried them, and found them worthy of himself. As gold in the furnace he hath proved them."

[Margin: We are commanded to endure tribulations, not to love them.]

The third question: Why then do the Saints complain of persecution, and pray that it be quickly ended? I answer, because we are commanded to endure tribulation, not to love it, and no man loveth what he endureth, although he love to endure: for though he rejoice that he endureth, yet he had rather there were nothing for him to endure, as St. Augustine wrote in Book X of the *Confessions,* chap. xxviii. Next, because persecution and temptation is dangerous, since the victory is uncertain, and the Saints do not presume of themselves.

[Margin: Temporal goods are given by God.]

The fourth question: Why doth God, as is said in this Psalm, sometimes bestow on his people temporal goods and victory over enemies, sometimes on the contrary take away the same temporal goods, and suffer his people to be overcome and vanquished by enemies? I answer, that all may understand that temporal goods are given by God, not by devils, or by fate or fortune; therefore he bestoweth them on his friends, when he judgeth it so to be expedient. But that the friends of God may not cleave to temporal goods, nor love the stable for the house, and exile for their country, he often taketh such goods from them, as we have explained more at large in the explanation of Psalm XL, verse 3.
